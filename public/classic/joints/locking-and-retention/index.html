Locking and retention

In one linePreloaded fasteners become loose because the joint slips sideways. A clamp force that is sufficient to stop the slip is the real lock. The locking feature is the backup that keeps the parts from coming off.
Before thisBolted joints · Random vibration

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

A fastener that becomes loose causes two types of damage. First, the joint loses its clamp: a closure leaks, or a bracket rattles and fatigues. Second, the loose part becomes debris that can jam a mechanism or puncture a different part. Motors and their hardware are shaken during transport, on the vehicle and by their own burn. NASA-STD-5020B records that fasteners that became loose and came free have had an effect on previous missions.

There are two ways to lose preload:

  • Non-rotational: embedding, relaxation, creep and temperature. The Bolted joints page gives these.
  • Rotational self-loosening: the nut turns back. This page gives this mechanism.

Why a tight nut turns back

Section titled “Why a tight nut turns back”

While there is no slip, a tightened thread locks itself. The friction on the thread and under the head resists rotation several times more than the ramp of the thread pushes the nut off. In 1969, Gerhard Junker showed that sideways (transverse) slip of the joint stops this lock. Each time the clamped parts slide relative to each other, the friction resists the slip. At that moment, only the ramp acts on the nut:

Tramp=Fi P2πT_\text{ramp} = \frac{F_i\,P}{2\pi}Tramp​=2πFi​P​

A very small rotation in each cycle is sufficient. At vibration frequencies of tens to hundreds of hertz, the rotation adds up quickly. Axial vibration causes much less loosening than transverse vibration. Local slip at the thread or under the head can start the process before the full joint slides.

Think of it like…

A book on a tilted board

A book is on a board with a small tilt, and friction holds it. Now move the board quickly from side to side. The book slips sideways each time. While it slips, the friction cannot also stop it from sliding down the tilt. Thus it moves down a little with each shake. The ramp of the thread is the tilt. The sideways slip of the joint is the shake.

The first lock is preload

Section titled “The first lock is preload”

If you stop the slip, the nut cannot become loose. The joint grips sideways with the interface friction times the clamp force:

FQ,slip=μT  q  (Fi,min−(1−Φ)P)  ≥  k FQF_{Q,\text{slip}} = \mu_T \; q \; \big(F_{i,min} - (1-\Phi)P\big) \;\ge\; k \, F_QFQ,slip​=μT​q(Fi,min​−(1−Φ)P)≥kFQ​

Here Fi,minF_{i,min}Fi,min​ is the minimum preload (with scatter, losses and temperature from the Bolted joints page). The share of the axial load, (1−Φ)P(1-\Phi)P(1−Φ)P, is subtracted. qqq is the number of slip planes, and kkk is the factor of your programme on the transverse load FQF_QFQ​. This is why NASA-STD-5020B calls preload the primary locking feature. If the slip check fails, a locking feature only limits the damage. To prevent the slip:

  • Increase the clamp force: more torque, a larger or stronger bolt, or more bolts.
  • Add friction or slip planes. The surface condition is important. Coatings, lubricant and contamination on the faying surfaces decrease the grip.
  • Carry the shear with other parts: dowels, shoulder bolts or a spigot. Then the bolts only clamp.

On small fasteners, the running torque of a prevailing-torque feature is a fraction of a newton-metre. The self-loosening moment of a slipping joint is several times larger. The standard gives FiP/2F_i P/2Fi​P/2 as an estimate (after Hess, 2017), which is larger than the ramp torque FiP/2πF_i P/2\piFi​P/2π. Thus the feature cannot stop rotation. It stops the fastener from falling out.

Safety wire, cotter pins and staking

Section titled “Safety wire, cotter pins and staking”
  • Safety wire and cable. Install the wire so that its tension pulls each fastener in the tightening direction. NASM 33540 and SAE AS567 give the practice. On a bolt that turns in operation, connect the nut and bolt only to each other, not to the structure.
  • Cotter pins. These go through a castellated nut and a drilled bolt. They are the usual choice for clevis bolts and pivot bolts.
  • Adhesive thread lockers. These are anaerobic adhesives that cure between the threads. They work only if the process is correct: clean parts, primer where necessary, the correct gap, and a full cure. NASA-STD-5020B requires that the process is developed and validated first. Pre-applied coatings that dry without bonding the threads are not adhesives. Do not use them for critical joints.
  • Epoxy staking. A fillet of epoxy connects the fastener head and the part. It is mostly used on small fasteners, up to approximately M4 or #8, and on electronics, where NASA-STD-8739.1 applies.

Requirements for spaceflight hardware

Section titled “Requirements for spaceflight hardware”

NASA-STD-5020B §4.6 gives the rules for NASA spaceflight hardware. Other programmes have equivalent rules:

  • At least one locking feature that does not depend on preload, on each threaded fastening system, for all preloads (§4.6.1). Jam nuts and serrated faces possibly do not qualify, because they depend on preload. A redundant feature can be advisable for some joints (FAA AC 20-71).
  • A mechanical locking feature (safety wire or a cotter pin) on each bolt that is an axis of rotation in operation (§4.6.2).
  • A validated process for adhesive lockers and staking (§4.6.3).
  • Verification of presence and performance (§4.6.4): inspection for mechanical features, running-torque measurement at installation for prevailing torque, and witness samples for adhesives.
  • Running torque and preload. If the torque is not specified as “above running torque”, subtract the maximum running torque from the minimum specified torque, and the minimum breakaway torque from the maximum specified torque, before you calculate the preload (Appendix A.2, Eqs. 27–30).
  • Thread engagement. The fastener must be sufficiently long for fully formed threads to engage a prevailing-torque feature (§4.7.4).

    Worked example: the electronics unit on the aft skirt

    Section titled “Worked example: the electronics unit on the aft skirt”

    The 4 kg unit from the Random vibration page has 29.0 g at 3σ (1137 N) in its lateral test axis. Four M6 class 10.9 screws in prevailing-torque inserts hold it. The maximum running torque is 0.4 N·m (an example, from the part standard of the insert). The screws are tightened to 10 ± 0.5 N·m, not specified as above running torque, with a friction of 0.12. The preload scatter of ±25% and the slip factor of 1.25 are for this example only. Push Load worked example.

    1. Load for each screw: 1137/4=2841137/4 = 2841137/4=284 N sideways, shared equally. This ignores the overturning moment of the unit.
    2. Effective torque: subtract the running torque from the minimum. This gives 9.1–10.5 N·m.
    3. Preload: 9.5 kN at mid-range torque, and 6.6 kN at the minimum torque with −25% scatter.
    4. Slip: with an interface friction of 0.15, the joint grips 0.15×6.6=1.00.15 \times 6.6 = 1.00.15×6.6=1.0 kN for each screw, against 1.25×284=3551.25 \times 284 = 3551.25×284=355 N. MS = +1.81. The joint does not slip, so it does not self-loosen. The preload does the locking.
    5. The insert: it does not depend on preload, so it meets §4.6.1. Measure the running torque at installation to verify it. But its 0.4 N·m is much less than the 1.5 N·m ramp torque (4.8 N·m with the estimate of the standard). It keeps the screws captive. It would not hold their preload if the joint slipped.

    Snug-tight at 2 N·m is a frequent shortcut on a “light” bracket. It gives only 1.5 kN of minimum preload and a grip of 219 N, which is less than the factored 355 N: MS = −0.38. The joint slips on each large cycle of the qualification test. The screws turn back to the running torque of the inserts, and the unit rattles. This is a test failure, but no screw falls out.

    Where this stops

    Section titled “Where this stops”
    • Equally shared shear. Overturning moments, prying and unequal bolt spacing put more load on some bolts than on others. Check the worst bolt.
    • 3σ is not the largest peak. Random vibration has occasional peaks above 3σ (see Random vibration). A slip margin at 3σ does not prevent rare micro-slip. This is one reason why the locking feature is still necessary.
    • Local slip under the head or in the threads can start the loosening before the full joint slides. Bending and thermal cycling can also cause it.
    • Interface friction changes with finish, coating, contamination and wear. Use measured or specified values.

    On the drawing

    Section titled “On the drawing”
    • The locking feature, by specification: insert or nut part number, adhesive and its cure, or safety wire to NASM 33540 with wire material and diameter.
    • The torque, and how to use the running torque: “above running torque”, or a measured running-torque range to subtract.
    • Verification: running torque recorded at installation, witness samples for adhesive, and inspection of wire and pins.
    • Reuse limits. Prevailing-torque features lose torque with each use. Tell if fasteners can be used again.
