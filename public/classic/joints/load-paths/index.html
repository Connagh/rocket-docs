Load paths & joints

In one lineThe pressure loads are very large, but they stay inside the case. Thrust, inertia and bending go out through the skirts. Between two attachments, the load goes to the stiffer path.
Before thisCase sizing · Rocket motor hardware
Used byBolted joints · Shell buckling

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

A motor is a pressure vessel and a structure at the same time. The pressure loads are very large: more than 700 kN on each end of the 300 mm case on this site. But they balance inside the case. The loads that go out of the motor (thrust, inertia and bending) are ten times smaller. They go through the stiffest path, which is not always the path that was designed to carry them.

When you follow the load path (which part carries which load, from where to where), you find:

  • the joints that carry the pressure, and the joints that never carry it;
  • the attachment that takes the thrust, and the attachment that must be free;
  • the redundant path that resists the case as it grows under pressure and temperature. This load is often larger than the thrust.

Follow the pressure

Section titled “Follow the pressure”

Pressure pushes on all the inside surfaces of the case. On a closure, the end load is the pressure times the area inside its seal:

Fend=p πD24F_{end} = p\,\frac{\pi D^2}{4}Fend​=p4πD2​

The pressure pushes the forward dome forward with p πD2/4p\,\pi D^2/4pπD2/4 over the case bore. It pushes the aft closure and the nozzle back with almost the same force. The case wall holds the two ends together in tension. This is the axial stress that case sizing uses. Almost all of the pressure load closes inside the case and never gets to the vehicle.

The throat is different. There is no surface opposite to it, so the pressure pushes the forward dome more than the aft end. The difference is the thrust:

F=CF p AtF = C_F\, p\, A_tF=CF​pAt​

Here CFC_FCF​ is the thrust coefficient of the nozzle. It includes the pressure on the divergent cone. The ratio of end load to thrust is D2/(CF dt2)D^2 / (C_F\,d_t^2)D2/(CF​dt2​), which is 24 for the motor in the worked example.

Think of it like…

You cannot push a car from inside it

Sit in a car and push on the dashboard with all your force. The car does not move. Your hands push the car forward, your back pushes the seat back, and the two forces cancel inside the car. Pressure in a motor does the same to each wall. Only at a hole (the throat) is there nothing to push back. That unbalanced area gives the thrust.

The same method gives the loads on the joints of the aft end:

  • The nozzle, attached over a seal diameter dnd_ndn​, has a push-off force of p πdn2/4p\,\pi d_n^2/4pπdn2​/4, minus the thrust that it makes: Fnoz=p πdn2/4−FF_{noz} = p\,\pi d_n^2/4 - FFnoz​=pπdn2​/4−F. This blow-off load goes into its attachment.
  • The aft closure, sealed at DsD_sDs​, carries p πDs2/4−Fp\,\pi D_s^2/4 - FpπDs2​/4−F during firing. In a static pressure test it carries the full p πDs2/4p\,\pi D_s^2/4pπDs2​/4, because there is no flow and thus no thrust to decrease it. Size its joint for the test as well as for firing.

Through the skirts to the vehicle

Section titled “Through the skirts to the vehicle”

The thrust accelerates the motor and all that it pushes. With thrust only, the acceleration is a=F/(mmotor+mahead)a = F / (m_{motor} + m_{ahead})a=F/(mmotor​+mahead​). The forward skirt passes on only the force that the mass ahead of it needs, mahead am_{ahead}\,amahead​a:

Fskirt=F maheadmmotor+maheadF_{skirt} = F\,\frac{m_{ahead}}{m_{motor} + m_{ahead}}Fskirt​=Fmmotor​+mahead​mahead​​

The remaining force accelerates the motor. The case and closures take their share through their walls, and the grain takes its share through its bond, with a mean shear of mgrain a/(πDL)m_{grain}\,a / (\pi D L)mgrain​a/(πDL). Thus:

  • The skirt load increases during the burn. As the propellant burns, the same thrust pushes less motor and more payload. The peak is near burnout, not at ignition.
  • The thrust compresses the case wall between the aft end and the forward skirt. In a firing motor, this only decreases the pressure tension a little. In a motor without pressure, the case and skirts are thin shells in compression. Examples are an upper stage that carries the thrust of the stage below, and any motor during handling and transport. See Shell buckling.
  • The aft skirt carries the motor on the ground, or connects it to an interstage, fins or steering actuators. One skirt is the thrust takeout. Design the other skirt with the knowledge that it is not.

Bending from flight loads and steering adds to the axial load. Thin shells are checked with the running load, which is the force for each unit length of circumference:

N=PπD±4MπD2N = \frac{P}{\pi D} \pm \frac{4M}{\pi D^2}N=πDP​±πD24M​

At the attach ring, the bolts share the same loads. For nnn equally spaced bolts on a bolt circle DbD_bDb​, with the joint closed and its neutral axis at the centre, the bolt with the most load carries:

Fbolt=4Mn Db−PnF_{bolt} = \frac{4M}{n\,D_b} - \frac{P}{n}Fbolt​=nDb​4M​−nP​

Here PPP is the axial compression. Compression from thrust decreases the bolt loads. Thus the worst bolt tension often occurs with bending and no thrust: during coast, handling, or before ignition. That bolt load is the external load for Bolted joints.

Stiffness and load sharing

Section titled “Stiffness and load sharing”

When two paths connect the same two points, they have the same displacement. Thus they share the load by stiffness. For a bar or tube, k=EA/Lk = EA/Lk=EA/L:

P1=P k1k1+k2P_1 = P\,\frac{k_1}{k_1 + k_2}P1​=Pk1​+k2​k1​​

For paths in series, add the flexibilities. For paths in parallel, add the stiffnesses. Three results are frequent causes of design errors:

  • Gaps decide which path takes the load first. A path with clearance carries no load until the joint has moved through the clearance. In a shear joint with clearance holes, the first bolt that touches takes the load. In a long bolted or riveted lap, the end fasteners take the most load.
  • Capacities do not add. In a bonded and bolted joint, the bond is much stiffer. It takes almost all the load, and it fails at a displacement that is too small for the bolts to carry load. The joint fails when its first path fails. The bolts then carry all the load alone. Thus they are a fail-safe, not additional strength.
  • Redundant paths resist each other. If one path grows more than the other (from pressure growth, thermal expansion or a tolerance), a connection between them causes a load in the two paths, with no applied load:
F=δ k1k2k1+k2F = \delta\,\frac{k_1 k_2}{k_1 + k_2}F=δk1​+k2​k1​k2​​

A motor case grows axially under pressure by δ=p r L (1−2ν)/(2E t)\delta = p\,r\,L\,(1 - 2\nu)/(2E\,t)δ=prL(1−2ν)/(2Et), and it grows more radially. If you attach it rigidly at the two skirts to a structure that does not grow, the attachments carry that growth. The usual solution is to fix one end and let the other end slide. Use an axial slip joint with more travel than the growth, or a flexure, which still carries the radial loads and shear loads.

Radial growth also loads the skirt. At MEOP, the radius of the 300 mm case grows by 0.68 mm. An integral skirt must follow this growth at its root. If a steel skirt followed it fully, it would have the 0.45% hoop strain of the case, approximately 930 MPa, with no pressure inside it. This stress decreases within a few times rt≈14\sqrt{rt} \approx 14rt​≈14 mm of the joint. In practice, the skirt also holds the case back locally, and the junction needs a shell analysis or a finite-element analysis. Composite cases have several times more strain. Thus their skirts are bonded through a rubber shear ply, which lets the case grow under the skirt.

Think of it like…

A four-legged table

A three-legged stool stands evenly on any floor. A four-legged table on an uneven floor moves until you put a shim under one leg. Then the stiffest and longest legs carry most of the weight. Redundant load paths are the same as the table. They share load by stiffness and fit, not by design intent. A small misfit can put more load on a leg than the weight does.

For shear joints, check the share VVV of each fastener (the load divided among the fasteners, plus any bending) in several ways against your allowables and factors:

  • Fastener shear: τ=V/(m A)\tau = V / (m\,A)τ=V/(mA), with mmm shear planes (one in a lap, two in a clevis).
  • Bearing: σbr=V/(d t)\sigma_{br} = V / (d\,t)σbr​=V/(dt) on the hole in each part. Usually the thinnest or softest part governs.
  • Shear-out and net section: the part tears out to the edge, or fails across the row of holes. The edge distance and the pitch set these. Pinned lugs and clevises have their own first checks on Hand calcs.

Also think about the sealing and the stiffness of each joint together. A joint that is sufficiently strong but turns or opens under pressure is a leak path. See Sealing strategy.

      Worked example: the 300 mm motor’s load path

      Section titled “Worked example: the 300 mm motor’s load path”

      This is the 300 mm D6AC case at its MEOP of 10 MPa (case sizing), with an illustrative 50 mm throat and CF=1.5C_F = 1.5CF​=1.5 from the grain page. The aft closure seals at 260 mm with 30 bolts (Bolted joints). The nozzle seals into it at an illustrative 110 mm. The masses are from the hardware page: 137.81 kg at ignition, 26.61 kg at burnout, and a 50 kg payload on the forward skirt. The thrust stays at its MEOP value until burnout, which gives the maximum loads.

      1. Pressure. The pressure pushes the forward dome forward with 10×π×1502=70710 \times \pi \times 150^2 = 70710×π×1502=707 kN, and the aft end back with 677 kN. The 29.5 kN difference is the thrust. It is 24 times smaller than the end loads, which close inside the case.
      2. Nozzle and closure. The push-off force on the nozzle is 10×π×552=95.010 \times \pi \times 55^2 = 95.010×π×552=95.0 kN, minus its thrust. This gives a 65.6 kN blow-off load into its attachment. The aft closure carries 501 kN during firing (16.7 kN for each bolt), but 531 kN in a static pressure test (17.7 kN). The bolted-joints example correctly used the test value.
      3. Acceleration. 29.5/(137.81+50)=15729.5 / (137.81 + 50) = 15729.5/(137.81+50)=157 m/s², which is 16.0 g at ignition. It increases to 39.2 g at burnout.
      4. Into the payload. The forward skirt passes on 29.5×50/187.81=7.8429.5 \times 50 / 187.81 = 7.8429.5×50/187.81=7.84 kN at ignition and 19.2 kN at burnout. The inertia of the motor takes the remaining force: 21.6 kN, then 10.2 kN. At ignition, the 17.4 kN of the grain is spread over its 0.94 m² bond, which gives a shear of 18.5 kPa. This is very small.
      5. Bending. With 3 kN·m at the forward skirt (the flight load that the shell-buckling example used), the running load of the skirt is 20.3+42.0=62.320.3 + 42.0 = 62.320.3+42.0=62.3 N/mm in compression at burnout. This is 31 MPa in a 2 mm wall.
      6. Attach ring. Sixteen bolts on a 280 mm circle each take 4M/(nDb)=2.684M/(nD_b) = 2.684M/(nDb​)=2.68 kN from the bending. The thrust decreases this: 2.19 kN at ignition and 1.48 kN at burnout. The worst case is bending without thrust, 2.68 kN. This is the external load for each bolt in Bolted joints.
      7. A redundant attachment. Now assume that the motor is also fixed at its aft skirt to an aluminium airframe tube (⌀330 × 2 mm, 1000 mm between the skirts). The axial stiffness of the case is 205 000×π×301.4×1.377/1000=267205\,000 \times \pi \times 301.4 \times 1.377 / 1000 = 267205000×π×301.4×1.377/1000=267 kN/mm, and the airframe stiffness is 149 kN/mm. In series against a mismatch, the stiffness is 95.5 kN/mm.
        • At MEOP, the case grows 10×150×1000×(1−0.58)/(2×205 000×1.377)=1.1210 \times 150 \times 1000 \times (1 - 0.58) / (2 \times 205\,000 \times 1.377) = 1.1210×150×1000×(1−0.58)/(2×205000×1.377)=1.12 mm. This causes 107 kN in the two attachments.
        • At −40 °C, 60 °C below the assembly temperature, the aluminium has also shrunk 0.68 mm more than the steel. The total is 1.80 mm, which gives 172 kN. This is almost six times the thrust, and nine times the force that the forward skirt passes to the payload.
        • The aft attachment has a capacity of 120 kN, and the ultimate factor is 1.25. These two values are for this example only. Push Load worked example in the second calculator. The result is MS = −0.44.
        • The solution: let the aft skirt slide axially, with 2 mm of travel. The mismatch never closes the gap, and the force is zero. The forward skirt alone takes the thrust.

      Where this stops

      Section titled “Where this stops”
      • Quasi-static, thrust only. Drag, weight, steering side loads, the ignition transient, thrust oscillation and vibration (Random vibration) come from the loads analysis of the vehicle. Here the thrust stays at its MEOP value, which is an upper limit of a real thrust trace.
      • Beam theory and an elastic bolt ring. Local load introduction, prying, flange contact and a joint that opens on the tension side need a detailed analysis.
      • Two paths. Real structures have many paths, and you need a finite-element model to find their stiffnesses.
      • The nozzle attach plane is at chamber pressure. The flow at the nozzle entrance decreases the pressure a little.
      • Elastic, room temperature. Stiffness and capacity change across the temperature range. Joints with gaps also slip and fret.

      On the drawing

      Section titled “On the drawing”
      • The interface: hole pattern, bolt circle, datums and mating surfaces, from the interface control document of the vehicle. Also tell which interface is the thrust takeout.
      • The fixed end and the sliding end: the axial travel of the slip joint, and the loads that it still carries.
      • Interface limit loads: axial, shear and bending at each attachment, usually in the interface control document.
      • Fastener torques and preload at the attach rings and closures. See Bolted joints.
      • Bond and shear-ply details for bonded skirts: adhesive, ply thickness, surface preparation, and the inspection that verifies the bond.
