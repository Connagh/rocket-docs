FEA & test

In one lineA finite-element model answers only the question that you put into it. Select the simplest model that answers the real question, show that the mesh has converged, and check that the result obeys physics before anyone reads it.
Before thisHand calcs · FEA material cards
Used byProof testing · Margin policy

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

A finite-element model always gives a result, in colour, to four significant figures. If the result is correct depends on decisions that the contour plot does not show: what the model does not include, how fine the mesh is where it is important, what holds the model, and how the loads are applied. Most incorrect analyses are not caused by a solver failure. They are incorrect because the model answered a different question, and nobody checked.

Two words help:

  • Verification. Are the equations solved correctly? This includes code checks, mesh convergence and equilibrium.
  • Validation. Are they the correct equations? Only a comparison with a test can show that the idealisation is sufficiently good.

NASA-STD-7009 and ASME V&V 10 give the two processes for models whose results support decisions.

Think of it like…

A map

A map is useful because it does not show everything. A road map has no contour lines, and a hiking map has no traffic lights. If you use the incorrect map, all the details can be accurate but the route is incorrect. A model is a map of the part for one question. A converged mesh makes the map sharper. It does not make it the correct map.

From question to model

Section titled “From question to model”

Start with the question, for example the burst margin of the case, the seal-gap opening at the aft joint, a bolt load, or the natural frequency of a bracket. Then select the simplest model that can answer it:

ModelGood forDoes not include
Hand calcSizing; far-field stresses; checks of all other modelsDetail, interaction
Axisymmetric shellCase, domes and skirts under pressure and axial load; junction bendingBolts, local 3-D detail, non-axisymmetric loads
Axisymmetric solidClosures, bosses, flange sections, thick parts, through-thickness stressDiscrete bolts (smeared into a ring), bending loads

Full 3-DBending and side loads, real asymmetric detail, assembliesNothing, but it is the most difficult to check
  • Symmetry also needs symmetric loads. An axisymmetric case model cannot take a bending moment. A quarter model cannot take an asymmetric load.
  • Shells or solids. Thin walls (r/tr/tr/t more than approximately 10) are shells. Junctions, lands and thick parts are solids. Linear solid elements lock in bending. Use quadratic elements, or several linear elements through the thickness.
  • Linear or nonlinear. Large deflection of thin shells, plasticity near burst, and contact in joints and seals each make the model nonlinear. Each has its own convergence and checks.

Singularities

Section titled “Singularities”

At some points, the stress does not converge. At a sharp internal corner, the elastic stress is infinite. Near the corner it increases as rλ−1r^{\lambda-1}rλ−1, with the Williams exponent λ=0.545\lambda = 0.545λ=0.545 for a 90° corner and 0.5 for a crack. Each time you halve the mesh, the peak increases by 21−λ=1.372^{1-\lambda} = 1.3721−λ=1.37, with no limit.

The same occurs at point loads, point constraints, contact edges and the corners of bonded dissimilar materials. Thus:

  • Model the real radius. If the drawing has a 0.5 mm fillet, the model must also have it, with a mesh that is sufficiently fine to converge. The radius then becomes a drawing requirement.
  • Read stresses where a method defines them. Use through-thickness linearisation into membrane and bending for pressure-vessel codes, hot-spot extrapolation from fixed distances for welds, and stress intensity for cracks.
  • Spread loads and supports over a realistic area. Apply them with a coupling that does not make the part stiffer, for example Nastran RBE3 or an Abaqus distributing coupling, unless the real part is rigid.

Boundary conditions and loads

Section titled “Boundary conditions and loads”
  • Remove all rigid-body motions, and no more. A model with too few constraints does not solve. A model with too many constraints solves, but it gives stresses that the real part never has. If you fix the end of a case model rigidly, but the real flange grows and turns with the wall, you get the clamped-edge bending of the hand calc. This is approximately two times the hoop stress, and it is not real.
  • Free bodies stay free. Nothing holds a motor in flight. Balance its loads with inertia (inertia relief). Do not add a support that does not exist.
  • Apply pressure to all wetted faces, which includes the closures and the nozzle as well as the wall. Check the reaction.
  • Symmetry planes need the symmetry conditions, not only a cut.

Contact and preload

Section titled “Contact and preload”

Joints and seals have contact: surfaces that can separate and slide, and that carry pressure only in compression.

  • Apply the preload first, then the load. Put the bolt pretension in its own step. Check the clamp force against the Bolted joints calculation before you apply the service loads.
  • Initial gaps and overlaps in the geometry become forces or free play at the first increment. Remove them.
  • Contact must also converge. Contact pressure needs a sufficiently fine mesh on the two surfaces. Check the penetration, and make sure that the contact status does not change back and forth between increments.
  • Monitor the artificial terms. Stabilisation energy or hourglass energy must be a small fraction of the strain energy (a few per cent maximum). If it is not, the result comes from the numerics, not from the part.
  • Seal gaps come from separation at the seal line, for the coldest case with the lowest preload. See Sealing strategy.

Before the results go in a report

Section titled “Before the results go in a report”
  1. Inputs. Geometry at the drawing revision, checked material cards, and units.
  2. Reactions. They are equal to the applied loads, to round-off. A static model is in equilibrium, or a load is missing.
  3. Deformed shape. Animate it at a sensible scale. It must move as the part would move.
  4. Far field. Compare membrane stresses, displacements and frequencies with the hand calc.
  5. Convergence of each reported quantity, at its point.
  6. Singularities. Find them, and do not read a result at one.
  7. Diagnostics. Check solver warnings, element distortion, negative eigenvalues, and the energy balance in nonlinear and explicit runs.
  8. Sensitivity. Put limits on the uncertain inputs: support stiffness, friction, gaps and material scatter.
  9. Validation. Compare with a test if there is one (Test correlation is the next step).
  10. Record. Write the question, the assumptions, the inputs and their sources, the checks and the results, so that a different engineer can do the analysis again.

The model and the test

Section titled “The model and the test”

The model helps to plan the test, and the test checks the model:

  • Put gauges in small gradients, where a small position error is not important. Compare them with the strain of the model averaged over the gauge length, on the same surface.
  • Put gauges on the two surfaces where the part bends. One surface cannot separate membrane from bending.
  • Use rosettes where you do not know the principal directions. For a rectangular rosette with gauges aaa, bbb, ccc at 0°, 45° and 90°:

    Worked example: checking a model of the case at its aft flange

    Section titled “Worked example: checking a model of the case at its aft flange”

    The question is the peak stress in the wall of the 300 mm D6AC case where it meets the aft flange, at MEOP (10 MPa). The model is the simplest one that can answer it: an axisymmetric shell. Here it is a real one-dimensional finite-element model of the wall, which runs in the Mesh convergence tab. The end is clamped to represent the flange.

    1. The free body. The first run had pressure on the wall but not on the closures. The far-field hoop stress was 1094 MPa, which is exactly pr/tpr/tpr/t, so it looked correct. But the axial reaction was zero. It must be 10×π×1502=70710 \times \pi \times 150^2 = 70710×π×1502=707 kN. The radial growth also showed the error: 0.804 mm, which is 17% more than the closed-end 0.688 mm and exactly the open-end value. With the end load applied, the two values agree. (These first-run values are the defaults in the Equilibrium & far field tab.)
    2. Mesh convergence. Elements of 16, 8 and 4 mm give peak axial stresses of 1811, 2108 and 2205 MPa. The convergence is monotonic, the observed order is 1.62, the extrapolated value is 2252 MPa, the GCI is 2.7% and the asymptotic ratio is 1.05. You can trust this study. The exact result for this model is 2241 MPa. The 4 mm mesh is 1.6% low and the extrapolation is 0.5% high. A study on 32, 16 and 8 mm meshes has fewer than five elements across the 35 mm edge zone. It gives an order of 0.87 and an extrapolated value of 2467 MPa, which is 10% high. With coarser meshes, the results do not converge.
    3. Converged is not the same as correct. The 2241 MPa is real for this model, but it is not real for the case. It comes from the clamp. The hand calc gives the same value for a rigid edge, approximately two times the hoop stress. The real flange grows and turns with the wall, so the model must include it, or at least its stiffness. The wall also needs a land where it joins the flange. If the inside corner of the land were a sharp 90°, the peak would increase by 1.37 times each time you halve the mesh. Model the fillet radius of the drawing.
    4. The test. At the 11 MPa proof test, a rosette is 100 mm from the flange. This is almost three edge zones away, in a small gradient. In this example it reads 1260, 3170 and 4950 µε at 0°, 45° and 90° from the axis. The Strain gauges tab gives 1190 MPa hoop and 603 MPa axial. The model gives 1204 MPa and 602 MPa, so the differences are 1.2% and 0.2%. This validates the membrane model at that location. The edge region needs its own gauges, or the comparison must wait for test correlation.

    Where this stops

    Section titled “Where this stops”
    • Method, not a solver manual. Element formulations, contact algorithms and solver settings are different between codes. Follow the documentation of your code and the modelling guidelines of your organisation.
    • Richardson extrapolation assumes smooth convergence in the asymptotic range. It is not applicable at singular points, or to quantities that change their behaviour, such as contact status or buckling modes.
    • The edge model is linear, axisymmetric and thin-shell. It is a demonstration with a known result, not a design tool.
    • Validation needs tests that are designed for it, with loads, boundary conditions and measurements that the model can represent.

    On the drawing

    Section titled “On the drawing”
    • The radii and lands that the model used, as minimum dimensions, so that nobody can make the part sharper than the analysis.
    • The drawing revision of each model, recorded in the analysis.
    • Gauge locations, orientations and surfaces on the test drawing or procedure, the same as the read-out points of the model.
