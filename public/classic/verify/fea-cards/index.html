FEA material cards

In one lineA material card is an engineering input, like a drawing. An incorrect card runs, converges and gives smooth contours. Check it before you trust its results.
Before thisHand calcs · Allowables
Used byFEA & test · Random vibration

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

You can see a bad mesh. You cannot see a bad material card. Some examples are a density in incorrect units, a Poisson’s ratio of 0.49 for propellant, an engineering stress–strain curve entered as true values, or a temperature table that stops below the operating temperature. Each of these runs, converges and gives smooth, plausible contours. Card errors are some of the most frequent errors in finite-element work, and some of the easiest to find.

Each card is also a set of decisions. Which data? On which basis (typical or minimum)? At which temperature and rate? In which form (elastic, plastic, orthotropic, hyperelastic or viscoelastic)? A motor model needs all of them: a steel or composite case, aluminium and titanium hardware, rubber insulation and seals, and a propellant grain that is very different from all the other materials.

Metals

Section titled “Metals”

Elastic. Use Young’s modulus EEE and Poisson’s ratio ν\nuν. These give G=E/2(1+ν)G = E/2(1+\nu)G=E/2(1+ν). Give a solver two of the three values. Never give three values that do not agree. Stiffness is always a typical value. There is no “minimum modulus”.

Plastic. Solvers need true stress against true plastic strain, which starts at zero plastic strain. From an engineering curve, up to necking:

σt=σ (1+ε),εt=ln⁡(1+ε)\sigma_t = \sigma\,(1 + \varepsilon), \qquad \varepsilon_t = \ln(1 + \varepsilon)σt​=σ(1+ε),εt​=ln(1+ε) εp=εt−σt/E\varepsilon_p = \varepsilon_t - \sigma_t/Eεp​=εt​−σt​/E

If you have only the tensile properties, use a Ramberg–Osgood curve ε=σ/E+0.002 (σ/Fty)n\varepsilon = \sigma/E + 0.002\,(\sigma/F_{ty})^nε=σ/E+0.002(σ/Fty​)n. Put it through the 0.2% yield, and through the ultimate at the uniform elongation eue_ueu​. This gives the shape:

n=ln⁡ ⁣[(eu−Ftu/E)/0.002]ln⁡(Ftu/Fty)n = \frac{\ln\!\left[(e_u - F_{tu}/E)/0.002\right]}{\ln(F_{tu}/F_{ty})}n=ln(Ftu​/Fty​)ln[(eu​−Ftu​/E)/0.002]​
  • Typical or minimum? Use typical curves for stiffness and to correlate with tests. Use minimum (A- or B-basis) curves when the plastic analysis is the strength check. Do not use the two in one model.
  • After the last row, many solvers keep the stress constant (Abaqus does this). The true curve continues to increase after necking. Thus a table that stops at the ultimate is conservative for load, but it tells nothing about failure.

Temperature. Each property that changes over the range of the model needs a table against temperature. The table must cover the full range. Outside the range, most solvers keep the end values. They do not extrapolate.

Thermal expansion. Handbooks give the instantaneous coefficient (the slope of the expansion curve at a temperature) or the secant (mean) coefficient from a reference temperature. Solvers need secant values and the reference temperature for them (Abaqus ZERO, the Ansys reference temperature, Nastran TREF):

αˉ(T)=1T−Tref∫TrefTα dT\bar\alpha(T) = \frac{1}{T - T_{ref}}\int_{T_{ref}}^{T} \alpha\,dTαˉ(T)=T−Tref​1​∫Tref​T​αdT

Composites

Section titled “Composites”

An orthotropic card has nine constants: E1,E2,E3E_1, E_2, E_3E1​,E2​,E3​, ν12,ν13,ν23\nu_{12}, \nu_{13}, \nu_{23}ν12​,ν13​,ν23​, G12,G13,G23G_{12}, G_{13}, G_{23}G12​,G13​,G23​. Symmetry relates the major and minor Poisson’s ratios, νji=νijEj/Ei\nu_{ji} = \nu_{ij}E_j/E_iνji​=νij​Ej​/Ei​. The constants describe a real material only if these conditions are true (Lempriere):

  • each modulus is positive, and ∣νij∣<Ei/Ej|\nu_{ij}| < \sqrt{E_i/E_j}∣νij​∣<Ei​/Ej​​;
  • the compliance determinant is positive:
Δ=1−ν12ν21−ν23ν32−ν13ν31−2ν21ν32ν13>0\begin{aligned} \Delta = 1 &- \nu_{12}\nu_{21} - \nu_{23}\nu_{32} \\ &- \nu_{13}\nu_{31} - 2\nu_{21}\nu_{32}\nu_{13} > 0 \end{aligned}Δ=1​−ν12​ν21​−ν23​ν32​−ν13​ν31​−2ν21​ν32​ν13​>0​
  • Conventions are different. Some sources and solvers give ν21\nu_{21}ν21​ where others mean ν12\nu_{12}ν12​. If you type the minor ratio in place of the major ratio, all the checks above pass, but the card is incorrect.
  • For a unidirectional ply, the 2–3 plane is usually isotropic: E3=E2E_3 = E_2E3​=E2​ and G23=E2/2(1+ν23)G_{23} = E_2/2(1+\nu_{23})G23​=E2​/2(1+ν23​).
  • Orientation. The correct constants on incorrect material axes also pass all the checks. Plot the axes in the model.
  • Ply or laminate. A wound case is made from ply data and winding angles. A smeared laminate card hides the bending–stretching coupling and the through-thickness behaviour.

Rubbers and propellant

Section titled “Rubbers and propellant”

It is easy to change the shape of rubber and propellant, but it is very difficult to change their volume. Their bulk modulus KKK is thousands of times their shear modulus. Poisson’s ratio is then a derived value, and its fourth decimal place is important:

ν=3K−E6K\nu = \frac{3K - E}{6K}ν=6K3K−E​

Think of it like…

A water balloon

When you squeeze a water balloon, it bulges in a different location. It is easy to change its shape, but almost impossible to make it smaller. If you give it a Poisson’s ratio of 0.49 in a model, it becomes a sponge. It is still soft in shape, but now it is also soft in volume: sixty times too soft for propellant. All the results that depend on its volume change are then incorrect, for example the pressure on a bonded grain, or a seal in its groove.

  • Get KKK from a volumetric test, and calculate ν\nuν from it. Never round to 0.49 or 0.5.
  • Hyperelastic forms (neo-Hookean with C10=G/2C_{10} = G/2C10​=G/2 and D1=2/KD_1 = 2/KD1​=2/K, Mooney–Rivlin, Ogden) are fitted to uniaxial, planar and equibiaxial tests together. A fit to one test only can become unstable in a different mode. Check the stability over the strain range of the model.
  • Viscoelastic. Stiffness depends on rate and temperature, thus a card is for one load case. Time-dependent loads need a Prony series and a shift function (see Viscoelasticity). Thermal loads need the stress-free temperature (Propellant grain).
  • Use hybrid (mixed) elements. Usual elements lock when the material is almost incompressible, and they are much too stiff.

A “typical” value from a supplier is not an allowable. A handbook value at room temperature tells nothing about 150 °C. Put the source, its basis, the temperature and the material condition on the card with the numbers.

Checking a card before you trust it

Section titled “Checking a card before you trust it”
  1. Units. Use one consistent system. Check the density with the wave speed E/ρ\sqrt{E/\rho}E/ρ​, which is some thousands of m/s for all metals. Compare the total mass of the model with the CAD or the scales. A static model does not show a density error. The first vibration or acceleration case shows it.
  2. Consistency. Isotropic: EEE, ν\nuν and GGG agree. Orthotropic: symmetry, the limits and the determinant. Almost incompressible: ν\nuν from a measured KKK.
  3. Basis and purpose. Typical or minimum, as necessary for the margin calculation.
  4. Plastic tables. True values, the first row at zero plastic strain, increasing values, the last row at necking, and known behaviour after it.
  5. Temperature and rate. The tables cover the range of the model. Rubber and propellant data agree with the load case. Expansion is secant, with its reference temperature.
  6. Orientation of orthotropic materials in the model.
  7. Single-element tests. Load one element and compare the result with the card:
TestLoadMust give
Uniaxial stressStretch, sides freeEEE; ν\nuν from the lateral strain; the plastic curve
Pure shearShearGGG
HydrostaticPressure on all facesKKK

MassGravity or a frequencyDensity
  1. Traceability. Record the source, basis, temperature, condition, revision and owner. A card is a controlled input, like a drawing.

Calculator

Section titled “Calculator”

The first tab writes a card for any material in the site’s material library. Select the material, the solver and the unit system. The tab writes the input text for Abaqus, Ansys MAPDL or Nastran, and you can copy it or download it. Use in the check tabs copies the same values into the other tabs, so that you can check them there.

  • The card contains the elastic constants, the density, the secant expansion, and the conductivity and specific heat when the library has them.
  • For a metal, enter the uniform elongation from your material data to add a true-stress plastic table.
  • A comment line in the card identifies each value that comes from a family default. The card leaves out each value that is not in the library, and the tab tells you which values these are.
  • The library values are typical room-temperature values. They are applicable to stiffness, mass and correlation. For a strength check, use the allowables from your material specification.

Material and solver

The library holds typical room-temperature values, and its expansion is the secant value from 20 °C. Enter the uniform elongation from your material data to add a plastic table.

Isotropic card, as typed

Enter the numbers exactly as they sit in the model. The wave speed √(E/ρ) shows at once whether the density is in the system's units.

Orthotropic engineering constants

Major Poisson's ratios: νij is the strain in j from a stress in i. Any consistent units. The defaults are a typical carbon/epoxy unidirectional ply.

Rubber or propellant

E at the rate and temperature of the load case; K from a volumetric test. Defaults: the propellant on the Propellant grain page.

Metal tensile properties

Uniform elongation is the engineering strain at the ultimate (where necking starts), not the elongation at break.

Instantaneous expansion data

Instantaneous α is the slope of the expansion curve at a temperature; handbooks give either kind: check which. Linear between the two points.

    Adding a material

    Section titled “Adding a material”

    All the tools on the site use one material library, src/data/materials.json. When you add an entry to this file, the new material appears in each tool that can use it: the material chart, case sizing, buckling, fatigue, fracture, proof testing, differential expansion, fits, bolted joints, sealing, transient thermal, insulation, the grain screen, the hand calcs, FEA & test, residual stress, random vibration and this card generator. A tool shows a material only when the entry has the properties that the tool needs.

    1. Copy an entry of the same family and change the values. The units are g/cm³ for density, GPa for EEE, MPa for the yield and the ultimate, µm/m·K for expansion, W/m·K for conductivity and MPa√m for toughness.
    2. Add the optional values that you have: nu (Poisson’s ratio), cp (J/kg·K), eu (uniform elongation, %) and kz (conductivity through the thickness of a laminate). If you do not give nu or cp, the tools use the family default and mark it.
    3. Give the source of the values in spec or summary.
    4. Run npm test. The tests make sure that each material has a unique name and a known family, and that the values are in a physical range.

    Worked example: cards for the 300 mm motor’s model

    Section titled “Worked example: cards for the 300 mm motor’s model”

    The starting values in each tab of the calculator are the values of this example. The library card starts with the D6AC case in mm, N, tonne and seconds. The uniform elongation, the ply constants and the expansion coefficients are illustrative.

    1. D6AC case, elastic values and units. In mm, N, tonne and seconds: E=205 000E = 205\,000E=205000 MPa and ν=0.29\nu = 0.29ν=0.29, so G=79 457G = 79\,457G=79457 MPa. The density is 7.85×10−97.85 \times 10^{-9}7.85×10−9 t/mm³. The wave speed is 205 000/7.85×10−9\sqrt{205\,000/7.85\times10^{-9}}205000/7.85×10−9​ mm/s =5110= 5110=5110 m/s, which is correct for steel. If you type the density as 7850, the wave speed decreases to 5 mm/s, and the 10.2 kg cylinder has a mass of 1.02×10101.02 \times 10^{10}1.02×1010 t. A static pressure analysis does not show this error. It shows at the first vibration load.
    2. D6AC case, plastic values. Fty=1450F_{ty} = 1450Fty​=1450 MPa, Ftu=1520F_{tu} = 1520Ftu​=1520 MPa and, for this example, 5% uniform elongation give n=64.9n = 64.9n=64.9. This is very little hardening, as in most high-strength steels. The table goes from 1394 MPa at zero plastic strain to 1596 MPa at 4.10% true plastic strain. The true stress at necking is 5% more than the engineering ultimate. One stretched element in the model must follow the table, and then keep 1596 MPa constant.
    3. Carbon-fibre skirt, orthotropic values. A typical carbon/epoxy ply has E1=160E_1 = 160E1​=160 GPa, E2=E3=9E_2 = E_3 = 9E2​=E3​=9 GPa, ν12=0.32\nu_{12} = 0.32ν12​=0.32, ν23=0.45\nu_{23} = 0.45ν23​=0.45, G12=5G_{12} = 5G12​=5 GPa and G23=3.1G_{23} = 3.1G23​=3.1 GPa. It passes: the determinant is 0.781, ν21=0.018\nu_{21} = 0.018ν21​=0.018, and G23G_{23}G23​ agrees with E2/2(1+ν23)=3.10E_2/2(1+\nu_{23}) = 3.10E2​/2(1+ν23​)=3.10 GPa. If you change E1E_1E1​ and E2E_2E2​ but keep the Poisson’s ratios, ν21\nu_{21}ν21​ becomes 5.7 and the determinant becomes −1.03: this is not a material. If you type the minor ratio 0.018 as ν12\nu_{12}ν12​, all the checks pass, but the sideways contraction of the ply is 18 times too small. Only the source can show this error.
    4. Propellant. The relaxed modulus of the grain is 3.6 MPa and its bulk modulus is 3.5 GPa (Propellant grain). Thus ν=0.499829\nu = 0.499829ν=0.499829, G=1.20G = 1.20G=1.20 MPa, C10=0.600C_{10} = 0.600C10​=0.600 MPa and D1=5.7×10−4D_1 = 5.7 \times 10^{-4}D1​=5.7×10−4 MPa⁻¹. An estimated ν=0.49\nu = 0.49ν=0.49 gives K=60K = 60K=60 MPa, which is 58 times too soft in volume. At 10 MPa, the volume of the grain would decrease by 16.7%, not 0.29%. The ignition-strain term that uses p/Kp/Kp/K on the grain page would be 10/6010/6010/60, not 10/350010/350010/3500, and the predicted bore strain would increase from 5.7% to 71%. If you round in the other direction, to 0.4999, KKK is 1.7 times too stiff.
    5. Aluminium closure, thermal values. After burnout, heat goes back into an aluminium closure and it gets to 150 °C. The instantaneous α\alphaα increases from 21×10−621 \times 10^{-6}21×10−6/K at −50 °C to 25×10−625 \times 10^{-6}25×10−6/K at 150 °C. The closure is assembled at 20 °C, and this is the reference temperature. The secant coefficient to 150 °C is 23.7×10−623.7 \times 10^{-6}23.7×10−6/K, and the thermal strain is 3081 µε. If you enter the instantaneous value of 25 as a secant value, the closure grows 5.5% too much. If the tables of the card stopped at 100 °C, the solver would use the stiffness, strength and expansion at 100 °C for all temperatures up to 150 °C.

    Where this stops

    Section titled “Where this stops”
    • Cards, not data. All the numbers on this page are examples. Design values come from your sources and tests.
    • Simple forms. The plasticity here is isotropic hardening from a monotonic curve. Cyclic loads, kinematic hardening, rate dependence of metals, damage and failure need more.
    • Linear expansion data. Real coefficients are curves. The calculator uses a straight line between two points.
    • Composites need failure criteria, through-thickness data and process data that the elastic card does not have.

    On the drawing

    Section titled “On the drawing”

    The card is not on the drawing, but the two must agree:

    • Material and condition (specification, temper or heat treatment) are the same as the source of the card.
    • Process-dependent properties (fibre volume, winding angle, cure, propellant batch and age) are controlled on the drawing or its process specifications if the model uses them.
    • The analysis report gives each card with its source, basis, temperature range and revision.
