Random vibration

In one lineA random environment is power spread over frequency. Each part responds mostly to the band around its own natural frequency, and you design it for three times its rms response.
Before thisFatigue · Margin policy
Used byRocket motor hardware · Storage & ageing

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

Vibration finds the problems that the static analysis did not find. Examples are a bracket sized for 10 g that gets 30 g at resonance, a connector that frets, a nut that becomes loose, and a harness that wears through. A solid motor is shaken during transport and handling, on the vehicle, and by its own burn. A static load has one value. A random environment does not: it is a spectrum. Each part responds to the region of the spectrum near its own natural frequency.

The PSD: power spread over frequency

Section titled “The PSD: power spread over frequency”

A random environment is specified as an acceleration spectral density (ASD, often called the PSD) in g²/Hz. This tells how much of the mean-square acceleration is in each 1 Hz band.

  • The area under the ASD is the mean square, in g². Its square root is the overall level, Grms.
  • Specifications give breakpoints, connected by straight lines on log-log axes, with slopes in dB per octave. Here a decibel is 10log⁡1010\log_{10}10log10​ of an ASD ratio, and an octave is a doubling of frequency.
  • +3 dB doubles the ASD, and increases Grms by 2\sqrt{2}2​ (41%). +6 dB doubles Grms.

The area of one segment with a slope of sss dB/oct, where n=s/3.01n = s/3.01n=s/3.01, is:

Think of it like…

A graphic equaliser

The PSD is the equaliser display of the vibration. It shows how loud each frequency band is. Grms is the overall volume. Two environments with the same Grms can sound very different. They can also break different parts, because each part responds only to its own band.

What Grms means

Section titled “What Grms means”
  • The rms of a zero-mean signal is its standard deviation. Thus a response of 10 Grms has σ = 10 g.
  • Design to 3σ. A Gaussian response is within ±3σ for 99.73% of the time. Thus 3 × Grms is the usual peak.
  • Some peaks are higher. For a narrow-band response, the peaks follow a Rayleigh distribution: a fraction e−k2/2e^{-k^2/2}e−k2/2 of them are more than kσk\sigmakσ. This is 1.1% above 3σ and 0.03% above 4σ. A two-minute test at 150 Hz has 18,000 cycles, thus approximately 200 peaks are above 3σ and 6 are above 4σ. If one overload can break the part (for example a brittle material, a bond or a ceramic), 3σ is not sufficient.

Miles’ equation: a part picks its own band

Section titled “Miles’ equation: a part picks its own band”

Install a part on a base and shake the base. A single mode amplifies the input near its natural frequency fnf_nfn​ by up to QQQ (the transmissibility peak). At 2 fn\sqrt{2}\,f_n2​fn​ it passes the input without change, and above that it isolates it. If the input is approximately flat across the resonance, Miles’ equation gives the rms response:

Grms=π2 fn Q  ASD(fn)Q=12ζG_\text{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}\, f_n\, Q\; \text{ASD}(f_n)} \qquad Q = \frac{1}{2\zeta}Grms​=2π​fn​QASD(fn​)​Q=2ζ1​

This is a peak response ASD of Q2×ASDQ^2 \times \text{ASD}Q2×ASD over an effective bandwidth of π2 fn/Q\tfrac{\pi}{2}\,f_n/Q2π​fn​/Q. Only the input near fnf_nfn​ is important. This is why the frequency of a part decides its environment.

Think of it like…

A child on a swing, pushed at random

Random pushes come at all times. Only the pushes that occur in rhythm with the swing make it go higher. Most of the other pushes cancel. A swing with good bearings (high Q) goes higher, but it responds only to a smaller range of rhythms.

Assumptions: one mode; light damping; an input that is approximately flat across the half-power bandwidth fn/Qf_n/Qfn​/Q; a rigid base whose motion the part does not change; and a Gaussian, steady input. The calculator compares Miles with the full integral of input × transmissibility². The integral does not need the flatness assumption.

The octave rule: keep the natural frequency of a part at least one octave away from that of the structure that holds it. The usual target is two times higher. If you do not, the two resonances multiply, and the part gets a much larger input than the input at the base.

From response to design load

Section titled “From response to design load”
  • The 3σ acceleration is 3 Grms3\,G_\text{rms}3Grms​ of the response. The equivalent static load is F=m⋅3Grms⋅gF = m \cdot 3G_\text{rms} \cdot gF=m⋅3Grms​⋅g. Apply it at the centre of mass, in the axis of the vibration, one axis at a time.
  • The margin uses the factor of your programme on that load, against the allowable at the mount, as in Margin policy. The method to combine random loads with steady loads (add or root-sum-square) is a programme rule.
  • Displacement. The motion relative to the base is zrms≈Grms g/(2πfn)2z_\text{rms} \approx G_\text{rms}\,g/(2\pi f_n)^2zrms​≈Grms​g/(2πfn​)2. With Miles it changes as fn−3/2f_n^{-3/2}fn−3/2​. Thus a softer mount has fewer g, but it moves much further. Check the 3σ displacement against all clearances.

Fatigue: Steinberg’s three bands

Section titled “Fatigue: Steinberg’s three bands”

A narrow-band response cycles at approximately fnf_nfn​. Thus an exposure of TTT seconds is approximately fnTf_n Tfn​T cycles. Steinberg’s method puts 68.3% of the cycles at 1σ stress, 27.1% at 2σ and 4.33% at 3σ. It then adds the damage with Miner’s rule on the S-N curve of the part.

The S-N line is steep: life changes with stress to a power of approximately 6 to 12. Thus the rare 3σ cycles cause most of the damage. An increase of +3 dB in the input multiplies the stress by 1.41 and the damage by 1.41m1.41^m1.41m. This is 8 times for m=6m = 6m=6, and approximately 70 times for m=12m = 12m=12. If you double the duration, the damage only doubles. For this reason, test levels are controlled in dB.

Stress-based and strain-based failure

Section titled “Stress-based and strain-based failure”

Each strength check or life check compares one quantity with its capability. The correct quantity depends on what controls the load, and if the material stays elastic.

  • Stress-based checks compare a stress with a stress allowable: static strength against FtyF_{ty}Fty​ and FtuF_{tu}Ftu​, and high-cycle fatigue on an S-N curve. They are valid while the material stays elastic, so that stress follows load. Most vibration of metal hardware is in this region: 10510^5105 to 10910^9109 cycles at stresses much less than yield.
  • Strain-based checks compare a strain with a strain capability. They are necessary when stress is not a good measure:
    • the material yields in each cycle, for example a notch root at proof, or a part cycled through large thermal or pressure loads. The stress then stops increasing, but the strain continues to increase;
    • the deformation is imposed, for example by thermal mismatch, cure shrinkage or an enforced deflection. The geometry then sets the strain, for all stiffnesses;
    • the stress relaxes. Propellant, liners and elastomers are viscoelastic. The stress decreases, but the strain stays (see Propellant grain);
    • the plies share a strain, not a stress. Composite laminates are usually designed to strain allowables.

The strain-life equation adds the elastic line of Basquin to the plastic line of Coffin–Manson. The transition life 2Nt2N_t2Nt​ is where they are equal:

Miner’s rule: adding up the damage

Section titled “Miner’s rule: adding up the damage”
D=∑iniNifailure predicted at D=1D = \sum_i \frac{n_i}{N_i} \qquad \text{failure predicted at } D = 1D=i∑​Ni​ni​​failure predicted at D=1

Each block of nin_ini​ cycles at one amplitude uses ni/Nin_i/N_ini​/Ni​ of the life at that amplitude. NiN_iNi​ comes from the S-N curve or the strain-life curve, as applicable to the cycle. The Fatigue page has the details and a calculator.

  • Count the cycles first. Use the three bands of Steinberg for a narrow-band random response, rainflow counting for a wide-band or measured history, and a table of blocks for a spectrum of discrete events.
  • Add all the environments that the part gets in its life. The bracket of a flight motor collects damage from transport, from each acceptance test (and each retest), and from flight: D=Dtransport+Dacceptance+DflightD = D_\text{transport} + D_\text{acceptance} + D_\text{flight}D=Dtransport​+Dacceptance​+Dflight​. Qualification tests are higher and longer than acceptance tests, so that one unit shows margin on that total. The levels and durations come from the test standard.
  • The rule is linear and does not consider the sequence. Real damage depends on the sequence: large cycles at the start usually decrease the remaining life at small amplitudes. Also, the measured D at failure has a large scatter around 1. Thus programmes set a limit on the damage sum below 1, or apply a factor on life. These are programme rules.
  • Cycles below the endurance limit cause no damage in the rule. But occasional large cycles can decrease the limit. The Haibach extension continues the line below the limit.
  • Propellant grains use cumulative-damage models with the same principle. Damage comes from cool-downs, thermal cycles, transport and ageing, at rates that depend on strain or stress, temperature and time. The calculation is similar to Miner’s rule, but the material model is different. See Storage & ageing.

What it means for a solid rocket motor

Section titled “What it means for a solid rocket motor”
ItemWhat vibration doesWhat to check
Nozzle and exit coneA heavy mass cantilevered from the aft closure, often with a low first mode3σ load at the nozzle-to-closure joint, displacement against the skirt and actuators, joint preload
Installed units: brackets, electronics, actuators, sensorsAmplified at their own frequency; connectors fret, fasteners become looseMiles and the 3σ load at the mount, the octave rule against the skirt, locking features
Harnesses and racewaysBundles move and wear across spans with no supportTie-down spacing, clearance to edges, strain relief at connectors
Igniter housing and closure fittingsMass on a boss; thread and seal loads3σ load on the thread and boss; seal compression kept at peak load
Propellant grain and bondlinesA heavy, well-damped viscoelastic mass, much stiffer at vibration frequencies than in storageBondline stress at the modes of the grain; cumulative damage over long transport
Case, skirts and attach ringsThe load path to the vehicle and fixtures; sound can drive thin skirts and panelsSkirt and ring loads; acoustic environment for thin panels; local modes near installed units

The environment comes from four sources:

  • Transport and handling. Low levels for long durations by road, rail, air or sea, over the life of the motor. Many hours of cycles add up. See Storage & ageing for the surveillance that finds the damage.
  • Launch or carriage. The environment of the vehicle at the motor interface, from the environment specification of the programme.
  • The burn of the motor. Combustion and flow shake the case across a wide band, and pressure oscillations can increase at the acoustic modes of the bore. Their prediction is a ballistics and test subject. When the environment is defined, the method on this page is applicable to the hardware.
  • Test. Qualification and acceptance on a shaker, with levels, tolerances and durations from the applicable test standard.

    Worked example: a 4 kg unit on an aft-skirt bracket

    Section titled “Worked example: a 4 kg unit on an aft-skirt bracket”

    An electronics unit of 4 kg is installed on a bracket on the aft skirt. The bracket gives fn=150f_n = 150fn​=150 Hz with Q=10Q = 10Q=10. The input at the skirt is the illustrative 6.79 Grms profile above, for 120 s in each axis. The mount allowable is 2000 N (illustrative), and the factor of safety is 1.25 for this example only.     2. 3σ load: 3×9.66=29.03 \times 9.66 = 29.03×9.66=29.0 g, so F=4×29.0×9.81=F = 4 \times 29.0 \times 9.81 = F=4×29.0×9.81= 1137 N.
    3. Margin: the design load is 1.25×1137=14211.25 \times 1137 = 14211.25×1137=1421 N, and MS=2000/1421−1=MS = 2000/1421 - 1 = MS=2000/1421−1= +0.41.
    4. Displacement: 0.106 mm rms and 0.32 mm at 3σ. This is much less than the 2 mm clearance to the adjacent harness.

    In this test, strength limits the bracket, not fatigue. Two changes show the trade-offs:

    • Increase the input by 3 dB (0.08 g²/Hz on the plateau). The damage increases by approximately 70 times, to 1.2×10−31.2\times10^{-3}1.2×10−3. If you double the duration, the damage only doubles.
    • Make the bracket softer, to fn=75f_n = 75fn​=75 Hz. The response decreases to 6.5 g rms, but the motion increases by 2.7 times, to 0.85 mm at 3σ. Also, a mode near the mode of the skirt would not obey the octave rule.

    Where this stops

    Section titled “Where this stops”
    • One mode on a rigid base. Real parts have several modes. Use the modal effective masses and a finite-element random response. On a shaker, the rigid fixture over-tests heavy items, compared with a flexible flight mount. Force limiting and notching (NASA-HDBK-7004) must be agreed with the programme.
    • Gaussian, steady input. Transport has shocks and non-Gaussian peaks. Pyroshock and other transients use a shock response spectrum. Sine vibration is a separate test.
    • Linear. Gaps, friction and preload loss change Q with level.
    • Steinberg is an approximation for narrow-band responses. For wide-band responses, use rainflow counting on a time history, or a spectral method such as the Dirlik method.
    • The grain is viscoelastic. Its stiffness and damping depend on frequency and temperature. See Viscoelasticity.

    On the drawing

    Section titled “On the drawing”
    • The drawing gives what the analysis assumed: fastener torque and locking features, staking or bonding of components, harness tie-down spacing, and minimum clearances.
    • The test specification gives the environment: levels, tolerances, axes and durations. The drawing or the qualification plan refers to it. It does not repeat it.
