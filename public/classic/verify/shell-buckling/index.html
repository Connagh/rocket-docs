Shell buckling

In one lineA case that easily holds 12 MPa from inside can collapse under one atmosphere from outside. Without pressure, stability limits a thin shell, not strength.
Before thisCase sizing (where the thin wall comes from)
RelatedRocket motor hardware (skirts, joints and load paths)

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

With pressure inside it, a motor case is a tension structure, and tension structures do not buckle. But a case is without pressure for most of its life: in storage and transport, on the launcher, on top of a booster that is already firing, after burnout, and during manufacture and test. In these conditions it is a thin shell in compression. Thin shells fail by buckling. They fail suddenly and elastically, at stresses much lower than yield, and much lower than the prediction of classical theory.

These are the loads on a case without pressure:

  • Axial compression: an upper-stage motor that carries the inertia of all the items above it while the stage below fires; stacking and handling; thrust that goes through a motor that is not yet burning.
  • Bending: ground winds on a vehicle on the launcher, lifting and transport, and flight loads before ignition.
  • External pressure: a case with a vacuum inside, for a vacuum leak check or for processing; a sealed case that cools down; submerged launch; pressure differences across closures.

Buckling is a stiffness problem. The critical stress depends on Young’s modulus and on t/rt/rt/r, not on the yield strength. Thus a stronger alloy gives no benefit unless it is also stiffer, or unless the wall becomes thicker.

Why thin shells buckle early

Section titled “Why thin shells buckle early”

For a perfect cylinder, classical theory gives the axial buckling stress:

σcl=E tr3 (1−ν2)  ≈  0.605 E tr(ν=0.3)\sigma_\text{cl} = \frac{E\,t}{r\sqrt{3\,(1-\nu^2)}} \;\approx\; 0.605\,\frac{E\,t}{r} \qquad (\nu = 0.3)σcl​=r3(1−ν2)​Et​≈0.605rEt​(ν=0.3)

Real cylinders in axial compression buckle much below this value. Thin cylinders buckle at a quarter of it or less. The cause is imperfections: out-of-roundness, weld shrinkage, thickness variation, small dents, load eccentricity and imperfect end supports. Axial compression is the worst case, because the buckling of the perfect shell is unstable. When it starts, the load that the shell can carry decreases quickly. A small initial dent puts the shell directly onto that decreasing path.

Think of it like…

Standing on an empty drinks can

Stand carefully on an empty aluminium can and it holds your weight. Hit its side lightly with a pencil and it collapses immediately. The material did not change. The pencil made a dent, and a thin shell in compression is very sensitive to dents. A motor case is a very large, very strong drinks can.

The SP-8007 knockdown

Section titled “The SP-8007 knockdown”

Designers do not try to predict the imperfections of each shell. They multiply the classical result by an empirical knockdown factor γ\gammaγ, which is a lower limit of many years of test results. For unstiffened cylinders, NASA SP-8007 gives:

Bending is gentler

Section titled “Bending is gentler”

In bending, only a narrow strip of the shell carries the peak compression. Thus it is less probable that the worst imperfection is exactly there. The knockdown is smaller, and the bending stress is:

σb=Mπr2t\sigma_b = \frac{M}{\pi r^2 t}σb​=πr2tM​

External pressure is a different problem

Section titled “External pressure is a different problem”

Under external pressure, the shell collapses into lobes around its circumference. For a cylinder of moderate length with its ends held round (Batdorf):

pcl=1.04Z  π2E t312 (1−ν2) r L2,Z=L2r t1−ν2p_\text{cl} = \frac{1.04\sqrt{Z}\;\pi^2 E\,t^3}{12\,(1-\nu^2)\,r\,L^2}, \qquad Z = \frac{L^2}{r\,t}\sqrt{1-\nu^2}pcl​=12(1−ν2)rL21.04Z​π2Et3​,Z=rtL2​1−ν2​

This gives pcl∝E t2.5/(L r1.5)p_\text{cl} \propto E\,t^{2.5} / (L\,r^{1.5})pcl​∝Et2.5/(Lr1.5). A very long tube becomes oval, with the limit pcl=E (t/r)3/(4(1−ν2))p_\text{cl} = E\,(t/r)^3 / \left(4(1-\nu^2)\right)pcl​=E(t/r)3/(4(1−ν2)). There are three results:

  • The wall thickness has the largest effect. The buckling pressure increases as t2.5t^{2.5}t2.5 to t3t^3t3, not as ttt.
  • The length is important. If you halve the distance between the features that hold the shell round, the buckling pressure doubles. A stiffening ring does this.
  • It is less sensitive to imperfections than axial compression. SP-8007 applies one correlation factor, 0.56, to lateral pressure.

Think of it like…

The crushed tank car

A steel rail tank car that is cleaned with steam and sealed while it is still hot can collapse when the steam inside condenses. Only the atmosphere pushed on it, approximately 0.1 MPa. A shell designed to hold pressure from inside can have very little resistance to the same pressure from outside.

Combined loads

Section titled “Combined loads”

SP-8007 recommends straight-line interactions for axial compression with bending, and for axial compression with lateral pressure. The sum of all three is a simple screening check:

Ra+Rb+Rp≤1,R=appliedallowableR_a + R_b + R_p \le 1, \qquad R = \frac{\text{applied}}{\text{allowable}}Ra​+Rb​+Rp​≤1,R=allowableapplied​

Does buckling govern at all?

Section titled “Does buckling govern at all?”

If γ σcl\gamma\,\sigma_\text{cl}γσcl​ is more than yield, the wall yields first, and buckling is not the problem. Between the two, as the buckling stress approaches the proportional limit of the material, the elastic formula gives a value that is too high, and a plasticity correction is necessary. The calculator tells you in which regime you are.

Internal pressure is a stiffener

Even a small internal pressure puts the wall in tension, removes imperfections and cancels compression. The stainless-steel “balloon” tanks of the early Atlas rocket were so thin that they had to stay pressurised, also on the ground. Without pressure, they would collapse under their own weight. Liquid-propellant tanks use this effect on purpose. A solid motor is usually at ambient pressure until ignition. Thus the wall itself must carry the load cases without pressure.

    • Vent the case. Never put a vacuum in, or seal and cool, a thin empty case before you check this load case.
    • Add a ring at mid-length. L=500L = 500L=500 mm doubles the buckling pressure: the reserve becomes 2.45.
    • Make the wall 10% thicker. The buckling pressure increases by 27%, and the reserve becomes 1.57. But this adds mass everywhere.

    Where this stops

    Section titled “Where this stops”
    • Unstiffened, isotropic, elastic cylinders with their ends held round. Shells with rings and stringers, cones (NASA SP-8019) and domes (NASA SP-8032) have their own methods. A dome under external pressure is also very sensitive to imperfections.
    • Composite cases are orthotropic. Their buckling depends on the bending and membrane stiffnesses of the laminate, and the knockdowns are different.
    • Local load introduction at skirts, joints, lugs and support cradles causes local compression that a uniform membrane check does not see. This needs a finite-element analysis.
    • Knockdowns are lower limits, not predictions. Less conservative designs use measured imperfections, non-linear analysis and test.
    • Plasticity. Near the proportional limit, the elastic formula is unconservative.
    • Internal pressure makes the shell more stable. This page treats only the case without pressure.
    • A case-bonded grain supports the wall as an elastic foundation, and it increases the buckling load of a loaded motor. Usually it is ignored for conservatism. It is not present in an empty case. This is why manufacture and test are often the most critical buckling cases.

    On the drawing

    Section titled “On the drawing”
    • Control roundness and dents. The knockdown assumes imperfections that are not worse than those in the test data. The out-of-roundness tolerance and the dent acceptance criteria on the cylinder are buckling requirements as well as manufacturing requirements.
    • Use the minimum wall. Calculate buckling allowables on the minimum thickness that the drawing permits, after machining and any chemical milling.
    • Define the supports. Give the lifting points, cradle positions and support pads on the drawing or in the handling specification, because they decide where the local compression goes.
    • Tell what must not occur. If the external-pressure reserve is small, write “Vent before you apply a vacuum” or “Do not seal while hot” in the handling notes.
    • Stiffening rings are structure. Their attachment, weld inspection and tolerances need the same care as the shell.
