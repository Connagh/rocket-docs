Margin policy

In one lineA margin has a meaning only when the load, the factor and the allowable are a consistent set: yield with yield, ultimate with ultimate, at the same condition.
Before thisAllowables (where the allowable comes from)
Used byCase sizing · Shell buckling · Fatigue

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

The margin of safety is the main result of a stress report. There is one number for each failure mode. It tells if a part is sufficiently strong, and by how much. Reviewers do not check each calculation. They check that each margin comes from a consistent set. Most review findings come from four errors:

  • Mixed pairs: a yield allowable against an ultimate load, or the opposite.
  • The incorrect basis: a typical value or a room-temperature value in a hot check with a single load path.
  • Missing factors: a fitting factor or casting factor that the specification requires, but that is not used.
  • Too much conservatism: a pessimistic load, times a pessimistic allowable, times a pessimistic model. This gives a heavy part and a margin that nobody believes.

Load levels

Section titled “Load levels”
  • Limit load is the most severe load that is expected in service. The load rules of your programme define it. For a pressure vessel, it is MEOP.
  • Yield design load is the limit load × the yield factor. At this load, there must be no yield that has an effect on the function: no important permanent set, no leak and no misalignment.
  • Ultimate design load is the limit load × the ultimate factor. At this load, there must be no rupture or collapse.
  • For pressure vessels, the proof and burst factors have these functions. See case sizing and proof testing.

The margin of safety

Section titled “The margin of safety”
MS=allowablek×applied−1pass when MS≥0MS = \frac{\text{allowable}}{k \times \text{applied}} - 1 \qquad \text{pass when } MS \ge 0MS=k×appliedallowable​−1pass when MS≥0

Here kkk is the factor for that check. The applied value is at limit level, and the allowable has the correct basis at the correct condition. European practice often gives the reserve factor, RF=MS+1RF = MS + 1RF=MS+1, which passes when RF≥1RF \ge 1RF≥1.

The formula is correct with stresses only when the stress is proportional to the load. For buckling, contact, preloaded joints, large deflections and plasticity, apply the factor to the load, and do the analysis at the factored load: MS=failure load/(k×limit load)−1MS = \text{failure load} / (k \times \text{limit load}) - 1MS=failure load/(k×limit load)−1. It is a programme rule if thermal stresses and preloads get a factor, and which factor. Give this rule in the report.

Think of it like…

A bathroom scale

A scale that shows pounds and a weight limit in kilograms can both be correct, and the comparison can still be incorrect. A margin is the same. The allowable, the factor and the load can each be correct, but they must be in the same “units”: yield with yield, ultimate with ultimate, the same temperature and the same direction.

Pairing: the most common finding

Section titled “Pairing: the most common finding”

The case-sizing worked example has a hoop stress of 1094 MPa at MEOP, a yield allowable of 1305 MPa and an ultimate allowable of 1368 MPa (with the weld efficiency), with example factors of 1.10 and 1.25:

PairingMarginResult
Yield allowable, yield factor1305/(1.10×1094)−1=+0.081305 / (1.10 \times 1094) - 1 = +0.081305/(1.10×1094)−1=+0.08Correct
Ultimate allowable, ultimate factor1368/(1.25×1094)−1=0.001368 / (1.25 \times 1094) - 1 = 0.001368/(1.25×1094)−1=0.00Correct
Yield allowable, ultimate factor1305/(1.25×1094)−1=−0.051305 / (1.25 \times 1094) - 1 = -0.051305/(1.25×1094)−1=−0.05A failure that is not real
Ultimate allowable, yield factor1368/(1.10×1094)−1=+0.141368 / (1.10 \times 1094) - 1 = +0.141368/(1.10×1094)−1=+0.14A pass that is not real

Always calculate yield and ultimate as separate rows.

Special factors

Section titled “Special factors”

Specifications often require additional factors for some features. These multiply the basic factor:

FactorApplies to
Fitting factorJoints and fittings, where the stress state is not certain
Bearing factorBearing at fastener holes with clearance or vibration
Casting factorCast parts, related to the inspection level
Weld factorWelded joints, related to the procedure and the inspection
Model-uncertainty factorAnalysis methods that a test has not validated

If a necessary factor is missing, all the margins that follow from it are incorrect. As with the basic factors, their values come from your specification. This site does not give them.

Combined loading

Section titled “Combined loading”
  • The same stress component from several loads. Add the stresses at limit level, then apply the factor to the total. For ductile metals under combined normal stress and shear stress, use von Mises: σvm=σ2+3τ2\sigma_{vm} = \sqrt{\sigma^2 + 3\tau^2}σvm​=σ2+3τ2​.
  • Different load types or failure modes, for example a fastener in tension and shear, or a shell under axial load, bending and pressure. Use an interaction equation:
∑iRi ei≤1,Ri=k×appliediallowablei\sum_i R_i^{\,e_i} \le 1, \qquad R_i = \frac{k \times \text{applied}_i}{\text{allowable}_i}i∑​Riei​​≤1,Ri​=allowablei​k×appliedi​​

Linear interaction (e=1e = 1e=1) is the most conservative. Elliptical interaction (e=2e = 2e=2) is usual for fasteners in tension and shear. Use the form and the exponents that your applicable standard specifies. For spaceflight fasteners, this is NASA-STD-5020.

The margin tells how much all the loads can increase together before they get to the curve: MS=λ−1MS = \lambda - 1MS=λ−1, where ∑(λRi)ei=1\sum (\lambda R_i)^{e_i} = 1∑(λRi​)ei​=1. For linear interaction this is 1/∑Ri−11/\sum R_i - 11/∑Ri​−1. For elliptical interaction it is 1/R12+R22−11/\sqrt{R_1^2 + R_2^2} - 11/R12​+R22​​−1. A frequent shortcut, 1/∑Ri2−11/\sum R_i^2 - 11/∑Ri2​−1, is incorrect. It gives a margin that is much too high.

Conservatism and the heavy-part rule

Section titled “Conservatism and the heavy-part rule”

Conservatism is acceptable when you know where it is. Record its location (the load, the allowable or the model), so that you can remove it on purpose if mass becomes a problem, and do not find it only at the end. On a part where mass is important, a large margin is also a finding. A general rule: above approximately 0.5, the part is heavier than necessary, or a different failure mode or load case governs and you have not found it yet.

    Worked example: a motor case and its closure bolts

    Section titled “Worked example: a motor case and its closure bolts”

    The rows of the calculator are the 300 mm D6AC case from the other pages, plus its aft-closure bolts. The factors are example values only: yield 1.10, ultimate 1.25, buckling 1.25, and a fitting factor of 1.15 on the bolts. Push Load worked example to load them.

    1. Hoop yield at proof: 1305/(1.10×1094)−1=+0.081305 / (1.10 \times 1094) - 1 = +0.081305/(1.10×1094)−1=+0.08.
    2. Hoop burst: 1368/(1.25×1094)−1=0.001368 / (1.25 \times 1094) - 1 = 0.001368/(1.25×1094)−1=0.00. The margin is exactly zero, because the case was sized to burst.
    3. Buckling of the empty case under vacuum: 0.1262/(1.25×0.101)−1=−0.00040.1262 / (1.25 \times 0.101) - 1 = -0.00040.1262/(1.25×0.101)−1=−0.0004, shown as −0.00. This is a fail. A negative margin fails, however small. See shell buckling.
    4. Closure bolt, tension and shear: 18 kN on a 60 kN allowable, and 4 kN on 40 kN (illustrative). The factor is 1.25×1.15=1.441.25 \times 1.15 = 1.441.25×1.15=1.44, so R1=0.431R_1 = 0.431R1​=0.431 and R2=0.144R_2 = 0.144R2​=0.144. Elliptical interaction gives λ=1/0.4312+0.1442=2.20\lambda = 1/\sqrt{0.431^2 + 0.144^2} = 2.20λ=1/0.4312+0.1442​=2.20, so MS = +1.20. Linear interaction would give +0.74. The incorrect shortcut 1/(R12+R22)−11/(R_1^2 + R_2^2) - 11/(R12​+R22​)−1 would give +3.84.

    The lowest margin is the buckling of the empty case at −0.00, so the case governs and the report has a fail. Add a ring or a thicker wall (as on the shell-buckling page), or support the case on a mandrel during the vacuum check. The hoop burst at 0.00 is next. The bolt margin of +1.20 is a finding under the heavy-part rule. Either a different check sizes these bolts (preload, joint separation or thread engagement) and needs its own row, or the bolts could be smaller. Bolted joints does those checks for the same closure.

    Where this stops

    Section titled “Where this stops”
    • Factors, load definitions and margin rules come from the requirements of your programme, for example NASA-STD-5001 or ECSS-E-ST-32-10 for spaceflight structures. This page tells how they fit together. It does not give them.
    • Linear responses only for stress-based margins. Non-linear responses need load-based margins at the factored load.
    • Fatigue and fracture use life and flaw size, with their own factors. See Fatigue and Proof testing.

    On the drawing and in the report

    Section titled “On the drawing and in the report”
    • The drawing gives what the margins assumed: material specification and temper, finish, inspection class, and weld and casting quality levels. If a margin needed a casting factor related to an inspection level, that level must be on the drawing.
    • The report gives the full set: for each row, the load, the factor and the allowable with their sources, and the lowest margin for each item at the start.
