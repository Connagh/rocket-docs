Test correlation

In one lineCorrelation asks if the errors of the model are small, and if they are small for the correct reason. Only then can you use the model where there were no gauges.
Before thisFEA & test · Proof testing
Used byMargin policy · Random vibration

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

A test measures a part at some tens of points, for one or two load cases. The model predicts all the other results: the stresses where there were no gauges, the flight loads that the test could not apply, and the next design. Correlation checks the model where you can compare it with the test. If the two agree for the correct reasons, you can use the model where you cannot compare them.

Correlation can also go very wrong. If you adjust any parameter until the model agrees with the test, the model can agree perfectly and predict nothing. This page is about the difference between correlating a model and tuning it.

Think of it like…

The bathroom scale

You can check a bathroom scale with a known weight and read the result. Or you can turn the adjustment wheel until it shows the number that you want. The two methods give the “correct” reading. Only the first one tells you something about the next thing that you weigh.

What to compare

Section titled “What to compare”
  • Compare the same quantities. A strain gauge gives the average over its grid length, on one surface, in one direction. Take the strain from the model in the same way. An accelerometer measures one direction at one point and adds its own mass. Read the model at that point, with that mass.
  • Use the test article as tested. Include its fixture, instrumentation, cables, measured dimensions and inert or real fill. Do not use the flight configuration.
  • Compare slopes, not points. Use strain against load through the test, not one reading at the peak. The slope removes zero offsets. The shape of the line shows non-linearity, the seating of joints, and yield (a reading that does not go back to zero).
  • Use all the gauges, also the ones that do not agree. To remove a gauge, you must have evidence that the gauge failed, and you must record it before the comparison.

Static correlation: strain gauges

Section titled “Static correlation: strain gauges”

Compare each gauge as a ratio, εtest/εmodel\varepsilon_{test}/\varepsilon_{model}εtest​/εmodel​, and then look at the full set:

  • The mean ratio shows a global bias: load calibration, modulus, or wall thickness in all locations.
  • The scatter shows how well the model gives the distribution of strain.
  • The pattern shows which gauges have an error and in which directions. This pattern shows the cause (see below).

The acceptance criterion comes from your programme. It must be wider than the resolution of the gauges. A reading has errors from the gauge-factor tolerance, transverse sensitivity, misalignment, position in a gradient and the instrumentation. Two of these errors need care:

  • Transverse sensitivity is important where the strain across the gauge is large. An axial gauge on a pressurised case has four times its own strain across it. The error is systematic, and you can correct it with the other readings of the rosette.
  • Misalignment is almost not important along a principal direction (the error is second order). It is very important at 45° to a principal direction (first order). Hoop and axial gauges on a cylinder are tolerant of misalignment. Shear gauges are not.

Modal correlation: frequencies and shapes

Section titled “Modal correlation: frequencies and shapes”

A modal survey measures the natural frequencies and the mode shapes at the accelerometer points. Compare:

  • Frequencies. Compare the model with the test for each mode, as a percentage.
  • Shapes, with the modal assurance criterion (Allemang and Brown):
MAC(ϕa,ϕt)=∣ϕaTϕt∣2(ϕaTϕa)(ϕtTϕt)\text{MAC}(\phi_a, \phi_t) = \frac{\left|\phi_a^{\mathsf T}\phi_t\right|^2}{\left(\phi_a^{\mathsf T}\phi_a\right)\left(\phi_t^{\mathsf T}\phi_t\right)}MAC(ϕa​,ϕt​)=(ϕaT​ϕa​)(ϕtT​ϕt​)​ϕaT​ϕt​​2​

MAC is 1 for the same shape at any scale, and 0 for shapes that are not related. Pair each test mode with the model mode that has the highest MAC. Do not use the nearest frequency, because modes can change their order.

  • Check the instrumentation with the AutoMAC, which compares the test modes with each other. With too few points, different shapes look the same and all the MAC values are too high.
  • MAC does not use mass. If you can reduce the mass matrix of the model to the test points, cross-orthogonality ΦaTMΦt\Phi_a^{\mathsf T} M \Phi_tΦaT​MΦt​ is a better check.
  • Criteria come from standards such as NASA-STD-5002 and ECSS-E-ST-32-11. Typical values are a few per cent on the frequencies of the main modes, and a cross-orthogonality of approximately 0.9 on the diagonal, with the off-diagonal terms at approximately 0.1 or less. Use the values of your applicable document.

Finding why they differ

Section titled “Finding why they differ”

The pattern of the differences usually shows the cause:

PatternLikely cause
All gauges off by the same ratioLoad or pressure calibration, modulus, or wall thickness in all locations
One station off by the same ratio in all directionsLocal thickness or material. Measure the as-built part
One direction off at a stationThe gauge (orientation, bonding, wiring), or local bending that the model does not have
Gauges near a joint or weld offJoint stiffness, weld mismatch or peaking, local geometry
Lowest mode furthest off, higher modes closeSupport or joint stiffness: fixture, bolted interface
All modes off by approximately the same ratioMass or modulus. Weigh the article, and check the fill and the instrumentation mass
A test mode with no model partnerA missing component, joint freedom or local mode, or too few measurement points
Readings that change with amplitudeNon-linearity: joint slip, gaps, seating. The model is linear

Updating the model correctly

Section titled “Updating the model correctly”
  • Measure before you adjust. Measure the as-built thickness, mass and fixture stiffness, and get the material certificates. A measured value replaces an assumption and does not need more justification.
  • Change physical parameters only within their known range. The stiffness of a bolted joint can have an uncertainty of ±50%. The modulus of a steel has an uncertainty of a few per cent. If the change necessary to close the gap is far outside the plausible range, the error is in a different location. The model does not include something.
  • Make one change at a time, with its reason. Record each change, its justification and its effect.
  • Keep some data separate. Check the updated model against gauges, modes or tests that you did not use for the update. A change that agrees with the data used to make it shows little. A change that also predicts the other data is a correlation.
  • If the model does not correlate, say so. Use the remaining difference as model uncertainty. Programmes often apply an additional factor to models that do not correlate. Do not force a fit.

The sensitivity of a frequency to a parameter gives a numerical plausibility check. The sensitivity is S=∂ln⁡f/∂ln⁡pS = \partial\ln f/\partial\ln pS=∂lnf/∂lnp. It is 0.5 for a stiffness that carries the full mode, and −0.5 for a mass. The change that closes a gap without other changes is:

Δpp=(ftestfmodel)1/S−1\frac{\Delta p}{p} = \left(\frac{f_{test}}{f_{model}}\right)^{1/S} - 1pΔp​=(fmodel​ftest​​)1/S−1

    Worked example: correlating the proof test and a modal survey

    Section titled “Worked example: correlating the proof test and a modal survey”

    The readings, mode shapes and frequencies are illustrative. The criteria (±5% on gauges, ±5% on frequencies, and a MAC of at least 0.9) are for this example only. Push Load worked example to apply them.

    1. Proof-test gauges. The 300 mm D6AC case has a proof test at 11 MPa with eight gauges. From the nominal 1.377 mm wall, the model predicts membrane strains of 5021 µε hoop and 1233 µε axial on the cylinder, and 4169 µε on the hemispherical aft dome. Six gauges agree within 2.2%. G5 and G6 are hoop and axial gauges at one station, 250 mm from the forward dome. They read 6.8% and 6.7% low. The mean ratio is 0.985, the scatter is 3.5%, and two gauges are outside ±5%.
    2. The pattern. The two directions at one station are low by the same ratio. A gauge fault affects one direction. A global error affects all gauges. The same ratio, in two directions, at one location, shows that the wall is thicker there. An ultrasonic thickness measurement at the station gives 1.47 mm. With the measured wall, the model gives 4703 µε and 1155 µε. G5 and G6 now agree within 0.5%, all gauges are in the band, and the scatter decreases to 1.3%.
    3. The incorrect alternative. An increase of 7% in the modulus also corrects G5 and G6. But it moves the other six gauges 5–9% out of the band. One measurement corrected a problem that a tuned parameter would have made worse.
    4. Can the gauges resolve ±5%? Take the axial gauge G4 (1260 µε, with 4950 µε across it). A gauge factor of ±1%, a transverse sensitivity of 0.5%, a misalignment of 2° and ±5 µε from the instrumentation give ±30 µε, 2.4%. Most of this comes from transverse sensitivity, which you can correct. A band of ±5% is resolvable. A band of ±2% is not.
    5. Modal survey. A bracket holds an electronics unit on the aft skirt (the 150 Hz unit of the random-vibration example). It has a survey with six accelerometers. The test and model frequencies are 138 and 150 Hz, 905 and 940 Hz, and 2590 and 2632 Hz. The model is 8.7%, 3.9% and 1.6% high. The MAC pairs each mode with its partner at 0.998 or more. The AutoMAC of the test is up to 0.11 off the diagonal. Six points cannot separate these shapes better, but the separation is sufficient. Mode 1 does not meet the ±5% criterion.
    6. What could cause the mode 1 difference? When the lowest mode has the largest error, the root joint is a probable cause. To close the 8% gap without other changes, the modulus would have to be 15% lower or the density 18% higher. This is not possible for a metal bracket. Alternatively, the thickness would have to be 8% less, which is outside its ±4%. The stiffness of the root joint has an uncertainty of ±50% and a sensitivity of 0.35 from the model. It must be 21% softer, which is plausible. With this change, mode 2 moves to 914 Hz and mode 3 to 2601 Hz. The two are now within 1% of the test, but you did not use them to select the change. This check makes the change a correlation.

    Where this stops

    Section titled “Where this stops”
    • Linear, deterministic comparison. Statistical correlation, Bayesian updating and formal model-updating methods go further. See Friswell and Mottershead.
    • Single-parameter plausibility. Real updates can change several parameters together. They then need more data than unknowns.
    • MAC is not cross-orthogonality. It does not use mass, and it cannot show if the modes of the model are orthogonal through the test points.
    • Criteria (gauge bands, frequency and orthogonality limits) come from your programme and the applicable standards.

    On the drawing and in the test documents

    Section titled “On the drawing and in the test documents”
    • The gauge and accelerometer map: locations, directions, surfaces and grid lengths, the same as the read-out points of the model.
    • As-built measurements that the correlation uses: wall thickness at gauge stations, mass and centre of gravity.
    • The test configuration: fixture, fill, instrumentation mass and cable routing. Record these so that the model can include them.
    • Calibration records for the transducers and the data system.
