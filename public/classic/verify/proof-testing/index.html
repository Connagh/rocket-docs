Proof testing

In one lineA proof test does not show that a vessel is perfect. It makes sure that the flaws in each vessel that passes are smaller than a size that you can calculate.
Before thisCase sizing (MEOP, proof and burst; leak before break)

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

Usually, each solid motor case has a proof test before it gets its insulation and propellant. It is the last check between manufacture and a vessel that will hold an energetic load for years. A proof test finds:

  • Workmanship and material errors: an incorrect heat treatment, a weak weld, a wall machined too thin, or a bad batch of material.
  • Cracks that nobody found. This is the less obvious result. It is the reason why proof is part of the fracture-control case, and not only a quality check.
  • Leaks at welds, bosses and seals.

What a proof test proves, and what it does not

Section titled “What a proof test proves, and what it does not”

Think of it like…

A sieve

Pour a bag of gravel through a sieve. You never look at one stone, but you know that each stone that went through is smaller than the mesh. A proof test is a sieve for cracks. Each case with a crack that is sufficiently large to fail at proof pressure fails on the test stand, safely. Each case that survives has only cracks smaller than the “mesh size”. This is also true for cracks that no inspection could find.

It does not prove:

  • That the case has margin to burst. The proof pressure is below burst on purpose. Analysis, and tests to failure of qualification articles, show the burst capability.
  • That flaws stay small. Cracks grow with each pressurisation and, in some environments, under sustained load. The proof screen gives the starting size for a life analysis. It is not the end of the analysis.
  • That the case survives loads other than pressure: thrust, bending, handling and external pressure.
  • That the test caused no damage. Proof is the highest stress that the part has before flight. It can grow sub-critical cracks. In a susceptible material and environment, it can leave the case in a worse condition than before.

Choosing the proof factor

Section titled “Choosing the proof factor”

The proof pressure is MEOP times a proof factor (see the pressure ladder). Requirements in two directions set the factor:

Increases the factorDecreases the factor
Smaller screened flaw, thus more crack-growth lifeThe proof stress must stay below yield, with margin for local peaks
Must cover the strength lost at the service temperatureThe test can grow cracks and cause sustained-load damage
Must cover the toughness lost at the service temperatureComposites: fibre damage, stress rupture and damage accumulation
Must screen to a size that NDT cannot reliably seeGood hardware lost in the test, schedule and cost

The applicable standard sets the factor for the class of vessel. The fracture-control plan checks that it gives sufficient life. This site never gives the value. The calculator below starts with this field empty. Two corrections are usually added:

  • Temperature. If the case is weaker at its service temperature than at the test temperature, increase the proof pressure by the ratio of the strengths. The ratio of proof stress to strength then covers the service condition.
  • Toughness. The screen works only if the material is at least as tough in the test as in service. The toughness of high-strength steels usually decreases with temperature. Thus a warm proof test can screen nothing for a cold-day firing. The calculator below shows how quickly this occurs.

    Worked example: the D6AC case

    Section titled “Worked example: the D6AC case”

    This is the case from the case-sizing example: a hoop stress of 1094 MPa at the 10 MPa MEOP, a 1.377 mm wall, KIC≈70K_{IC} \approx 70KIC​≈70 MPa√m, a proof factor of 1.10, and a long surface crack (Y=1.12Y = 1.12Y=1.12). The proof factor and the life factor are for this example only. Push Load worked example in the calculator to load these inputs.

    1. Proof stress. 1.10×1094=12041.10 \times 1094 = 12041.10×1094=1204 MPa, which is 83% of the 1450 MPa yield.
    2. Screened flaw. 1π(701.12×1204)2=0.86\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{70}{1.12 \times 1204}\right)^2 = 0.86π1​(1.12×120470​)2=0.86 mm. No case that passes proof has a crack deeper than this.
    3. Critical flaw at MEOP. 1π(701.12×1094)2=1.04\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{70}{1.12 \times 1094}\right)^2 = 1.04π1​(1.12×109470​)2=1.04 mm. The ratio is 1.102=1.211.10^2 = 1.211.102=1.21.
    4. Life. For martensitic steel, the upper limit of Barsom is da/dN=1.35×10−10 ΔK2.25da/dN = 1.35 \times 10^{-10}\,\Delta K^{2.25}da/dN=1.35×10−10ΔK2.25. With full cycles from zero to MEOP, the crack takes approximately 105 pressurisations to grow from 0.86 to 1.04 mm. For one firing plus one leak check, with a life factor of 4, the requirement is 8. The margin is approximately 13.
    5. NDT. If inspection can only find cracks deeper than approximately 1.3 mm, it cannot support this case alone, because the critical size is smaller than the size that NDT can find. The proof test carries the fracture case.

    The same numbers give two warnings:

    • Cold service. If the toughness at −40 °C is 20% lower (56 MPa√m), the ratio becomes 1.21×0.82=0.771.21 \times 0.8^2 = 0.771.21×0.82=0.77. Cases that passed a room-temperature proof can then have cracks that fail on a cold day. The proof screen has no value unless the test is done cold, or unless you show that the toughness does not decrease.
    • Deep cracks. The critical crack is 75% of the wall. At that depth, a constant YYY gives a value of KKK that is too low. Thus the real sizes are smaller. This is also the break-before-leak case that the case-sizing page identified. A released analysis uses a correct surface-crack solution.

    Test media and safety

    Section titled “Test media and safety”

    Why we proof with liquid

    Section titled “Why we proof with liquid”

    Think of it like…

    A full suitcase versus a compressed spring

    If you sit on a suitcase full of books, it compresses only a little, and almost nothing springs back when you stand up. A liquid is the same: it almost does not compress, so it stores very little energy. A gas is a coiled spring. All the work used to compress it comes back out at the moment when the vessel opens.

    The energy released if the vessel fails is:

    Eliquid=p2V2KEgas=pVγ−1[1−(patmp)γ−1γ]Ewall=12 p ΔVE_\text{liquid} = \frac{p^2 V}{2K} \qquad E_\text{gas} = \frac{p V}{\gamma - 1}\left[1 - \left(\frac{p_\text{atm}}{p}\right)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\right] \qquad E_\text{wall} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,p\,\Delta VEliquid​=2Kp2V​Egas​=γ−1pV​[1−(ppatm​​)γγ−1​]Ewall​=21​pΔV

    For the 85-litre example case at 11 MPa, water stores approximately 2.3 kJ, and the elastic strain energy of the steel wall adds 5.2 kJ. The total is 7.6 kJ, which is approximately 2 g of TNT. The same test with nitrogen stores approximately 1.7 MJ, which is approximately 0.4 kg of TNT: more than 200 times more. This is why proof tests are hydrostatic unless there is a very good reason, and why a pneumatic proof test needs special approval, remote operation and much larger exclusion zones.

    Trapped air is gas energy inside a hydrostatic test. It is most important in large tests at low pressure, where the liquid stores little energy. Fill the vessels from the bottom, and bleed them from the highest point until only liquid comes out.

    The medium must not attack the vessel

    Section titled “The medium must not attack the vessel”
    • Stress-corrosion cracking. High-strength steels can crack under sustained stress in plain water. Thus the proof water is inhibited and its chemistry is controlled, the hold times are short, and the case is drained and dried immediately.
    • Chemistry is important for all alloys. Titanium pressure tanks failed during tests with methanol in the Apollo era, and chlorides attack stainless steels. Check the test medium against the material as carefully as you check the propellant.
    • Temperature. Steel cases are tested well above any ductile–brittle transition, and at a temperature that keeps the toughness argument above valid.

    The rig and the schedule

    Section titled “The rig and the schedule”
    • Remote operation. Nobody is near a pressurised vessel. The cell is cleared and barricaded before the pressure goes above a low leak-check level.
    • A relief valve set above the proof pressure and below any pressure that would damage the vessel, with rupture discs as backup.
    • Two independent, calibrated pressure measurements. One gauge that reads low can over-pressurise a case.
    • Pressurisation in steps with holds, and a slow final ramp. Thus a leak or an abnormal strain reading stops the test early.
    • Records. The gauge calibrations, the liquid temperature, the pressure and strain histories, and the serial number of the part make the acceptance record.

    Acceptance criteria

    Section titled “Acceptance criteria”

    A case passes proof when it meets all the criteria of the test specification. Usually these are:

    1. No burst and no leak. Leaks are checked visually and by the pressure hold, after the temperature correction.
    2. No significant permanent deformation. Measure it by volumetric expansion (water jacket), strain gauges or dimensional checks before and after. A frequent convention for gas cylinders limits the permanent expansion to 10% of the total expansion at test pressure. Motor cases use limits specific to the programme.
    3. The strain follows the prediction. Strain gauges must follow the predicted elastic response linearly. A knee in the strain–pressure curve shows local yield.
    4. NDT after proof, to look for cracks that the test possibly grew.
    5. Composite cases add acoustic-emission criteria, because fibre damage and matrix damage make noise long before you can see them.

    Why hold pressure drifts

    Section titled “Why hold pressure drifts”

    A vessel full of liquid is very stiff. Thus very small volume changes cause large pressure changes. A change in liquid temperature changes its volume:

    dpdT=βliquid−3αvessel1/K+C\frac{dp}{dT} = \frac{\beta_\text{liquid} - 3\alpha_\text{vessel}}{1/K + C}dTdp​=1/K+Cβliquid​−3αvessel​​

    Here β\betaβ is the volumetric expansion coefficient of the liquid, α\alphaα is the linear expansion coefficient of the vessel, and C=1VdVdpC = \frac{1}{V}\frac{dV}{dp}C=V1​dpdV​ is the volumetric compliance of the vessel. For the example case it is approximately 0.12 MPa for each kelvin, which is more than 1% of the proof pressure for one degree. Most “leaks” during a pressure hold are the liquid that cools.

      Where this stops

      Section titled “Where this stops”

      Screening, not a fracture-control plan

      These tools use linear-elastic fracture mechanics with a constant geometry factor and growth data typical of the class. A released proof-test case uses measured toughness and crack-growth data at the correct temperature and environment, surface-crack and embedded-crack solutions, crack growth during the proof cycle, sustained-load (environment-assisted) cracking, and the factors of the applicable standard.

      • Thin, tough walls can tear in a ductile way before linear-elastic fracture mechanics applies. Then you need elastic-plastic methods.
      • Composite cases are not screened in this way. Their proof logic is about damage, stress rupture and acoustic emission, not one crack.

      Differences from REF·DB v1

      Section titled “Differences from REF·DB v1”

      Crack-growth constants corrected

      v1 gave the ferrite–pearlite constants (6.9×10−126.9\times10^{-12}6.9×10−12, m=3.0m = 3.0m=3.0) the label “low-alloy / maraging steel”.
