Propellant properties

In one lineYou do not select the propellant, but you must use its numbers. All of these numbers change with temperature.
Before thisHow a solid motor works (burn rate and the pressure balance)
Used byCase sizing (MEOP) · Propellant grain (strain capability)

What this page covers, and what it does not cover

On this page, propellant is a material with properties: how it burns, how stiff and strong it is, how it ages, and which materials can touch it. There are no formulations, proportions, processing or burn-rate tailoring. These are the responsibility of your energetics and propulsion specialists and their safety authority.

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

Two of the numbers that size motor hardware come from the propellant. The first is the MEOP, which is the design pressure of the case, the joints and the proof test. The second is the strain capability, which decides if the bore cracks on a cold night. Both change with temperature much more than the properties of a metal. Between −40 °C and +60 °C, the illustrative propellant on this page changes its chamber pressure by approximately 22%. Its stiffness changes by more than ten times, as a function of the loading rate.

The hardware designer does not select these numbers. But you must know where they come from, what they depend on, and exactly which numbers to ask for.

Temperature: why the hot day sets MEOP

Section titled “Temperature: why the hot day sets MEOP”

A propellant grain that starts warm burns faster. Over a firing-temperature range, an exponential describes this effect well:

a(T)=a0 e σp(T−T0)a(T) = a_0\,e^{\,\sigma_p (T - T_0)}a(T)=a0​eσp​(T−T0​)

The pressure balance gives pc∝a1/(1−n)p_c \propto a^{1/(1-n)}pc​∝a1/(1−n). Thus at constant KnK_nKn​, the chamber pressure follows the same law, with a larger coefficient:

pc(T)=pc(T0) e πK(T−T0),πK=σp1−np_c(T) = p_c(T_0)\,e^{\,\pi_K (T - T_0)}, \qquad \pi_K = \frac{\sigma_p}{1 - n}pc​(T)=pc​(T0​)eπK​(T−T0​),πK​=1−nσp​​

At constant KnK_nKn​, the burn rate increases by the same factor. Thus the burn time decreases as e−πK(T−T0)e^{-\pi_K (T-T_0)}e−πK​(T−T0​). A hot motor pushes harder for a shorter time. The total impulse almost does not change, because the same propellant burns with almost the same c∗c^*c∗. Thus the hot and cold pressure traces have almost the same area.

Think of it like…

Kindling from a sunny windowsill

Kindling that was in the sun catches fire and burns faster than kindling from a cold shed. The flame uses less of its heat to bring the fuel to its burning temperature, so it moves on sooner. Warm propellant is the same. In a motor there is a second step. Faster burning increases the pressure, which increases the burn rate again (the 1/(1−n)1/(1-n)1/(1−n) amplification), until the throat flow balances it.

Building MEOP

Section titled “Building MEOP”

MEOP is the nominal pressure taken to the worst firing condition of the motor, plus the manufacturing variation:

  1. Hot day. Take the nominal peak pressure at the reference temperature to the hottest firing temperature with πK\pi_KπK​.
  2. Manufacturing variation. Each variation is amplified by 1/(1−n)1/(1-n)1/(1−n):
    • burn rate: the scatter in aaa from batch to batch;
    • burning area: grain dimensions and bore tolerances;
    • throat area: a smaller throat gives a higher pressure.
  3. Combining them. Independent variations are usually combined by root-sum-square (RSS). A worst-case stack puts each variation at its limit at the same time, and it is more conservative. Your programme sets the method and the probability level. This page does not.
  4. Transients from firings. These are the ignition spike, erosive burning early in the burn in long, narrow bores, and grain deformation under pressure. They come from test data, and you add them to the result.

Ask for the build-up, not only the number

A MEOP without its temperature range has little meaning. Ask your ballistics team for the build-up: the hottest firing temperature, the variations included, the combination method, and if the transients are included. If the operating temperature range increases later in the programme, MEOP also increases, and so does the case wall.

    The difference between nominal and MEOP is 1.22 MPa. Of this, the hot day gives 0.73 MPa (60%). Usually, temperature is the largest single term, not manufacturing.

    Part 2: Mechanical properties

    Section titled “Part 2: Mechanical properties”

    Stiffness depends on the clock and the thermometer

    Section titled “Stiffness depends on the clock and the thermometer”

    Composite propellant is a highly filled rubber. As with Silly Putty, its stiffness depends on how fast you load it and how cold it is. Cold has the same effect as fast. This equivalence is time–temperature superposition. It lets one set of laboratory tests describe years of storage and also milliseconds of ignition.

    The shift factor aTa_TaT​ connects the two. It tells how many times slower all relaxation processes are at the temperature TTT than at a reference temperature TsT_sTs​. For many polymers, which include propellant binders, it follows the Williams–Landel–Ferry (WLF) equation:

    log⁡10aT=−C1 (T−Ts)C2+T−Tsξ=taT\log_{10} a_T = \frac{-C_1\,(T - T_s)}{C_2 + T - T_s} \qquad\qquad \xi = \frac{t}{a_T}log10​aT​=C2​+T−Ts​−C1​(T−Ts​)​ξ=aT​t​

    A load applied for a time ttt at a temperature TTT has the same effect as a load applied for the reduced time ξ\xiξ at TsT_sTs​. Read the stiffness for that ξ\xiξ from the master curve of the propellant. This is its relaxation modulus, measured over many decades of time and shifted to one temperature.

      There are three results for hardware:

      1. Thermal strain analyses use the relaxed modulus, and ignition analyses use the stiff, short-time modulus. The grain page does this.
      2. The bond stress at a cold ignition is high, because the grain is stiff at the time when the case stretches.
      3. Measure the capability at the rate and temperature of each load case. There is no single strength.

      Strength and strain capability

      Section titled “Strength and strain capability”

      Strength is measured on dog-bone tensile specimens across a matrix of temperatures and strain rates. Each test gives the maximum stress σm\sigma_mσm​, the strain at maximum stress εm\varepsilon_mεm​ and the strain at break εb\varepsilon_bεb​. Design margins usually use εm\varepsilon_mεm​ or εb\varepsilon_bεb​, as the programme specifies.

      • Dewetting. At higher strains, the binder pulls away from the filler particles. Small voids open, the volume of the material increases, and the stress–strain curve bends over. This sets εm\varepsilon_mεm​.
      • Pressure helps. A superimposed pressure (as at ignition) prevents dewetting and increases the strain capability. Thus capability measured at atmospheric pressure is conservative for ignition. Programmes test under pressure, or they accept the conservatism.
      • Biaxial is worse than uniaxial. The bore surface has strain in two directions at the same time, and the biaxial capability is usually lower than the uniaxial capability. It is measured on biaxial specimens, or the analysis rules of the programme include it.
      • Rate and temperature collapse onto one curve. If you plot stress at break against strain at break, the results from all rates and temperatures are on a single failure envelope (Smith, 1963). This is the strength equivalent of the master curve.
      • Damage accumulates. Each thermal cycle uses part of the capability. Long-life motors are assessed with cumulative-damage methods, not one load case at a time.

      Ageing

      Section titled “Ageing”

      Propellant properties continue to change in storage. The hardware margins must be sufficient at the end of life, not only on the delivery day.

      • Composite propellants usually become harder. The binder continues to cross-link, so the modulus increases and the strain capability decreases. Plasticiser can migrate into the liner and insulation. Where the oxidiser is hygroscopic, the propellant absorbs moisture.
      • Nitrate-ester propellants also have a chemical limit. The stabiliser is used up over time, and this sets a safe life that is separate from the mechanical life.
      • What this means for hardware. Grain and bondline margins are calculated with aged properties. The ageing evidence comes from accelerated and real-time ageing, and from surveillance motors taken from the stockpile. See Storage & ageing.

      What may touch it: materials compatibility

      Section titled “What may touch it: materials compatibility”

      A material is not “compatible with propellant” in general. Nitrate-ester propellants and composite propellants degrade by different mechanisms. Thus the same metal can be excluded near one and only controlled near the other. Read across the row. A test shows compatibility. A datasheet does not. STANAG 4147 is the applicable agreement. It uses methods such as vacuum stability, heat-flow calorimetry and, for nitrocellulose-based propellants only, stabiliser depletion.

      Two important rules

      The copper rule. Copper and copper alloys are excluded near nitrate-ester (double-base) propellant. Do not use brass fittings, bronze bushes, beryllium-copper tools, solder that contains copper, or copper plating. This applies to the hardware, the tooling, the handling equipment and the store. A bought-in part can easily break this rule.

      The silicone rule. Silicone is not permitted in most propellant areas and bond areas. It does not react, but it migrates, transfers on contact and destroys adhesive bonds. Bondlines are structure, so a silicone fingerprint can cause a motor to fail. This includes release agents, sealants, lubricants and hand creams.

      Where this stops

      Section titled “Where this stops”
      • r=a pnr = a\,p^nr=apn is a fit over a pressure range. Some propellants change their exponent within their operating range. Use the law only over the range of the fit.
      • The exponential temperature law is also a fit. It is valid over the conditioning range that it came from.
      • The MEOP build-up shows the mechanism. The real calculation follows the statistical method of your programme and includes transients measured on firings.
      • The master curve and its constants are illustrative. Real analyses use measured relaxation data and fitted shift factors, and treat dewetting, pressure and ageing separately.
      • The contents of the propellant and how it is made are outside the scope of this page.

      On the drawing

      Section titled “On the drawing”
      • Specify the propellant by its specification or designation, never by a description of its contents. Batch acceptance (burn rate at reference conditions within a band, density and mechanical properties) is in the propellant specification. The motor drawing refers to this specification.
      • Give the operating and storage temperature limits on the motor drawing or interface document. MEOP and all grain margins are valid only within these limits.
      • Name each material that can touch propellant (metal, finish, seal, adhesive, release agent, and also temporary covers) from the approved list. Never write “or equivalent”, and never let the shop select.
      • Define the bondlines as structure: surface preparation, liner, cure and inspection. See Insulation.
