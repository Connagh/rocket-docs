Insulation & ablative liners

In one lineThickness = what the flame removes (erosion + char), with margin, plus sufficient virgin material to keep the bondline cool, also after burnout.
Before thisPropellant grain (exposure comes from burn-back) · Case sizing

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

Combustion gas is at approximately 3000 K. High-strength case steels lose strength quickly above a few hundred °C. Composite case resins become soft at approximately 120–200 °C, and the adhesive that bonds the insulation to the case can have a lower limit. Only the insulation is between the gas and these materials. If it is too thin, the case or the bond becomes too hot or burns through. This is a catastrophic failure. If it is too thick, it is inert mass, which decreases the performance in the same way as case mass. Insulation can be a large part of the inert mass of a motor. Thus it is sized for each region, not with one thickness for all regions.

Two jobs at once

Section titled “Two jobs at once”

An ablative insulator protects the structure in two ways:

  1. It gives up its surface on purpose. Heat decomposes the rubber or resin (pyrolysis) and leaves a porous carbon char. The decomposition absorbs heat. The gases that it releases flow out through the char and make the boundary layer thicker, which blocks some of the incoming heat (“blowing”). The flow removes the char slowly. This is erosion.
  2. The virgin material behind it insulates. Its low conductivity keeps the heat near the surface, so the bondline and the case stay cool.

Think of it like…

A log in a campfire

A thick log burns for hours, because its outside becomes charcoal, and charcoal is a good insulator. The wood inside stays brown and cool while the surface glows. If you hit the log and knock the char off, it burns much faster. Motor insulation is the same. The char layer protects the material behind it. Anything that removes the char (fast gas, or aluminium-oxide droplets that hit the surface) makes the attack faster.

Material affected depth (MAD) is all the material that the flame has used: eroded material plus char. You measure it when you cut sections of insulation after a firing. It is the basis of most insulation design rules.

Think of it like…

An oven glove versus a cast-iron pan

A cast-iron pan does not burn on the hob, but its handle becomes too hot to hold. It resists the flame and passes the heat on. An oven glove chars if you hold it in the flame, but your hand stays cool. Graphite and carbon-carbon are similar to the pan: very good surface resistance, but poor insulation. Rubbers and phenolics are similar to the glove. This is why a graphite throat insert always has an insulator behind it.

Exposure time sets the thickness

Section titled “Exposure time sets the thickness”

Insulation works only while hot gas touches it. Thus the first question for each location is: how long is it exposed? The grain design gives this directly.

  • Under the grain, propellant covers the cylinder insulation until the web burns through. The exposure is only the tail-off, so this is usually the thinnest insulation in the motor.
  • Domes and grain ends are exposed from ignition, so their exposure is almost the full burn time.
  • The aft dome and the nozzle entrance also have the fastest flow, which changes direction, and aluminium-oxide particles hit the surface. Thus erosion is the largest effect. The thickest and toughest grades go here.

With a star grain or a slotted grain, the flame gets to the case under the star tips long before it gets to the case under the valleys. Thus the exposure, and the necessary thickness, change around the circumference, not only along the motor:

The physics of charring

Section titled “The physics of charring”

Heat soaks in as the square root of time

Section titled “Heat soaks in as the square root of time”

Assume that the insulation is a thick slab, and that its surface temperature suddenly becomes high and stays high. Heat spreads into it by conduction. The temperature increase at a depth xxx after a time ttt is:

T−T0Ts−T0=erfc⁡ ⁣(x2αt),α=kρ cp\frac{T - T_0}{T_s - T_0} = \operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}\right), \qquad \alpha = \frac{k}{\rho\,c_p}Ts​−T0​T−T0​​=erfc(2αt​x​),α=ρcp​k​

Here α\alphaα is the thermal diffusivity. All depths change with αt\sqrt{\alpha t}αt​. If you double the time, the heat goes only approximately 1.4 times deeper.

Think of it like…

Roasting a joint of meat

The cooking time changes with the square of the thickness. This is why a joint two times as thick takes approximately four times as long, not two times. The same √t law sets how far heat goes into insulation. For insulation, this is good: a long burn causes less damage for each second than simple arithmetic suggests.

Erosion changes the shape of the problem

Section titled “Erosion changes the shape of the problem”

If the surface also moves back at a steady rate vvv, the exact solution for a semi-infinite solid with its surface at TsT_sTs​ is:

Here ξ\xiξ is the depth below the current surface. The char front is where θ\thetaθ decreases to the pyrolysis level θp=(Tp−T0)/(Ts−T0)\theta_p = (T_p - T_0)/(T_s - T_0)θp​=(Tp​−T0​)/(Ts​−T0​). Two limits make it easy to understand:

  • No erosion (v=0v = 0v=0): the char grows as t\sqrt{t}t​ with no limit.
  • Long times: the profile becomes θ=e−vξ/α\theta = e^{-v\xi/\alpha}θ=e−vξ/α, which moves with the surface. The char layer stops its growth at a steady thickness:
δchar=αv ln⁡1θp\delta_{\text{char}} = \frac{\alpha}{v}\,\ln\frac{1}{\theta_p}δchar​=vα​lnθp​1​

Thus char controls short burns and erosion controls long burns. This is why the erosion rate is the number that insulation designers discuss most. It depends on the local flow, not only on the material.

After burnout: soak-back

Section titled “After burnout: soak-back”

When the motor stops, the hot char and the insulation still contain much heat. There is no gas flow to remove it. Thus much of it flows inward, because that is the only direction that it can go. The bondline often gets to its peak temperature minutes after burnout, long after the insulation surface has started to cool.

Think of it like…

A stone pizza oven

Remove the fire from a stone oven and it continues to cook for hours, because the heat stored in the stone continues to flow into the bread. The insulation layer is the stone. The bondline and the case are the bread.

The quantity of stored heat that goes inward depends on how the motor cools after burnout: radiation to the cold nozzle opening, venting, and heat absorbed by continued pyrolysis. The calculator gives two limits: insulated face (all the stored heat goes inward, which is conservative) and radiating face (the surface radiates to cold surroundings, which is optimistic for most of a closed case). For hardware behind the liner that is thicker than a case wall (polar bosses, nozzle housings, seal grooves), see Transient thermal.

Sizing rule

Section titled “Sizing rule”

There are two requirements, and the thicker result applies:

t  ≥  SF×MADandTbond,peak(t)  ≤  Tlimitt \;\ge\; SF \times \text{MAD} \qquad\text{and}\qquad T_\text{bond,peak}(t) \;\le\; T_\text{limit}t≥SF×MADandTbond,peak​(t)≤Tlimit​

There is also a minimum gauge for manufacture and handling. The safety factor on the affected depth comes from the requirements of your programme. It is often different for each region. It is larger where the uncertainty is larger, for example at the aft dome. The calculator starts with this field empty.

    Worked example: EPDM over a thin steel case

    Section titled “Worked example: EPDM over a thin steel case”

    Aramid-filled EPDM (k=0.25k = 0.25k=0.25 W/m·K, ρ=1100\rho = 1100ρ=1100 kg/m³, cp=2000c_p = 2000cp​=2000 J/kg·K) is on the 1.4 mm D6AC case from the case-sizing example, in a low-velocity region with an exposure of 20 s. The effective surface temperature is 1200 °C, the char front is at 450 °C, the erosion is 0.025 mm/s, the bondline limit is 150 °C, and the safety factor is 1.5 for the example only. Push Load worked example in the calculator to load these inputs.

    1. Diffusivity. α=0.25/(1100×2000)=1.14×10−7\alpha = 0.25 / (1100 \times 2000) = 1.14 \times 10^{-7}α=0.25/(1100×2000)=1.14×10−7 m²/s, so αt=1.51\sqrt{\alpha t} = 1.51αt​=1.51 mm at 20 s.
    2. Char front level. θp=(450−20)/(1200−20)=0.364\theta_p = (450 - 20)/(1200 - 20) = 0.364θp​=(450−20)/(1200−20)=0.364.
    3. Without erosion, the char would get to 2αt  erfc⁡−1(0.364)=3.02×0.64=1.932\sqrt{\alpha t}\;\operatorname{erfc}^{-1}(0.364) = 3.02 \times 0.64 = 1.932αt​erfc−1(0.364)=3.02×0.64=1.93 mm.
    4. With erosion, 0.5 mm of surface is removed. The moving-surface solution puts the char front 1.64 mm below the new surface. The affected depth at burnout is 0.5+1.64=2.140.5 + 1.64 = 2.140.5+1.64=2.14 mm. The transient model of the calculator gets the same 1.64 mm, and the automated tests check this agreement.
    5. Soak-back moves the char front a little deeper after burnout: MAD ≈ 2.4 mm, so 1.5×MAD=3.61.5 \times \text{MAD} = 3.61.5×MAD=3.6 mm.
    6. Bondline. To keep the peak bondline temperature below 150 °C, you need approximately 8.9 mm with the insulated-face assumption (the peak is approximately 6 minutes after burnout), or 6.7 mm with the radiating face.

    The bondline governs, with a large margin. The affected depth alone gives 3.6 mm. But a 1.4 mm steel case has very little heat capacity to absorb the soak-back. Try the carbon/epoxy case, a 60 s exposure, or the high-velocity regime, and see which requirement then governs. The steady char thickness is (α/v)ln⁡(1/θp)=4.6(\alpha/v)\ln(1/\theta_p) = 4.6(α/v)ln(1/θp​)=4.6 mm. This shows that, at this erosion rate, the char layer never becomes thicker than approximately 4.6 mm, for any burn time.

    Choosing the material

    Section titled “Choosing the material”
    MaterialWhere it is usedStrengthsPossible problems
    EPDM, aramid-fibre filledCase insulation, most regionsLowest density and conductivity, good ageing, good bondLower erosion resistance in high-velocity flow with particles
    EPDM, carbon-fibre filledAft domes, high-flow regionsTougher char that adheres betterHigher conductivity than aramid grades
    NBR, silica filledCase insulation (older and some current designs)Good propellant compatibility, tough charHigher density; previously filled with asbestos, now not used
    Silicone, silica filledFlexible joints, wide-temperature serviceChar with much silica, flexible when coldBonding needs special primers
    Carbon phenolicNozzle throat region, entrance (large motors)High erosion resistance, good insulationPrecursor supply; delamination and pocketing if made badly
    Silica / glass phenolicExit cones, insulation behind insertsLower cost and lower conductivity than carbon phenolicLower erosion resistance; silica melts at high heat flux
    Carbon-carbonThroats and exit conesVery low erosion, light, strong when hotConducts heat, so it needs backing insulation; cost; oxidation
    Bulk graphiteThroat inserts in small motorsLow cost, low erosionCracks from thermal shock; very conductive

    Select in this sequence:

    1. Exposure and flow first. Long exposure in fast flow with particles needs an erosion-resistant grade. For a short exposure with gentle flow, conductivity and density are the important properties.
    2. Then mass. Density × thickness is the cost. In low-velocity regions, an insulator with slightly less erosion resistance but much lower density is often the better choice.
    3. Compatibility with the propellant. Plasticiser and burn-rate catalysts can migrate from the propellant into the liner and the insulation over years of storage. This makes the bond weaker and changes the propellant near it. The liner formulation is partly a barrier against this migration.
    4. Bond and processing. How the insulation is laid up (calendered sheet, cured in place, or co-cured with a composite case), and how it bonds to the case and to the propellant liner, are as important as its thermal values.
    5. Ageing. The insulation must keep its properties and its bonds for the full service life of the motor.

    Where this model stops

    Section titled “Where this model stops”

    Screening model, not a qualified thermal analysis

    The calculator keeps the surface at one effective temperature and uses constant virgin properties everywhere. It does not include the heat absorbed by pyrolysis, gas blowing, or the different properties of the char. Thus the bondline result is conservative in some ways and optimistic in others. Real insulation is sized with charring-ablation codes, calibrated with material-affected depths from sectioned motors and with bondline thermocouple data from firings.

    • Erosion is an input, not a prediction. It depends on the local velocity, particle impact, pressure, aluminium content of the propellant and char strength of the material. Use measured rates.
    • 2-D effects at joints, flaps, grain terminations and the nozzle entrance can be the largest effects locally.
    • Nozzle throats erode much faster than case insulation, and this changes the chamber pressure. The planned nozzle page will cover this.

    On the drawing

    Section titled “On the drawing”
    • Thickness map: the insulation thickness in each zone, with tolerances. Show where it is measured and with which method.
    • Material and process specifications: compound, lay-up and cure, bond surface preparation and primers.
    • Bond inspection: NDT of the bonds between case and insulation and between insulation and liner, with acceptance criteria.
    • Stress-relief flaps: the geometry, and the release agent that lets them open.
