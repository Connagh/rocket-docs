Propellant grain

In one lineThe shape of the grain sets the thrust curve. Its rubber-like mechanics decide if it survives when it is cold.
Before thisHow a solid rocket motor works · Case sizing

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

The grain is the fuel tank, the combustion chamber wall and the throttle of the motor, all at the same time. Its geometry sets how much burning surface is exposed at each instant. Thus it sets the chamber pressure and the thrust for the full burn. It also sets how much propellant fits in the case, and how high the peak pressure is. The case must be sized for this peak.

Its structural integrity is a safety subject, not a performance subject. A crack is new burning surface. More surface gives more gas and a higher pressure. Because pressure amplifies changes by 1/(1−n)1/(1-n)1/(1−n), this can cause a case failure. This is why cold-soak cracking and debonding are very important.

This page has two parts: geometry and burn-back, then structural integrity.

Part 1: Geometry and burn-back

Section titled “Part 1: Geometry and burn-back”

The burning surface moves like a grass fire

Section titled “The burning surface moves like a grass fire”

During most of a burn, each exposed point of propellant moves back perpendicular to the local surface at the same burn rate rrr. After the flame burns a distance yyy (the web burned), the surface is the set of all points that are at a distance yyy from the original surface.

Think of it like…

A grass fire spreading from a burning outline

Light the edge of a field along an irregular line. With no wind, the fire front moves into the grass at the same speed everywhere. Bumps that point into the grass become smooth arcs. Narrow areas of grass between two fronts burn from the two sides and disappear first. If you draw the front each minute, you get contour lines. The burn-back map in the simulator below uses these contours for its colours.

This has two direct results:

  • Port corners that point into the propellant (star tips, slot ends) become round arcs as they burn.
  • Propellant ridges that point into the port (between star points) stay sharp and move back quickly, because they burn from the two sides. When these ridges burn out, the burning area decreases quickly.

Shape sets the thrust curve

Section titled “Shape sets the thrust curve”
  • Tube (internal-burning cylinder). The port only becomes larger, so the burning area and the thrust increase until the end: progressive. It is simple and robust, but the peak pressure at the end of the burn sizes the case.
  • Star. The area of the points decreases while the circular region increases. You can balance these to get an almost neutral thrust. The disadvantage is a sliver behind the star valleys, unless the design adds fillets or sliver-filling inserts.
  • Core plus radial slots (wagon wheel, and the slotted section of a finocyl). A very large initial burning area for a high-thrust boost. It is usually regressive as the slots open.
  • End burner. It burns face-on, like a cigarette. It is perfectly neutral, with the longest burn time and the most propellant for the case, but low thrust. Also, the case wall behind the face has hot gas on it for the full burn.
  • BATES (stacked short tubes, ends burning). The bore increases while the ends become smaller. The two effects almost cancel.

The BATES balance is a good example of geometry analysis. For a segment of outer diameter DDD, port ddd and length LLL, with the two ends burning, the burning area after a web yyy is:

    Where the burn-back model stops

    Real motors have effects that this model does not include. Erosive burning: fast gas along the bore increases the local burn rate. This is why a low port-to-throat ratio gives an ignition spike. Also: the ignition transient; throat erosion (the pressure decreases during the burn); the burn-rate scale factor and “hump” between small test motors and full-scale grains; 3-D geometry (finocyl fins, conical ports, domed ends); and grain deformation under pressure, which changes the port. Production ballistics use full internal-ballistics codes, calibrated with motor firings.

    Part 2: Structural integrity

    Section titled “Part 2: Structural integrity”

    The material: a filled rubber with a memory

    Section titled “The material: a filled rubber with a memory”

    Composite propellant is approximately 85–90% solid particles (oxidiser and aluminium) in a rubber binder. Mechanically it is a viscoelastic solid. Its stiffness and strength depend on how fast you load it and how cold it is. The models for this are on the viscoelasticity page.

    Think of it like…

    Silly Putty

    If you pull Silly Putty slowly, it stretches a long way. If you pull it quickly, it breaks. If you put it in the freezer, even a slow pull breaks it. Propellant follows the same pattern, and cold has the same effect as fast. This equivalence (time–temperature superposition) lets a few laboratory tests describe years of storage and milliseconds of ignition.

    Three facts control all the results below:

    1. Propellant is almost incompressible. It is easy to change its shape, but its volume almost does not change (the bulk modulus is thousands of times the shear modulus). Where its volume tries to change, the geometry decides where the movement goes.
    2. Relaxation decreases the stress, not the strain. In a case-bonded grain, the geometry and the expansion coefficients set the bore strain, not the stiffness. Slow cool-downs relax the stress, but the strain stays.
    3. Capability depends on rate, temperature, pressure and age. There is no single “strain to failure”. Compare each load case with the capability measured at its own rate and temperature. The capability decreases as the propellant ages.

    Cool-down: why the bore cracks

    Section titled “Cool-down: why the bore cracks”

    Propellant cures warm and bonds to the case at that temperature, where it has no stress. Its thermal expansion coefficient is approximately 10−410^{-4}10−4/K, which is approximately eight times that of steel. Thus when it cools, it tries to shrink much more than the case. The outside is bonded to the case and cannot follow. The propellant also cannot change its volume. Thus the shrinkage must come from the bore, which opens.

    Think of it like…

    A ring doughnut glued inside a steel bangle, put in the freezer

    The doughnut tries to shrink, but its outer edge is bonded to the bangle, which almost does not shrink. The doughnut cannot become smaller from the outside, so its hole becomes larger. This stretches the inside surface around its circumference. A doughnut that is thicker relative to its hole has more volume to accommodate, and its small hole must stretch more.

    For a long, case-bonded cylindrical grain with a bore radius aaa and an outer radius bbb, let m=b/am = b/am=b/a. Because the propellant is incompressible, the area of each ring of propellant can change only by thermal expansion. This sets the displacement field. With the boundary condition at the case, the bore hoop strain on cooling by ΔT\Delta TΔT (negative for cooling) is:

    εθ(a)=ΔT[3αp−αc2 (1−m2)+m2 αc]  ≈  32 (αp−αc) ∣ΔT∣ (m2−1)\varepsilon_{\theta}(a) = \Delta T \left[\frac{3\alpha_p - \alpha_c}{2}\,(1 - m^2) + m^2\,\alpha_c\right] \;\approx\; \tfrac{3}{2}\,(\alpha_p - \alpha_c)\,|\Delta T|\,(m^2 - 1)εθ​(a)=ΔT[23αp​−αc​​(1−m2)+m2αc​]≈23​(αp​−αc​)∣ΔT∣(m2−1)

    The approximate form is within αc∣ΔT∣\alpha_c |\Delta T|αc​∣ΔT∣ (approximately 0.1%) of the full form. The important term is m2m^2m2: the bore strain increases with the square of the outer-to-bore ratio. A grain with a small bore and a thick web gives the most propellant, but it is the most difficult grain to keep free of cracks. This is the main structural trade in grain design.

    The same analysis gives the radial tension that pulls the grain off the case at the bond. It uses the relaxed modulus EEE of the propellant for this slow load:

    σr(b)=E (m2−1) (αp−αc) ∣ΔT∣  ≈  23 E εθ(a)\sigma_r(b) = E\,(m^2 - 1)\,(\alpha_p - \alpha_c)\,|\Delta T| \;\approx\; \tfrac{2}{3}\,E\,\varepsilon_\theta(a)σr​(b)=E(m2−1)(αp​−αc​)∣ΔT∣≈32​Eεθ​(a)

    A soft propellant keeps the bond stress low. It does not decrease the bore strain.

    Ignition: the case stretches, the bore stretches more

    Section titled “Ignition: the case stretches, the bore stretches more”

    At ignition, the pressure pushes the grain against the case, and the case expands by a fraction of a per cent. The propellant cannot change its volume much. Thus the small outward movement at radius bbb causes a much larger movement of the bore:

    εθ(a)=m2 εθ,case+m2−12(εz,case+pK)\varepsilon_{\theta}(a) = m^2\,\varepsilon_{\theta,\text{case}} + \frac{m^2 - 1}{2}\left(\varepsilon_{z,\text{case}} + \frac{p}{K}\right)εθ​(a)=m2εθ,case​+2m2−1​(εz,case​+Kp​)

    Here KKK is the bulk modulus of the propellant. For a thin closed-end case with a modulus EcE_cEc​, a Poisson’s ratio νc\nu_cνc​ and a wall hhh:

    εθ,case=p bEc h(1−νc2)εz,case=p bEc h(12−νc)\varepsilon_{\theta,\text{case}} = \frac{p\,b}{E_c\,h}\left(1 - \frac{\nu_c}{2}\right) \qquad \varepsilon_{z,\text{case}} = \frac{p\,b}{E_c\,h}\left(\frac{1}{2} - \nu_c\right)εθ,case​=Ec​hpb​(1−2νc​​)εz,case​=Ec​hpb​(21​−νc​)

    This connects the grain directly to case sizing. A lighter case with more stress stretches more, and the bore of the grain gets more strain. The worst case is usually a cold firing, where the cool-down strain is already present when ignition adds its share.

      Design levers

      Section titled “Design levers”
      • Larger bore / smaller web. This has the largest effect, because the strain changes as m2m^2m2. It decreases the propellant loading.
      • Stiffer or thicker case. This decreases the ignition strain directly. Composite cases are light, but they stretch more.
      • Increase the minimum firing temperature. Each degree removed from ΔT\Delta TΔT removes strain. Usually this needs an agreement at system level.
      • Decrease the stress-free temperature. A cooler cure, or a cure under pressure, decreases the cool-down ΔT\Delta TΔT.
      • Stress-relief flaps and boots at the grain ends. Where the bond stops, the stresses are much higher than the long-cylinder values above.
      • Cartridge-loaded grains (made separately and put into the case) do not have the bonded cool-down problem. But their support and inhibition are more complex. They are usual for double-base grains, which become glassy at low temperature.

      Where these formulas stop

      Section titled “Where these formulas stop”

      Screening, not qualification

      These are long-cylinder, elastic, closed-form results. A real structural analysis of a grain uses viscoelastic finite elements with measured relaxation data. It includes the grain ends and all geometric features, adds the damage over the full thermal and handling history, includes ageing, and is validated against instrumented motors and analogue tests.

      • Ends and terminations. Bond stresses at the grain ends can be several times the values above.
      • Non-circular ports. Star points, slot tips and fin roots need FEA. The concentration factor here is only an estimate.
      • Dewetting. At high strains, the binder separates from the solid particles and voids open. The material is then not incompressible, and its response changes.
      • Cumulative damage. Each thermal cycle uses some capability. Programmes monitor this with cumulative damage models and with surveillance tests of aged motors.

      Differences from REF·DB v1

      Section titled “Differences from REF·DB v1”

      Two corrections in this version

      Pressurisation strain. v1 used p2G b2b2−a2\tfrac{p}{2G}\,\tfrac{b^2}{b^2 - a^2}2Gp​b2−a2b2​. This is the bore strain of a grain with no case around it. For a soft composite propellant, it predicts strains of hundreds of per cent. In a case-bonded grain, the case controls the bore strain, as in the formula above.

      Relaxation credit. v1 decreased the cool-down bore strain by a viscoelastic relaxation factor. In a case-bonded grain, the bore strain is kinematically imposed. Thus relaxation decreases the stress but not the strain. The page now applies relaxation only where it is correct: to the bond stress, through the relaxed modulus.

      On the drawing

      Section titled “On the drawing”
      • Grain geometry: port profile, fillet radii and web, with the tolerance on each. Fillet radii at star points and slot tips are structural features, not cosmetic features.
      • Liner, insulation and inhibitor: specifications, thicknesses, and which surfaces are inhibited.
      • Bond and void acceptance: the NDT method (usually radiography or CT for voids, cracks and debonds) and its acceptance criteria.
      • Stress-relief features: the flap geometry, and the release treatment that lets the flaps separate.
      • Temperature limits: give the storage and firing temperatures in the motor specification, because the structural margin depends on them.
