Transient thermal

In one lineTwo resistances decide the result: the resistance to heat that goes into the surface, and the resistance to heat that spreads inside. Their ratio is the Biot number. It tells if one temperature is sufficient.
Before thisInsulation (soak-back through ablating liners)
RelatedPropellant properties (MEOP depends on grain temperature)

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

Seals, bondlines, adhesives, electronics and heat-treated alloys all have temperature limits. Most hardware never gets to steady state. It heats and cools in transients. Three problems occur frequently:

  • The structure is often hottest after the motor stops. When the gas stops, the heat stored in the hot parts continues to move inward, because that is the only direction that it can go. This soak-back has its peak minutes after burnout. It can damage the seals, bonds and electronics that were not damaged during the burn.
  • The outside temperature is not the inside temperature. A thin steel case follows the weather within half an hour. The propellant inside takes days. Thus a thermocouple on the case tells very little about the grain, and MEOP depends on the grain.
  • Fast heating causes gradients. When heat arrives faster than it can spread, the surface and the core can have a difference of hundreds of degrees, with the related thermal stresses and strains.

When one temperature is not enough: the Biot number

Section titled “When one temperature is not enough: the Biot number”
Bi=h LckBi = \frac{h\,L_c}{k}Bi=khLc​​

This compares the resistance to heat that spreads inside (Lc/kL_c/kLc​/k) with the resistance to heat that goes in (1/h1/h1/h). Below approximately 0.1, the inside follows the surface and the lumped model is acceptable. Above 0.1, the part has a real gradient, and the lumped results are not correct.

Think of it like…

A pea and a potato

Put a pea into boiling water and it is hot all the way through in seconds. One temperature describes it. A large potato is soft on the outside and hard in the middle for many minutes. The water supplies heat faster than the potato can carry it inward. The pea has a small Biot number, and the potato has a large one.

Soak-back

Section titled “Soak-back”

During a firing, the insulation and liners take the heat and store it. At burnout the source stops, but the stored heat stays. It flows down the temperature gradient: inward, into the structure. The back face continues to get hotter long after the gas has stopped, and it has its peak minutes later.

Think of it like…

A pan handle off the hob

Take a cast-iron pan off the hob. The handle continues to get hotter for a few minutes. The flame has stopped, but the heat stored in the pan still spreads along it. Motor hardware does the same after burnout.

These items decide the peak:

  • How much heat is stored near the surface. A thicker and hotter heated layer has more heat to release.
  • What is behind it. A thick metal part absorbs the heat, and its peak is lower and later. A thin wall has a high peak, and the peak comes sooner.
  • What occurs at the hot face after burnout. If the face can radiate or convect heat away, less heat goes inward. An insulated hot face gives the upper limit. The insulation page gives the two limits.

    2: Conditioning a grain

    Section titled “2: Conditioning a grain”

    Before a test firing, a motor is kept at its hot-limit temperature, for example 60 °C, so that the firing represents the hot-day MEOP. Assume that the 300 mm grain is a solid cylinder of propellant (k=0.4k = 0.4k=0.4 W/m·K, ρ=1770\rho = 1770ρ=1770 kg/m³, c=1300c = 1300c=1300 J/kg·K, illustrative values). It starts at 20 °C in a conditioning chamber with forced air at 60 °C (h≈10h \approx 10h≈10 W/m²K). Use the Conditioning a grain preset.

    • Bi=10×0.075/0.4=1.9Bi = 10 \times 0.075 / 0.4 = 1.9Bi=10×0.075/0.4=1.9. This is far from lumped.
    • The lumped model gives τ=1770×1300×0.075/10=4.8\tau = 1770 \times 1300 \times 0.075 / 10 = 4.8τ=1770×1300×0.075/10=4.8 h. It says that the grain gets to 58 °C in 14 hours.
    • The conduction model says that the centre gets to 58 °C after 34 hours. After six hours, the surface is at 50 °C, the average is at 40 °C and the centre is still at 29 °C. The lumped model gives 49 °C.

    For this reason, conditioning is specified in days, not hours. If a motor is fired before the heat has gone through it, its temperature is not known. It also has the thermal strains of a grain that is hot outside and cold inside.

    3: Soak-back into a seal behind a boss

    Section titled “3: Soak-back into a seal behind a boss”

    A seal is behind a 20 mm steel polar boss, with 6 mm of EPDM for protection. During a 20 s burn, the hot face of the insulation is kept at 1200 °C (an effective surface temperature, as on the insulation page). After the burn, it cools to 20 °C air at h=10h = 10h=10 W/m²K. Use the Soak-back into a boss preset.

    • At burnout, the temperature of the back face of the boss has almost not changed: 20.1 °C. The heat has not arrived yet.
    • The peak comes approximately 8 minutes after burnout, at 62 °C.
    • Behind a thin wall. The same insulation over the 1.4 mm case wall has a peak of 225 °C, less than four minutes after burnout. The heat capacity of the boss absorbs the heat. The thin wall has almost no heat capacity.

    This model has no charring, pyrolysis or erosion, so it tells nothing about the liner. Use the insulation calculator for the liner. The model shows how important the part behind the liner is.

    Where this stops

    Section titled “Where this stops”
    • Constant properties. Conductivity and specific heat change with temperature, and polymers decompose.
    • Perfect contact. Real joints between layers have a contact resistance. At bolted and pressed interfaces, this resistance can be the largest.
    • One dimension. Corners, bosses, fins and fasteners spread heat in two or three dimensions. Use a finite-element model for these.
    • Film coefficients are seldom known to better than a factor of two without test data. Add radiation where it is important.
    • No internal heat generation: no reaction and no electrical heating.

    On the drawing and in the specifications

    Section titled “On the drawing and in the specifications”
    • Give each temperature limit with its duration. The maximum temperature of a seal compound for minutes is different from its maximum for years.
    • Specify the conditioning fully: the temperature, the tolerance, the minimum soak time, and where to measure the temperature. Measure it on the grain or in a representative dummy, not on the case.
    • Put thermocouples where the peaks are (behind the liners, at the seals and at the bondlines), and continue to record after burnout. The important peak can be ten minutes later.
