Case & pressure vessel sizing

In one lineSize the wall so that it does not yield at proof and does not burst below the design burst pressure. Then check that it leaks before it breaks.
Before thisHow a solid rocket motor works

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

The case is usually the heaviest inert part of a solid motor. Each kilogram of case is a kilogram of propellant that the motor cannot carry. The propellant mass fraction (propellant mass divided by total motor mass) is the hardware parameter with the largest effect on performance. Thus case sizing is a balance. The case must be light enough to give performance, and strong enough to never fail, because there is no leak warning and there is an energetic payload inside.

The intuition: why cylinders split lengthwise

Section titled “The intuition: why cylinders split lengthwise”

Think of it like…

A sausage in a hot pan

Overcooked sausages and frozen water pipes always split along their length, never around their circumference. This is not a weakness in the skin. It is geometry. The force that tries to open a lengthwise seam increases with the diameter. The force that tries to pull the end off increases with the end area, and the full circumference resists it. The calculation shows that the lengthwise seam has exactly two times the stress.

For a thin-walled cylinder with an inner radius rrr and a wall ttt, at an internal pressure ppp:

σh=p rtσa=p r2t=σh2\sigma_h = \frac{p\,r}{t} \qquad\qquad \sigma_a = \frac{p\,r}{2t} = \frac{\sigma_h}{2}σh​=tpr​σa​=2tpr​=2σh​​

A sphere has no “long direction”. All cuts are the same as cut B. Thus its wall stress is p r/2tp\,r/2tpr/2t in all directions. This is why a hemispherical dome needs only half the wall of the cylinder that it closes, and why the sphere is the lightest possible pressure vessel. Domes are bubbles. Flat closures are plates in bending, and they are heavy.

The three pressures

Section titled “The three pressures”

A case is not designed to the operating pressure. It is checked against a set of pressures, which all refer to the maximum expected operating pressure (MEOP):

  • MEOP already includes the hot-day grain temperature, the manufacturing variation, the ignition spike and erosive burning. See why pressure is amplified.
  • Proof is applied to each case (or to a defined sample) to show no yield and no leak. It is also your best flaw screen. Any crack that is large enough to fail at proof fails on the test stand, not in service.
  • Design burst is the minimum ultimate capability that the design must have. Analysis shows it, and tests to failure on qualification articles also show it.

This gives two sizing requirements, and the thicker wall applies:

tproof:    σh(pproof)≤η σytburst:    σh(pburst)≤η σUTSt_{\text{proof}}:\;\; \sigma_h(p_{\text{proof}}) \le \eta\,\sigma_y \qquad\qquad t_{\text{burst}}:\;\; \sigma_h(p_{\text{burst}}) \le \eta\,\sigma_{\text{UTS}}tproof​:σh​(pproof​)≤ησy​tburst​:σh​(pburst​)≤ησUTS​

Here η\etaη is the weld (joint) efficiency. High-strength case steels have a yield strength near the UTS, so burst usually governs. For lower yield-to-tensile ratios, proof can govern.

When “thin” stops being thin

Section titled “When “thin” stops being thin”

The thin-wall formula assumes that the stress is uniform through the wall. It is not uniform. It is highest at the bore. The Lamé thick-cylinder solution gives the exact hoop stress at the inner surface:

σh,bore=p ro2+ri2ro2−ri2⟹t=ri(σ+pσ−p−1)\sigma_{h,\text{bore}} = p\,\frac{r_o^2 + r_i^2}{r_o^2 - r_i^2} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad t = r_i\left(\sqrt{\frac{\sigma + p}{\sigma - p}} - 1\right)σh,bore​=pro2​−ri2​ro2​+ri2​​⟹t=ri​(σ−pσ+p​​−1)

Below approximately t/r=0.1t/r = 0.1t/r=0.1, the two formulas agree within a few per cent. Motor cases are usually much thinner than that, but closures, bosses and high-pressure gas generators are not. The calculator below always uses Lamé, and it also shows the thin-wall value, so that you can see the difference.

Leak before break

Section titled “Leak before break”

A case can fail in two ways. In leak before break, a crack grows through the wall, releases gas, and somebody notices it. In break before leak, a crack gets to its critical size while it is still below the surface, and the case ruptures without warning. The simplest screen (Irwin) asks if a crack that has just gone through the wall would be stable. Such a crack is approximately as long as the wall is thick on each side, with a half-length c≈tc \approx tc≈t:

ccrit=1π(KICσh)2leak before break ifccrit≥tc_{\text{crit}} = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{K_{IC}}{\sigma_h}\right)^2 \qquad\text{leak before break if}\qquad c_{\text{crit}} \ge tccrit​=π1​(σh​KIC​​)2leak before break ifccrit​≥t

The full method is on Leak before break. It grows a surface flaw through the wall and checks the through crack that it becomes, with bulging and plastic collapse.

Think of it like…

A balloon versus a football

If you make a small hole in a balloon, it bursts. The material has a high stress and little toughness, so the hole runs. If you make a small hole in a football, it only becomes flat. The hole stays a hole. You want your case to be like the football. Higher-strength steels let you make the wall thinner, but their toughness usually decreases as their strength increases. This moves you towards the balloon.

This is only a screening check. Real fracture-control plans use solutions for surface cracks and embedded cracks, measured toughness at the correct orientation, temperature and thickness, and a crack-growth analysis over the pressure history of the motor.

    What the example shows

    The membrane sizing is easy, and the result is very thin. Relative to its size, this wall is thinner than an eggshell. The difficult part is step 6. At this strength level, the case does not leak before it breaks. Thus its safety depends on NDT that reliably finds small flaws and on the proof test, which screens all larger flaws. Load the calculator with these inputs, then try 15CDV6 or maraging 18Ni-250 to see how the trade changes.

    Where this tool stops

    Section titled “Where this tool stops”

    Membrane stress is the easy part

    The calculator sizes the cylinder away from its ends. Real cases fail at the discontinuities that it does not model.

    • Junctions between dome and cylinder. A cylinder and a dome at the same pressure try to grow by different amounts. When they are forced to match, local bending occurs, which can increase the stress to much more than the membrane values. A 2:1 ellipsoidal head also has a compressive hoop stress in its knuckle, which can buckle thin heads.
    • Closures, bosses and joints. Load paths concentrate at polar bosses, bolted or pinned closure joints, clevis joints and skirt attachments. These need their own analysis (usually FEA). See Load paths & joints.
    • External and flight loads. Axial thrust, bending from flight loads, and external pressure or handling loads add to the pressure stresses. Buckling, not burst, can govern thin cases without pressure.
    • Temperature. The allowables must be at the temperature of the case during and after the burn. This includes the heat that soaks back from the insulation after burnout.
    • Composite cases. Filament-wound cases are sized by netting analysis on fibre strength and wind angle, not by isotropic membrane theory. A separate page (planned) will cover them.

    Defining it on the drawing

    Section titled “Defining it on the drawing”
    • Give more than the alloy. Give the alloy, the condition or temper, the product form, the applicable specification and the melt practice. “D6AC bar per AMS 6431, VAR, heat treated to 1520 MPa min UTS, double temper” is a specification. “D6AC” is not.
    • Special processes get their own notes. Heat treatment (with pyrometry to AMS 2750), welding procedure, NDT method and acceptance class, coatings and cleanliness are each separate callouts with their own specification.
    • Fracture-critical parts have different rules. When a part is classified as fracture-critical, the drawing gives the flaw-size and NDE requirements, and the material comes from a controlled quality class.
    • The proof test is a drawing or acceptance requirement, with the pressure, hold time, medium, temperature and acceptance criteria (no leak, permanent set limits).
