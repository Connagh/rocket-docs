Rocket motor hardware

In one lineEach part must survive its loads with the minimum possible mass. Each kilogram of hardware is propellant that the vehicle cannot carry.
Before thisHow a solid motor works (what the parts are)
After thisCase sizing · Insulation · O-ring design

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

A solid motor has few parts: a case with two closures, insulation, the propellant grain, a nozzle, an igniter, and the joints and seals between them. When a motor fails, the cause is usually one of these hardware parts, or the interface between two of them. It is usually not the propellant chemistry. The Space Shuttle Challenger was lost in January 1986 because the seals of a booster field joint could not follow the joint as it opened under pressure (see O-ring design). Later that year, a Titan 34D was lost after insulation separated from the case of one of its solid boosters and let the flame get to the steel.

After the propellant has burned, all of these parts are dead weight. Hardware design must balance these two requirements: sufficient strength, temperature capability and margin to survive, and no more mass than necessary.

Component by component

Section titled “Component by component”

The cutaway on the primer page shows what each part is. This section gives the view of the engineer: what each part carries, how it fails, what it was made of before, what it is made of now, and what must go on its drawing.

Interfaces fail first

Look at the Fails by rows. Bosses, joints, bondlines and liner-to-housing gaps occur many times. Engineers analyse each part alone, but an interface is between two drawings and two analysts. Give each interface an owner, a load path and an inspection.

The number every part answers to: mass fraction

Section titled “The number every part answers to: mass fraction”

The propellant mass fraction of the motor is the fraction of its mass that is propellant:

ζ=mpmp+minert\zeta = \frac{m_p}{m_p + m_\text{inert}}ζ=mp​+minert​mp​​

The rocket equation tells what this gives. m0m_0m0​ is the mass at ignition and mfm_fmf​ is the mass at burnout:

Here Isp g0I_{sp}\,g_0Isp​g0​ is the effective exhaust velocity. For a solid motor, it is CF c∗C_F\,c^*CF​c∗ (see the primer). The inert mass is in the denominator, so it has two bad effects: the motor pushes it for the full burn, and it is still there at the end. Two results follow directly:

  • The cost of one inert kilogram. By differentiation: ∂ Δv∂minert=− Isp g0 mpm0 mf\frac{\partial\,\Delta v}{\partial m_\text{inert}} = -\,I_{sp}\,g_0\,\frac{m_p}{m_0\,m_f}∂minert​∂Δv​=−Isp​g0​m0​mf​mp​​
  • The exchange ratio. To keep the same Δv\Delta vΔv when the inert mass increases by δ\deltaδ, the mass ratio m0/mfm_0/m_fm0​/mf​ must stay the same. Thus the propellant must increase by (m0/mf−1) δ(m_0/m_f - 1)\,\delta(m0​/mf​−1)δ. With a mass ratio of 2.5, each additional kilogram of hardware needs 1.5 kg more propellant. This needs a longer case, more insulation and a larger nozzle. This chain is why it is very difficult to correct an overweight motor late in a design.

Think of it like…

Packing for a long hike

Each gram of tent, stove and pack frame is a gram of food that you cannot carry. You also carry the tent all the way to the end, long after you have eaten the food. Ultralight hikers do not eat less. They weigh their equipment. A motor designer does the same with the case, the insulation and the nozzle.

Large motors with composite cases get mass fractions of more than 0.9. Small motors have much lower values, because the igniter, the nozzle throat, the bosses and the seals do not become smaller in proportion to the propellant.

A design sequence that works

First set the MEOP and the proof and burst factors. Then select the class of case material, size the wall and add the insulation. Only then check the remaining mass fraction. If you select the material last, after the geometry is frozen, the motor can be overweight with no remaining options.

    Designing the case to fail gently

    Section titled “Designing the case to fail gently”

    If a motor is in a fuel fire, or if a fragment hits it, it can ignite whatever the designer does. The hardware decides what occurs next. If the case continues to hold pressure, the burning propellant pressurises it until it bursts. This is an explosion. If the case opens early and stays open, the propellant burns at low pressure. This is a fire. From the hardware side, insensitive-munitions (IM) design makes sure of the second result.

    A design is assessed against these threats: fast heating (a fuel fire around the item), slow heating (a slow increase, for example from a fire in an adjacent store), bullet impact, fragment impact, sympathetic reaction (a reaction of an adjacent munition) and shaped-charge jet. The NATO policy is STANAG 4439. AOP-39 gives the assessment method and the necessary response for each threat. As with all factors on this site, get these requirements from the current editions, not from a page such as this one.

    What the hardware designer controls:

    • Composite cases usually vent themselves in a fast fire. The resin becomes soft and the case loses strength early.
    • Metal cases need a special feature. The feature makes the case or a closure weaker, or releases it, at a temperature below the temperature where the propellant reacts violently.
    • Slow heating is the difficult case. Fast heating heats the case before the propellant. Slow heating heats the full item together. Thus the propellant can get to its reaction temperature while the case is still cool, strong and intact. Most venting features exist because of slow heating.
    • A venting feature is structure with two functions. It must hold MEOP with margin at all storage temperatures and for the full service life, and it must still open when necessary. It needs the same proof, ageing and qualification evidence as the remainder of the case.

    Note

    IM qualification is a specialist activity and a safety-authority activity. This section gives the view of the hardware designer. The propellant selection and the reaction behaviour are the responsibility of the energetics specialists.

    Where this stops

    Section titled “Where this stops”
    • The atlas is descriptive. Each part is designed to the requirements of your programme. Several parts have their own pages on this site: case, insulation, seals and proof test.
    • The Δv\Delta vΔv is ideal: in vacuum, with no gravity or drag losses, and one stage. It is correct for the comparison of designs, not for the prediction of a trajectory.
    • The igniter charge and the propellant are energetics, which are outside the scope of this site. The site covers only the hardware around them.

    Defining motor hardware on the drawing

    Section titled “Defining motor hardware on the drawing”
    • Give more than the alloy. Give the alloy, condition, product form, applicable specification and melt practice. See the specification builder. “D6AC bar per AMS 6431, VAR, heat treated to 1520 MPa min UTS, double temper” is a specification. “D6AC” is not.
    • Special processes get their own callouts. Heat treatment (with pyrometry to AMS 2750), welding procedure, NDT method and acceptance class, coatings and cleanliness each need a note and a specification. If you cannot verify a property by a measurement of the finished part, it needs an approved process and a certificate.
    • Fracture-critical parts have different rules. When a part is classified as fracture-critical, its drawing gives the flaw-size and NDE requirements, and its material comes from a controlled quality class. Agree the classification before you finalise the tolerances, because it controls surface finish, radii and inspection access.
    • Traceability is a drawing requirement. Give the batch or heat marking, its location and its method. Stamping is a stress raiser and is not permitted on many fracture-critical surfaces. Etching usually is permitted.
    • Dissimilar metals. A steel case with an aluminium closure, or carbon fibre against any metal, makes a galvanic cell. Specify isolation (sealant, primer, isolating film) and an electrical bonding path on purpose. These two requirements are in opposite directions. See Galvanic corrosion.
    • Differential expansion. A carbon throat insert in a metal housing has large temperature changes across materials with very different expansion. If a fit depends on temperature, give the assembly temperature on the drawing.
    • Seals in hot environments. Confirm the seal compound against the real fluids and temperatures. Check if your programme requires approved seal materials. Many programmes do.
    • Bondlines are structure. Bonds between insulation and case, and liner bonds, carry load and are the usual failure path. Define the surface preparation, adhesive, bondline thickness, cure and the NDT that verifies the bond. A bond without an inspection requirement is a joint that nobody has verified.
