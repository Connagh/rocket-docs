Hand calcs

In one lineA hand calc is a model that is small enough to check in your head. It sizes the part before the finite-element model, finds the failure mode that governs, and tells you if the model is correct.
Before thisAllowables · Margin policy
Used byLoad paths & joints · Shell buckling

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

A finite-element model gives a result for all the data that you put into it, also incorrect loads, incorrect units and incorrect boundary conditions. A hand calc tells you what result to expect. You use it three times:

  • Before the model, to size the part. The thickness, section and pin size come from closed-form solutions. The model then refines a design that is already almost correct.
  • To find the failure mode that governs. Five quick checks often show that four failure modes are not important and one is. That one tells you where the model needs detail.
  • After the model, to check it. Reactions, far-field stresses and deflections must agree with the hand calc where its idealisation is valid. If they do not agree, there is an error. It costs less to find it now.

Reviewers also expect hand calcs. It is difficult to check a margin that comes only from a contour plot. A different engineer can follow a hand calc line by line.

How to make one

Section titled “How to make one”
  1. Draw the free body. Show all loads and all reactions, and check that they balance.
  2. Decide what the part is: a beam, a plate, a shell, a lug or a column. Also decide which region of the part the idealisation describes. Most real parts are several of these together.
  3. Put limits on the boundary conditions. A bolted edge is not simply supported and not clamped, so calculate the two cases. If the design passes the two cases, the edge condition is not important. If it does not, the edge needs a better model.
  4. Calculate in one consistent set of units (N, mm and MPa on this site). Compare with the allowables and the factors of your programme (Margin policy).
  5. Check the result. Check the order of magnitude, the limiting cases, and the direction of change when you change an input.
  6. Write the assumptions. Each one is a requirement on the drawing or a question for the detailed model.

Think of it like…

Checking the bill

You do not calculate a restaurant bill again to the penny. You round the values and add them in your head. If the total is two times your estimate, you look more carefully. A hand calc is that estimate for a finite-element model. It is approximate and quick, and it is very good at finding a result that is ten times incorrect.

  • Compression is different. Under external pressure or axial load, a thin shell buckles long before it yields. See Shell buckling.

Hand calcs and the finite-element model

Section titled “Hand calcs and the finite-element model”
  • Before meshing, the hand calc tells where detail is necessary: several elements across each edge zone (π/β\pi/\betaπ/β), and several through the thickness where a solid part bends.
  • Check the material cards first. Check the units, constants and tables before all other items. See FEA material cards.
  • Then check the reactions. The reactions of the model must be equal to the applied loads. If they are not, you cannot trust any other result from the model.
  • Then check the far field. Away from details, the membrane stresses and deflections of the model must agree with the hand calc. Deflections must be between the simply supported limit and the clamped limit.
  • Then check the peaks. They must be where the hand calc puts them (at the edge, the hole or the root), and of the correct order. A peak at one node that is much higher is frequently a singularity at a sharp corner or a point load, not a stress.
  • If they do not agree where the hand calc must be good, find the cause before you trust either result. The cause can be the model, its loads or units, or the idealisation of the hand calc. The full set of checks is on FEA & test.

    Worked example: hand checks for the 300 mm motor’s test campaign

    Section titled “Worked example: hand checks for the 300 mm motor’s test campaign”

    These are five quick checks on the motor and on the hardware that tests it. The yield factor of 1.10 and the ultimate factor of 1.25 are for this example only. Push Load worked example and use the tabs.

    1. The case as a beam. During transport, the loaded motor (137.81 kg) sits in two cradles 1000 mm apart. Use 3 g vertical for this example: 4.05 kN along the span. As a simply supported ⌀302.75 × 1.377 mm tube, M=WL/8=0.51M = WL/8 = 0.51M=WL/8=0.51 kN·m. Thus the bending stress is 5.2 MPa, the shear stress is 3.1 MPa and the deflection is 0.017 mm. The margins are more than +200, so the case as a beam is not important. At 3.3 diameters between supports it is a short, deep beam, so the numbers are only approximate. But they are two hundred times too small to be important. The important load is the concentrated load at each cradle on a 1.4 mm wall. This is a shell problem, thus the cradles must be wide or have rings.
    2. The case wall. At MEOP (10 MPa), the hoop stress is 10×150.69/1.377=109410 \times 150.69 / 1.377 = 109410×150.69/1.377=1094 MPa and the axial stress is 547 MPa. These are the case sizing numbers. The margins are +0.20 on yield and +0.11 on ultimate, before any weld efficiency. The edge zone is π/β=35\pi/\beta = 35π/β=35 mm. If the aft flange held the wall rigidly, the inner surface would have 547+1.55×1094=2241547 + 1.55 \times 1094 = 2241547+1.55×1094=2241 MPa, which is more than the ultimate strength. The wall must become thicker, with a land of approximately 2.05×1.377=2.82.05 \times 1.377 = 2.82.05×1.377=2.8 mm where it meets the flange. The hand calc sets this before there is a model.
    3. The proof-test blanking plate. For the proof test at 11 MPa, a flat steel plate replaces the aft closure, on an illustrative 285 mm bolt circle. Assume that it is simply supported at the bolt circle and loaded over its full area. This is conservative, because the pressure stops at the 260 mm seal. With a quenched and tempered steel at Fty=655F_{ty} = 655Fty​=655 and Ftu=860F_{tu} = 860Ftu​=860 MPa (typical values), it needs 21.5 mm. A 25 mm plate has 441 MPa at the centre (MS +0.35 yield, +0.56 ultimate) and deflects 1.0 mm. This is 0.04 of its thickness, so the formula is valid. Its edge turns 0.61°, and a face seal must follow this (Sealing strategy). A hemispherical head of the same radius would need only 1.3 mm.
    4. The thrust rod of the test stand. The 29.5 kN thrust of the motor at MEOP (Load paths) goes to the load cell through a 25 × 2.5 mm S355 tube, 600 mm from pin to pin. r=8.0r = 8.0r=8.0 mm, so KL/r=75KL/r = 75KL/r=75, which is below the transition at 107. Johnson gives 47.3 kN, MS +0.28 at 1.25 × the thrust. Euler would give 63.6 kN and +0.73, which is 35% unconservative. With 0.6 mm (L/1000) of crookedness, the secant formula gives 233 MPa at 1.10 × the thrust: +0.53 to first yield.
    5. The clevis ends of the rod. Each end is a 10 mm S355 lug, 36 mm wide, with a 12.2 mm hole 18 mm from the end. It is on a 12 mm alloy-steel pin between two 6 mm clevis plates, with 0.5 mm gaps. Typical allowables: lug Ftu=470F_{tu} = 470Ftu​=470, Fsu=282F_{su} = 282Fsu​=282, Fbru=705F_{bru} = 705Fbru​=705 MPa; pin Ftu=1000F_{tu} = 1000Ftu​=1000, Fsu=600F_{su} = 600Fsu​=600 MPa. At 1.25 × 29.5 = 36.8 kN, the margins are: net tension +2.04, shear-out +0.82, bearing +1.30, pin shear +2.69, and pin bending +0.54. Pin bending governs, as it usually does. If the gaps increase to 1 mm, it decreases to +0.42.

    These are five checks on one page of arithmetic. Each check clears a part, or tells the detailed model exactly where to look: the cradles, the flange land, and the seal at the edge of the plate.

    Where this stops

    Section titled “Where this stops”
    • Idealisations. Real parts are partly beam, partly plate and partly shell. The hand calc describes the regions where one idealisation is valid, and puts limits on the other regions.
    • Linear elastic, small deflection. No plasticity, no large-deflection membrane action in plates, and no contact.
    • Static loads only. Dynamic loads, fatigue and fracture need their own checks. See Fatigue and Random vibration.
    • Lugs: axial load and first-check allowables only. Columns: no local wall buckling, and no lateral–torsional buckling of open sections.
    • Rectangular plates use coefficients for ν = 0.3, and uniform pressure with all four edges the same.

    On the drawing

    Section titled “On the drawing”

    The assumptions of a hand calc become requirements:

    • The dimensions that it sized, as minimums where they are important: the plate thickness, the flange land and its blend.
    • The material and condition of the allowables: the specification, the heat treatment and the assumed properties.
    • Straightness of struts if the column check assumed it, and pin fit and clevis gaps if the pin bending check used them.
    • Edge distances of lugs and holes.
    • The supports that the beam check found necessary: cradle width, saddle or ring.
