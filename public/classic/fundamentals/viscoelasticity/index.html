Viscoelasticity

In one lineIn an elastic material, stress depends on strain. In a viscoelastic material, it depends on the full history of strain: how fast the strain was applied and how long you waited.
Used byO-ring design · Propellant properties · Propellant grain
RelatedStorage & ageing (slow chemistry, not slow mechanics)

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

All the rubber seals, propellant grains, adhesive bondlines, insulation layers and composite matrices in a motor are viscoelastic. If you treat them as springs, you will:

  • calculate a sealing force that is too high after storage, and a seal that follows a joint too fast when cold;
  • get grain stresses incorrect by a factor of ten, because the load can be a slow cool-down or a fast ignition;
  • not see the slow loss of bolt preload through a composite flange, or the creep of a bonded joint under a sustained load.

All the complications on this page come from one property: the material remembers.

Two experiments, mirror images

Section titled “Two experiments, mirror images”
  • Relaxation. Keep a strain constant and monitor the stress decrease. The relaxation modulus is E(t)=σ(t)/ε0E(t) = \sigma(t)/\varepsilon_0E(t)=σ(t)/ε0​.
  • Creep. Keep a stress constant and monitor the strain increase. The creep compliance is J(t)=ε(t)/σ0J(t) = \varepsilon(t)/\sigma_0J(t)=ε(t)/σ0​.

They describe the same material, but they are not reciprocals: J(t) E(t)≤1J(t)\,E(t) \le 1J(t)E(t)≤1, and creep occurs later in time than relaxation. The exact relation is ∫0tE(t−s) J(s) ds=t\int_0^t E(t-s)\,J(s)\,ds = t∫0t​E(t−s)J(s)ds=t. The calculator uses it to change one into the other.

Think of it like…

Memory foam

If you push on a memory-foam pillow, it moves slowly. If you remove your hand, the dent fills slowly. If you put the pillow in a cold location, it becomes hard and slow, and the dent takes much longer to recover. Seals, propellant and adhesives do the same, on time scales from microseconds to years.

The Prony series

Section titled “The Prony series”

Real polymers relax over many decades of time. Thus the useful model is the generalised Maxwell model: a long-term spring in parallel with many spring–dashpot arms, one for each decade. Its relaxation modulus is a Prony series:

E(t)=E∞+∑iEi e−t/τi,E0=E∞+∑iEiE(t) = E_\infty + \sum_{i} E_i\, e^{-t/\tau_i}, \qquad E_0 = E_\infty + \sum_i E_iE(t)=E∞​+i∑​Ei​e−t/τi​,E0​=E∞​+i∑​Ei​

The relaxation times τi\tau_iτi​ are usually fixed at one for each decade over the important range. The EiE_iEi​ are fitted to test data by least squares, and they must stay positive. The same series gives the response to a sinusoidal load:

Into a solver

Section titled “Into a solver”

Finite-element codes use the series in normalised form, gi=Ei/E0g_i = E_i/E_0gi​=Ei​/E0​, with the instantaneous value as the elastic modulus. Two errors are frequent:

  • The sum of the gig_igi​ must be less than 1. The remainder, 1−∑gi=E∞/E01 - \sum g_i = E_\infty/E_01−∑gi​=E∞​/E0​, is the long-term fraction. A sum of 1 means that the material relaxes fully, and the solver rejects it.
  • The elastic card must agree. Give the instantaneous modulus, not a datasheet “Young’s modulus” measured at an unknown rate. Alternatively, declare the long-term basis and give E∞E_\inftyE∞​. If you mix the two, all the stresses in the model are incorrect by a factor.

A third error is less easy to see: put the arms sufficiently close together. One arm for each decade fits E(t)E(t)E(t) well, but it causes visible ripples in tan⁡δ\tan\deltatanδ, because each arm adds its own loss peak. Two arms for each decade give smooth dynamic properties. The presets in the calculator use two.

Solvers usually apply the gig_igi​ to the shear modulus. For an almost incompressible material (E≈3GE \approx 3GE≈3G), the normalised values are the same. If the bulk modulus also relaxes, it needs its own terms.

Time–temperature superposition

Section titled “Time–temperature superposition”

Cold has the same effect as slow. A lower temperature makes all relaxation processes slower by the same factor aTa_TaT​. Thus a test at low temperature over a few seconds shows what occurs at room temperature over days. If you move short tests at several temperatures along the log-time axis until they overlap, you get a master curve. It covers many more decades than one test.

Above the glass transition, the shift factor follows the WLF equation:

log⁡10aT=−C1 (T−Tr)C2+T−Tr\log_{10} a_T = \frac{-C_1\,(T - T_r)}{C_2 + T - T_r}log10​aT​=C2​+T−Tr​−C1​(T−Tr​)​

With the reference at the glass transition, the general constants are C1=17.44C_1 = 17.44C1​=17.44 and C2=51.6C_2 = 51.6C2​=51.6 K. For a different reference temperature TrT_rTr​, they become C1=17.44×51.6/(51.6+Tr−Tg)C_1 = 17.44 \times 51.6 / (51.6 + T_r - T_g)C1​=17.44×51.6/(51.6+Tr​−Tg​) and C2=51.6+Tr−TgC_2 = 51.6 + T_r - T_gC2​=51.6+Tr​−Tg​. At Tr=Tg+50T_r = T_g + 50Tr​=Tg​+50 K, this gives 8.86 and 101.6 K, which are the values that the propellant page uses. WLF is valid from approximately TgT_gTg​ to Tg+100T_g + 100Tg​+100 K. Below TgT_gTg​, glassy polymers such as composite matrices usually follow an Arrhenius-type shift.

Dynamic mechanical analysis

Section titled “Dynamic mechanical analysis”

A DMA test applies a sinusoidal load to a sample while the temperature changes. As the material goes through its glass transition, E′E'E′ decreases by two or three orders of magnitude and tan⁡δ\tan\deltatanδ has a peak. The peak is above the TgT_gTg​ from a slow thermal test, and it moves up with the test frequency. Thus always give the frequency with a DMA TgT_gTg​.

    What it means for seals, propellant and composites
    Section titled “What it means for seals, propellant and composites”
    

    Seals
    Section titled “Seals”
    

      

    • The sealing force relaxes in storage, and real compounds also take a chemical set as they age. Design the minimum
squeeze for the relaxed, aged condition. See Storage & ageing.
      • 

    • The low-temperature recovery is measured by the temperature-retraction (TR) test. Specify it at the lowest
temperature at which the seal must follow movement, not only at the lowest temperature that it must survive.
      • 

    • Below the glass transition, a seal is a hard plastic, and it cannot seal a part that moves.
      • 

    

    Propellant
    Section titled “Propellant”
    

      

    • During slow cool-downs, the grain has a relaxed modulus. At ignition, it has a modulus nearer to the glassy
value, often ten times stiffer. This is why the grain page treats the two load cases
separately.
      • 

    • The strain capability also depends on rate and temperature. Grain analyses use measured Prony series and shift
factors, with cumulative damage over the service life.
      • 

    

    Composites and adhesives
    Section titled “Composites and adhesives”
    

      

    • Fibre-dominated properties (along the carbon fibres) almost do not creep. Matrix-dominated properties (shear,
transverse and through-thickness) do creep, and more so as the temperature approaches the glass transition of the
matrix. Moisture decreases that glass transition. Thus hot and wet is the critical condition.
      • 

    • Bolted composite joints lose preload as the laminate creeps through its thickness. The usual solutions are metal
inserts or bushes, checks of the preload loss over time, and a retorque policy where permitted.
      • 

    • Stress rupture. Aramid and glass fibres can fail after long periods under a constant load. Carbon fibre is much
less susceptible. This is the basis of the stress-rupture requirements for composite-overwrapped pressure vessels.
      • 

    • Bondlines creep under sustained shear, especially when hot. Sustained-load allowables are much lower than the
short-term strength.
      • 

    • Composite design practice keeps the maximum operating temperature a margin below the wet glass transition
temperature of the matrix.
      • 

    

    Where this stops
    Section titled “Where this stops”
    

      

    • Linear viscoelasticity: small strains and no damage. Filled rubbers become softer after the first load (the
Mullins effect), and propellants dewet. These two effects are non-linear.
      • 

    • Thermorheologically simple: one shift factor for all arms. Multi-phase materials can be different.
      • 

    • WLF range: approximately TgT_gTg​ to Tg+100T_g + 100Tg​+100 K.
      • 

    • Pressure also shifts the response, in the opposite direction to temperature. Ageing changes the Prony series.
      • 

    • Bulk behaviour is elastic here. Only the deviatoric response relaxes.
      • 

    

    On the drawing and in the specifications
    Section titled “On the drawing and in the specifications”
    

      

    • Seal compounds: specify the low-temperature retraction and the compression set at a stated temperature and time,
not only the hardness.
      • 

    • Each viscoelastic property on a drawing or in a specification needs its temperature and its rate or time:
“modulus at 20 °C, 1 minute”, not only “modulus”.
      • 

    • Composites: give the maximum operating temperature, with its basis in the wet glass transition. Use
sustained-load allowables for joints that have a permanent load.
      • 

    • Analyses: record the source of the Prony data, its reference temperature and its shift law, so that somebody can
check the material model.
      •
