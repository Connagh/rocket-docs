O-ring design

In one lineCheck the squeeze at the worst tolerance condition and the coldest temperature, with the joint gap at its widest under pressure. Do not check it at nominal.
Before thisCase sizing (the joint where the seal is installed)

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

O-rings are the cheapest parts on a motor, and some of the most safety-critical. Case joints, the nozzle-to-case joint, igniter bosses and closures all use them to hold hot gas at high pressure. If gas gets past a seal, the combustion gas cuts through metal in seconds. The worst known example is the loss of the Space Shuttle Challenger in 1986. The cause was an O-ring in a booster field joint that could not seal on a cold morning.

How an O-ring seals

Section titled “How an O-ring seals”

Think of it like…

A plug-type aircraft door

The passenger door of an airliner is slightly larger than its frame, and it fits from the inside. Thus the cabin pressure pushes it harder into the frame as the aircraft climbs. An O-ring works in the same way. The squeeze from assembly gives it an initial seal. The system pressure then pushes it harder into the low-pressure corner of the groove. The seal becomes tighter as the pressure increases, until the pressure is sufficient to push rubber through the clearance gap.

In bulk, rubber is similar to a very stiff liquid. In shape, it is similar to a very soft solid. It is easy to change its shape, but its volume almost does not change.

Think of it like…

A water balloon in a box

Push down on a water balloon in a box, and it bulges to the sides. Its volume cannot change, only its shape. If the box is too small, the balloon has no space to go, and the pressure inside becomes very high. This is why the groove volume (gland fill) is as important as the depth. The ring must have space to change shape, and space to grow when it is hot or when it swells in a fluid.

Nominal is not enough

Section titled “Nominal is not enough”

All of these numbers change across the tolerance band, and the extreme values cause the failures. A 5.33 mm section has a tolerance of approximately ±0.13 mm. Alone, this changes the squeeze by approximately ±2.5 percentage points. Add the tolerances of the groove, the bore and the piston, and let the parts sit off-centre (the gland is then shallow on one side and deep on the other). A nominal squeeze of 19% can then be anywhere from approximately 13% to 25%.

Then add the changes that occur after assembly:

  • Temperature. Elastomers expand approximately ten times more than steel (approximately 1.6×10−41.6\times10^{-4}1.6×10−4/K). Cold rings shrink and lose squeeze. Hot rings grow and fill the groove.
  • Fluid swell. A volume increase of a few per cent in service is normal, and the groove must have space for it.
  • Compression set. Over time and at temperature, the ring takes a permanent set, and it pushes back with less force. Include this in the minimum squeeze.
  • Joint opening. Under pressure, case joints turn, bores expand and flanges lift. Thus the gap that the ring must fill becomes larger exactly when the pressure arrives. The bolt preload keeps a flanged gland closed. See Bolted joints. Sealing strategy tells where to put the seal so that the gap almost does not move, and how to back it up and do a leak check on it.

The Challenger lesson: the seal must follow the joint

Section titled “The Challenger lesson: the seal must follow the joint”

A seal is a spring, and cold springs are slow

In the field joint of a Shuttle booster, the ignition pressure made the joint turn a little and open the gap that the O-rings sealed. A warm O-ring expands sufficiently fast to follow. On the cold morning of 28 January 1986 (approximately 2 °C ambient, and the joint was colder), the fluoroelastomer rings recovered too slowly. Hot gas got past before they sealed, and the joint failed approximately one minute into the flight.

During the Rogers Commission hearings, Richard Feynman showed the effect with a sample of the O-ring rubber clamped in iced water. When he released the clamp, the rubber stayed flat and did not spring back. Two design rules come directly from this:

  1. Size the minimum squeeze with the joint open under pressure, at the coldest temperature. The “joint opening” input of the calculator does this.
  2. Do not use the resilience of rubber to follow motion near its low-temperature limit. Specify the compound by its low-temperature recovery (TR-10 and resilience tests), heat the joint, or design the joint so that it does not open. The redesigned booster joint added a capture feature to limit the rotation, a third O-ring and joint heaters. The viscoelasticity page shows why: when cold, all the recovery processes in the rubber are thousands of times slower.

Two more lessons about joints:

  • Redundancy is real only if the two seals work. A primary and a secondary O-ring with a leak-check port between them is standard. Show that the secondary can still seal when the joint is open and the primary has already let gas past.
  • A leak check pushes the primary O-ring in the incorrect direction. Pressure between two rings pushes the primary towards the motor side of its groove. Thus at ignition, it must first move across the groove before it can seal. The leak-check pressure and procedure are part of the seal design.

Worked example: a nozzle-to-case radial seal

Section titled “Worked example: a nozzle-to-case radial seal”

These are the defaults of the calculator: a static radial seal in a piston-type closure, a 150 mm bore at 10 MPa, a steel housing, and a service range of −40 to +60 °C. The ring has a 5.33 mm section and a 139.29 mm stock ID. The groove root is Ø141.45–141.55, the width is 7.0–7.2 mm, the bore is Ø150.000–150.063, and the plug is Ø149.860–149.900. The parts can sit off-centre.

  1. Nominal. The gland depth is (150.03−141.50)/2=4.27(150.03 - 141.50)/2 = 4.27(150.03−141.50)/2=4.27 mm. The stretch onto the root is (141.5−139.29)/139.29=1.6%(141.5 - 139.29)/139.29 = 1.6\%(141.5−139.29)/139.29=1.6%, which makes the 5.33 section thinner, at 5.29 mm. The squeeze is (5.29−4.27)/5.29=19.3%(5.29 - 4.27)/5.29 = 19.3\%(5.29−4.27)/5.29=19.3%, and the fill is 73%. These values are well in the band.
  2. Worst conditions. Fully off-centre, the gland is from 4.16 to 4.41 mm. The maximum squeeze (largest ring, hot, with 5% swell) is 25%. The minimum squeeze (smallest ring, most stretch, −40 °C) is 13.5%, and 10.8% after 20% compression set. This is just above the 10% screening minimum.
  3. Extrusion. The clearance can be up to 0.203 mm. At 10 MPa, a 70 Shore ring needs approximately 0.08 mm, and even a 90 Shore ring needs approximately 0.15 mm. A back-up ring is necessary, or a tighter fit and a harder compound.
  4. Compound. Standard FKM usually has a limit of approximately −20 °C. At −40 °C, it does not pass the temperature check. Use a low-temperature fluoroelastomer grade or a different compound, and confirm the TR-10.
  5. Joint opening. Enter 0.25 mm of radial opening under pressure. The minimum squeeze decreases to 6.9%, and the cold-resilience check fails. This is the Challenger situation on a small scale. Make the joint stiffer. Do not change the seal.

Choosing the compound

Section titled “Choosing the compound”

Select the compound for the coldest, the hottest and the chemically worst conditions, in that sequence. The table in the calculator checks each compound against your temperature range and medium. These errors are frequent, also for experienced engineers:

  • EPDM with petroleum grease. EPDM is very good in water, steam and phosphate-ester fluids. But it swells and fails in mineral oil, which includes the assembly lubricant. Specify the lubricant on the drawing, adjacent to the compound.
  • Standard FKM when cold. It is very good when hot and in fuels, but standard grades become stiff at approximately −20 °C. Low-temperature grades are available. Specify one by its TR-10.
  • Silicone in dynamic duty. It has a wide temperature range, but poor tear strength and abrasion strength. Use it for static seals only.
  • Oxygen service. Usual elastomers can ignite in oxygen. LOX seals and high-pressure oxygen seals use specially tested materials and controlled cleanliness.
  • The brittle point is not the limit. The seal fails at a temperature much higher than the brittle point. Use TR-10 or resilience data.

Groove details that decide the outcome

Section titled “Groove details that decide the outcome”
  • Surface finish. Static sealing faces are usually approximately Ra 0.8 µm (finer for gases and vacuum), dynamic counterfaces Ra 0.2–0.4 µm, and groove walls approximately Ra 1.6–3.2 µm. The machining lay must not cross the seal. See Surface finish.
  • Lead-in chamfers of 15–20°, deburred and blended, on all edges that the ring moves over. Assembly cuts more O-rings than service failures, and you cannot see a cut ring after it is installed.
  • Groove corner radii sufficiently small for the ring to sit correctly, but never sharp.
  • Back-up rings on the low-pressure side, or on the two sides if the pressure can reverse. Add their width to the groove.
  • Runout of the groove root to its pilot. The calculator assumes that the groove is coaxial with the part that centres it. With a general tolerance, it is possibly not coaxial, and each 0.1 mm of runout decreases the squeeze by approximately one point. See GD&T, and Tolerance stack for the tolerances that decide the squeeze.

Where this stops

Section titled “Where this stops”

Screening, not a seal qualification

The squeeze bands, the fill limit and the extrusion table are typical guidance, not ISO 3601-2 dimensions. Real joint seals are qualified by test at temperature, with the joint deflecting as it does in service, and with a direct measurement of the low-temperature recovery of the compound.

References and further reading

Section titled “References and further reading”

  • SAE AS568 (the inch-series O-ring sizes that ISO 3601-1 class A uses).
  • Parker Hannifin, O-Ring Handbook (ORD 5700) (groove design, extrusion limits and compound data).
