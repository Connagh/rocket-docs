Material designations

In one lineYou can translate freely between naming systems. But substitute between specifications only with engineering approval.
Leads toSpecifying materials

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

An old drawing specifies HE30, the supplier gives EN AW-6082, the US partner says 6061, and the certificate has a UNS number. These materials are related, but they are not identical. If you can read each system, you can follow a material across drawings, suppliers and countries. If you know where they are different, you can prevent a “nearest equivalent” from becoming a non-conformance.

Think of it like…

A person's names on different documents

Your passport, your driving licence and your employee badge all identify you. But each has different details, and each is accepted for different purposes. The designations of a material are the same. EN, UNS and AMS all identify almost the same alloy, but the specification (the document) sets the limits, the tests and the acceptance.

How the numbering systems read

Section titled “How the numbering systems read”

EN aluminium (EN 573). EN AW-6082: AW means aluminium wrought (AC means cast). The four digits follow the international series (1xxx pure, 2xxx Cu, 5xxx Mg, 6xxx Mg–Si, 7xxx Zn). The temper follows the designation (EN AW-6082-T6), and EN 515 defines it. The composition is in EN 573-3. The properties come from the product standard: EN 485 for sheet and plate, EN 755 for extrusions, and EN 754 for drawn bar and tube.

EN steels (EN 10027). There are two parallel systems. The name gives the composition: 34CrNiMo6 has approximately 0.34% carbon with chromium, nickel and molybdenum. The last number is the content of the main alloying element multiplied by a factor. The number (1.6582) is a catalogue entry: 1.xxxx for steels.

EN stainless (EN 10088). X2CrNiMo17-12-2: X identifies a high-alloy steel. Then come carbon ×100, the elements and their percentages. This one is 316L, number 1.4404. Part 2 covers sheet and plate, and part 3 covers bar, rod and sections.

UNS. One letter and five digits, for all metals: A aluminium, C copper, G carbon and alloy steels, K miscellaneous steels (D6AC is K24728), N nickel, R reactive and refractory metals including titanium, and S stainless. It is often the clearest way to identify exactly which grade a person means.

Aerospace specifications (AMS, BS L-series, AIR). These are specifications, not only names. They set the chemistry, product form, condition, testing and acceptance. Two L-series specifications can have the same alloy and temper with different tolerances. Stock certified to one does not meet a drawing that specifies the other.

Nearest equivalents, not interchangeable grades. Composition limits, melt practice, testing and acceptance differ between systems.

Equivalent is not identical

Cross-reference tables give the nearest grades. Composition ranges, permitted impurities, melt practice, test requirements and acceptance criteria are different between systems. Never substitute across a row on a flight part or a safety-significant part without engineering approval and a materials review.

Which one goes on the drawing?

In the UK, with BS 8888, the usual callout is the BS EN designation plus the applicable product standard, for example EN AW-6082-T6 TO BS EN 755-2. Add the UNS or AMS reference only where the supply chain really needs it. Old BS names (HE30, 817M40, 304S11) are for reading old drawings, not for writing new drawings.
