Specifying materials

In one lineA complete callout gives the alloy and its condition, the product-form standard, and the finish with its own specification.
Before thisSelecting materials · Designations

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

The material block is the part of a drawing that is easiest to write badly, and errors in it are the most expensive. “6082-T6” or “D6AC” tells a supplier the chemistry and nothing about what you will get: which product form, which tolerance class, which melt practice, which strength and which certificate. You get the strength of your design only if the drawing asks for it in a form that an inspector can check.

The three-part rule

Section titled “The three-part rule”

Think of it like…

Ordering a coffee

If you ask for “a coffee”, you get a coffee. If you ask for “a large flat white with oat milk and an extra shot”, you get what you want. If it is not correct, you can say exactly what is incorrect. A material callout works in the same way. The alloy is “coffee”. The form, the standard, the condition and the finish are the items that make the order possible to check.

Each metallic callout needs three items. Most drawings give only one or two:

  1. The alloy and its condition. Give the temper or heat treatment in a form that can be tested (for example a minimum UTS), with the process specification that gives it.
  2. The product form and its standard. Properties and tolerances are different for bar, plate, extrusion and forging. Thus the standard for that form applies. Many forms have a separate tolerance standard.
  3. The finish, with its own specification: anodise, plating, passivation, primer or peening. Never “to suit”.

Product form and grain direction

Section titled “Product form and grain direction”

Rolled and extruded metal is anisotropic. In thick plate, the short-transverse direction (through the thickness) usually has the lowest elongation and fracture toughness, and the highest sensitivity to stress-corrosion cracking. This is the usual failure path for high-strength aluminium and steel fittings loaded through their thickness. There are two results for the drawing:

  • Select the form for the load path. A closure, a boss or a Y-ring that carries pressure load around a contour is usually forged, so that the grain follows the shape. It is not machined from bar or plate.
  • Give the grain direction when it is important. A grain-direction note on the drawing and on the forging drawing removes the uncertainty.

Practical tips
    

    Worked example: a D6AC motor case component
    Section titled “Worked example: a D6AC motor case component”
    

    The default of the builder is the D6AC sheet, one of the two case-steel sheets added in v2.
    

      

    1. Alloy and form. Bar to AMS 6431, the material specification for D6AC bar, forgings and tubing. It also
specifies vacuum-arc remelting (VAR). Sheet and plate for a rolled-and-welded cylinder are to AMS 6439.
      2. 

    2. Condition. The case strength (a minimum UTS of approximately 1520 MPa in the v1 example) comes from hardening
and double tempering. Specify it with the heat-treatment specification (AMS 2759/2 for low-alloy steels at this
strength), with furnace pyrometry to AMS 2750. Write the design strength on the drawing, not only the process.
      3. 

    3. Finish. Paint over a phosphate or primer base. Electroplating of ultra-high-strength steel can cause hydrogen
embrittlement. Thus use it only where you can justify it, and always follow it with an embrittlement-relief bake.
      4. 

    4. Additional items that are important for a pressure part: an inspection certificate (BS EN 10204 type 3.1 in the
UK), batch or heat marking with a method that does not cause stress raisers, and NDT after heat treatment. The v1
example shows magnetic particle inspection to ASTM E1444.
      5. 

    

    Special processes get their own lines
    Heat treatment, welding, NDT, coating and cleanliness are each separate notes with their own specification. If you
cannot verify a requirement by a measurement of the finished part, it needs an approved process and a certificate.
    

    Where this stops
    Section titled “Where this stops”
    

    Check the current issue
    Standards are revised and superseded, and sometimes they are withdrawn. The sheets here are a starting point from
REF·DB v1, with two v2 additions. Use the issue that your programme specifies, and use the preferred specifications of
your organisation where they exist.
    

      

    • Equivalent is not identical. Two specifications for “the same” alloy can have different impurity limits, melt
practice, testing and acceptance. Read designations before you substitute across systems.
      • 

    • Fracture-critical parts add flaw-size and NDE requirements and controlled quality classes to the drawing.
      • 

    • Composites and non-metallics are specified very differently. The lay-up, the process and the cure are the
material.
      •
