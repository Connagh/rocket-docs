Residual stress

In one lineResidual stress is the stress in a part that has no load on it. The drawing does not show it, it can be as large as yield, and it adds to each service stress.
Before thisFatigue · Allowables
Used byProof testing · Leak before break

Why this matters

Section titled “Why this matters”

Each weld, each machined or ground surface, and each quenched or straightened part has stresses that no load analysis shows. They can be as large as yield. They add to the service stress at each point, and they are always present:

  • Fatigue. Residual tension decreases the life. Residual compression from peening or rolling increases it, often by several times.
  • Fracture. Residual tension decreases the flaw size that a part can tolerate.
  • Stress-corrosion cracking. This needs only sustained tension, and residual stress is always sustained. A high-strength steel part can crack in storage with no load on it.
  • Distortion. If you machine away a stressed layer, the part moves.

On a solid motor, residual stress is in the welds of a high-strength steel case, the ground surfaces near them, rolled threads, peened fillets, and quenched aluminium skirts and closures. There are also thermal residual stresses in composite cases, bonded joints and the propellant grain.

What it is

Section titled “What it is”

A residual stress is the elastic response to a misfit. A region has yielded, transformed or shrunk, so that it does not fit the material around it. The material around it forces it to fit. With no external load, the stresses must balance across all cuts through the part, with no net force and no net moment:

∫Aσres dA=0\int_A \sigma_{res}\,dA = 0∫A​σres​dA=0 ∫Aσres y dA=0\int_A \sigma_{res}\,y\,dA = 0∫A​σres​ydA=0

Thus tension in one location always has compression in a different location. When machining removes part of the section, the stresses in the remaining material balance again, and the part distorts.

Think of it like…

Toughened glass

To make toughened glass, you heat a glass panel and blow cold air on its surfaces. The surfaces become solid first. The hot core then cools and shrinks. It pulls the surfaces into compression, and the core stays in tension. A scratch in the surface must overcome that compression before it can grow, so the glass is several times stronger. But the stored energy is still there. If a crack gets through to the core, the full panel breaks into small pieces. A Prince Rupert’s drop works in the same way, in one drop of glass.

How it adds to service stress

Section titled “How it adds to service stress”

While all the material is elastic, the residual stress and the service stress add. This has three results.

The static strength of a ductile part almost does not change. The residual stress balances itself, so it yields away as the part approaches collapse. This is not true for brittle parts, buckling and distortion. In these cases the residual stress stays.

Yield sets a limit. If the service stress plus the residual stress is more than yield, the excess yields away, and less residual stress stays after the load:

σres′=min⁡(σres,  Fty−σmax)\sigma'_{res} = \min\left(\sigma_{res},\; F_{ty} - \sigma_{max}\right)σres′​=min(σres​,Fty​−σmax​)

There is the same limit in compression. Thus one large load (a proof test or an overload) is a mechanical stress relief, and it can leave compression at notches that yielded. Also, large fatigue cycles remove residual stress. Thus residual stress has the largest effect in high-cycle fatigue.

In fatigue it is a mean stress. At the surface where cracks start, the residual stress adds to the service mean:

σm,eff=σm+σres′\sigma_{m,eff} = \sigma_m + \sigma'_{res}σm,eff​=σm​+σres′​

Then use Goodman or a different mean-stress rule, as on the Fatigue page. Tension moves the cycle towards failure. Compression moves it away. This page, as the fatigue page, gives credit to compression only until it cancels the tensile mean stress.

In fracture it is a crack-driving stress, with or without a load:

K=Y (σ+σres)πaK = Y\,(\sigma + \sigma_{res})\sqrt{\pi a}K=Y(σ+σres​)πa​

The critical flaw depth changes as 1/(σ+σres)21/(\sigma + \sigma_{res})^21/(σ+σres​)2. Thus even a small residual stress causes a large decrease in the tolerable flaw size (Leak before break). Fitness-for-service procedures such as BS 7910 and API 579-1 give residual stresses where they are not measured: up to yield in as-welded joints, and a fraction of yield after post-weld heat treatment.

In stress corrosion it is often the only cause. Stress-corrosion cracking needs a susceptible material, an environment and sustained tension. Residual tension always gives the tension. A flaw grows when KKK from the residual stress alone is more than KISCCK_{ISCC}KISCC​. Typical examples are high-strength steels, and 7075-T6 aluminium loaded across its short-transverse grain. This is why over-aged tempers such as T73 and T7351 exist.

Autofrettage

Section titled “Autofrettage”

If you pressurise a thick cylinder above its first-yield pressure, it yields from the bore outwards to a radius ccc. When you remove the pressure, the elastic outer wall squeezes the yielded inner wall, and the bore stays in hoop compression. For a bore of radius aaa and an outside radius bbb (Hill, Tresca):

pA=Fty[ln⁡ca+b2−c22b2]p_A = F_{ty}\left[\ln\frac{c}{a} + \frac{b^2 - c^2}{2b^2}\right]pA​=Fty​[lnac​+2b2b2−c2​]

This goes from first yield at Fty(b2−a2)/2b2F_{ty}(b^2-a^2)/2b^2Fty​(b2−a2)/2b2 to full overstrain at Ftyln⁡(b/a)F_{ty}\ln(b/a)Fty​ln(b/a). In service, the pressure cycles the bore around a compressive mean. Thus cracks at the bore almost do not open, and the peak stress moves out to the edge of the yielded zone. Too much overstrain on a thick wall causes reverse yield of the bore when you remove the pressure. In real steels this occurs sooner, because the reverse yield strength decreases after overstrain (the Bauschinger effect). Autofrettage is used on gun tubes, high-pressure pumps and tubing, and on the metal liners of composite-overwrapped pressure vessels. The liner yields one time. After that it operates elastically, in compression at zero pressure.

Almen strips control the shot-peening intensity (SAE J442 and J443). They measure the process, not the part. Each measurement gives an average over its own gauge volume. Steep gradients near the surface or a weld need a method that is applicable to them.

Relieving and controlling it

Section titled “Relieving and controlling it”
  • Thermal stress relief. At temperature, the yield strength decreases and creep relaxes the stress. Slow cooling prevents its return. Stay below the tempering temperature of quenched-and-tempered steels, or they become softer. The temperature and time come from the material specification.
  • Weld, then heat-treat. High-strength steel cases are usually welded before their final hardening and tempering, which also relieves the welds. A repair weld after heat treatment causes near-yield tension again and an untempered heat-affected zone. Thus it needs the full heat treatment again, or it is not permitted.
  • Mechanical relief. After quenching, aluminium plate and forgings are stretched or compressed by a few per cent (the T651, T7351 and T652 tempers), because heat would over-age the alloy. A proof test relieves tensile residual stress where it causes yield.
  • Process control. Control the welding sequence and heat input. Use sharp tools and light finishing cuts. Grind with controlled feeds and coolant, and inspect for burns. For parts that easily distort: machine roughly, relieve the stress, then finish.
  • Put compression where cracks start. Use shot peening, laser peening, deep rolling, threads rolled after heat treatment, and cold-expanded holes. Peen after the last machining operation. Check at temperature, because peened layers relax when hot.

    Worked example: residual stress in the 300 mm motor’s hardware

A repair weld after heat treatment would cause near-yield tension again, in an untempered heat-affected zone.

    3. The actuator bracket. The 4340 bracket from the Fatigue page lasts 6.2 service lives as machined. Its fillet has local cycles of 444 on 444 MPa in handling, and 320 on 107 MPa in firings.
      • Shot peened, with −400 MPa at the surface. The handling mean decreases to 44 MPa, the equivalent amplitude decreases from 680 to 460 MPa, and the handling life increases from 16,000 to 126,000 cycles. Firings get less benefit, because their mean becomes compressive and compression gets no credit. The bracket lasts 25 service lives, 4.1 times longer.
      • Abusively ground, with +300 MPa. The first handling cycle takes the surface to yield, so the residual stress relaxes to 292 MPa and stays there. The handling life decreases to 1,670 cycles, and the bracket lasts 0.73 service lives. It fails in service, because of a surface treatment that no drawing shows.
    4. Autofrettage. Motor cases at 10 MPa never need it, but high-pressure test equipment does. A steel tube has a 10 mm bore and a 25 mm outside diameter, with Fty=1000F_{ty} = 1000Fty​=1000 MPa. It operates at 300 MPa. Without autofrettage, its bore has 714 MPa of Tresca stress. Autofrettage at 650 MPa yields the inner 23% of the wall, and leaves −548 MPa of hoop stress at the bore. At working pressure, the bore is still in compression (−133 MPa). The peak stress is 537 MPa at the edge of the yielded zone, which is 25% lower. Pressure cycles at the bore now go between −548 and −133 MPa, so a crack there almost does not open. 650 MPa is almost the best value for this tube. More overstrain increases the peak again.

    Where this stops

    Section titled “Where this stops”
    • Elastic–perfectly plastic, uniaxial. Real relaxation depends on the cyclic stress–strain curve, multiaxial stress and the number of cycles. Residual stresses also decrease at temperature.
    • Uniform residual stress across a flaw. Real profiles change quickly with depth. Fitness-for-service procedures integrate them through the crack.
    • Autofrettage without the Bauschinger effect, with Tresca and elastic unloading. Codes such as ASME VIII Division 3 use corrected methods.
    • Values come from measurement or your assessment procedure, not from typical numbers such as the numbers in this example.

    On the drawing

    Section titled “On the drawing”
    • Stress relief. Heat treatment after welding, forming or straightening, with the temperature and time of the material specification. Also tell if straightening or repair welding is permitted.
    • Shot peening. Areas and masking, intensity (Almen), coverage and media to the applicable specification (for example SAE AMS 2430). Peening must follow the last machining operation.
    • Grinding. Inspection for grinding burns on high-strength steel, and limits on stock removal.
    • Thread rolling after heat treatment for fatigue-critical fasteners. See Threads.
    • Stress-relieved tempers (T651, T7351) for aluminium parts machined from thick stock.
    • Autofrettage pressure, and the verification after it, for autofrettaged parts.
