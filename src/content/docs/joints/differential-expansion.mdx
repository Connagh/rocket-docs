## Why this matters

A motor is assembled at room temperature. It is then stored, transported and fired anywhere in its qualified range. It
is made of materials that grow very differently with temperature: a steel or composite case, aluminium skirts and
closures, titanium fittings, rubber insulation, carbon nozzle parts, and a propellant grain that expands approximately
eight times as much as steel. Temperature squeezes, opens or slides each interface between these materials. Examples
are skirts that become loose, bonds that peel at the edges, seal glands that open, bolts that lose preload, and grains
that crack.

## Free and constrained expansion

A free part grows by $\varepsilon = \alpha\,\Delta T$. If you stop it, the same strain becomes stress:

$$
\sigma = E\,\alpha\,\Delta T \quad \text{held in one direction}
$$

$$
\sigma = \frac{E\,\alpha\,\Delta T}{1-\nu} \quad \text{held in a plane}
$$

For steel over 100 °C, held in one direction, this is $205\,000 \times 12\times10^{-6} \times 100 = 246$ MPa, from
temperature only. Between two joined parts, the important values are the **mismatch**, $(\alpha_1 - \alpha_2)\,\Delta T$,
and the stiffness with which the joint makes them share it.

<Analogy title="A kettle thermostat">
  A bimetal strip is two metals bonded face to face. When you heat it, the metal that grows more cannot move away from
  the other metal, so the strip bends. Each bonded or tightly joined pair of materials in a motor is a bimetal strip. The
  mismatch must go somewhere: into bending, into stress or into slip.
</Analogy>

## Fits that change with temperature

An inner part of diameter $d$ in an outer part changes its diametral fit by:

$$
\Delta\delta = d\,(\alpha_{inner} - \alpha_{outer})\,\Delta T
$$

If the outer part expands more (aluminium around steel), the fit becomes looser when hot and tighter when cold. The
contact pressure for an interference $\delta$ follows the Lamé thick-cylinder solution:

$$
p = \frac{\delta}{\dfrac{d}{E_o}\left(\dfrac{D^2 + d^2}{D^2 - d^2} + \nu_o\right) + \dfrac{d}{E_i}\left(\dfrac{d^2 + d_i^2}{d^2 - d_i^2} - \nu_i\right)}
$$

Here $D$ is the diameter of the outer part and $d_i$ is the bore of the inner part. Thus:

- **Check the two ends.** The interference must stay at the loose extreme, and the outer part must not yield at the
  tight extreme.
- **Do not use friction across dissimilar metals** to locate a part or carry load if the fit becomes zero in the range.
  Use a pin, a key, a bolt or a shoulder, and let the fit only centre the part.
- **Use materials with similar expansion where possible.** Titanium inside steel becomes tighter when hot, not looser.

## Bonded joints

A thin layer bonded to a thick part takes the full mismatch as an equal-biaxial stress,
$\sigma = \tfrac{E}{1-\nu}(\alpha_{sub} - \alpha_{layer})\,\Delta T$. Between two adherends of similar thickness, the
adhesive transfers the mismatch by shear. The shear is highest at the **ends** of the overlap (shear lag):

$$
\tau_{max} = \frac{G}{t_a}\,\frac{\Delta\alpha\,\Delta T}{\beta}\tanh\frac{\beta L}{2}
$$

$$
\beta^2 = \frac{G}{t_a}\left(\frac{1}{E_1 t_1} + \frac{1}{E_2 t_2}\right)
$$

- **A longer overlap does not decrease the peak** when $L$ is more than a few times $1/\beta$. The middle of a long bond
  carries no shear.
- **A thicker or softer bondline decreases the peak.** For a long overlap, the peak changes as $\sqrt{G/t_a}$. Tapered
  ends and adherends with closer expansion also decrease it.
- **Adhesives are viscoelastic.** A slow cool-down relaxes some of the stress. A fast thermal shock does not. This is
  why cold shock is the difficult case. See [Viscoelasticity](../../fundamentals/viscoelasticity/).
- **The bond between the grain and the case** is the largest bonded mismatch on a motor. The
  [Propellant grain](../../motor/propellant-grain/) page analyses it.

## Bolted joints

- **Preload changes with temperature** when the bolt and the clamped parts expand differently. Steel bolts in an
  aluminium flange lose preload when cold. See the temperature section of [Bolted joints](../bolted-joints/#temperature).
- **Plates slide against each other.** Two plates of different materials bolted together move relative to each other by
  $\Delta\alpha\,\Delta T\,R$, at a distance $R$ from their common fixed point. Within the hole clearance, friction
  stores this movement as shear until the joint slips. This causes preload loss and fretting. After the clearance is
  closed, the outer bolts bear, and each cycle shears them. Use one fixed hole with radially slotted holes for the
  others, or use flexures.

## Transients

A uniform temperature is the easy case. During firing, the inner parts of the nozzle and the insulation heat by
hundreds of degrees in seconds, while the outer shells stay cool. The hot inner part then grows against a cold outer
part. This causes compression during heating and gaps during cooling. For this reason, nozzle throat inserts are
installed in their housings with designed gaps and compliant layers. Use [Transient thermal](../../motor/transient-thermal/)
for the temperatures, and then the methods of this page for the fit at each moment.

## What it means for a motor

| Interface | Cold | Hot | Design response |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Propellant grain on the case | Grain shrinks: bore strain, bond tension | Grain grows: compression | Stress-free temperature, bore shape, stress-relief flaps ([Propellant grain](../../motor/propellant-grain/)) |
| Aluminium skirt or closure on a steel case | Grips harder | Becomes loose | Pin or bolt; the fit only centres |
| Steel or titanium fitting in an aluminium part | Becomes tighter | Becomes loose | Size the fit for the hot end; inserts |
| Metal ring bonded to a composite case | Ring shrinks onto the composite: edge shear | The opposite | Tapered ends, compliant adhesive, check the two extremes |
| Carbon or graphite throat in a steel housing | Housing grips the throat | Throat heats and grows during firing | Designed gaps, compliant layers |
| Seal gland across two materials | Gap decreases or increases | The opposite | Check the squeeze at the two ends ([Sealing strategy](../../seals/sealing-strategy/)) |
| Bolted flange, aluminium on steel | Preload decreases; plates slide | Preload increases | Preload margin at the cold end; slotted holes |

## Worked example: the 300 mm motor from −40 to +60 °C

The motor is assembled at 20 °C. Thus the cold end is 60 °C below assembly and the hot end is 40 °C above.

1. **Aluminium skirt ring on the steel attach ring.** The interface is ⌀310 mm, the ring OD is 330 mm, the attach-ring
   bore is 290 mm, the interference at assembly is 0.10 mm, the length is 30 mm and the friction is 0.15. The mismatch
   is $310 \times (12.3 - 23.4)\times10^{-6} = -3.4$ μm for each degree:
   - **at −40 °C,** the interference increases to 0.31 mm. This gives 3.3 MPa of contact pressure and 53 MPa in the
     aluminium, and the fit holds 14.5 kN;
   - **at +60 °C,** it becomes a **0.04 mm clearance**: the skirt is loose.

   The interference alone cannot carry the skirt. It must have pins or bolts. A titanium ring would become tighter when
   hot, and it keeps 0.03 mm of interference when cold.
2. **Carbon-fibre skirt bonded to the steel case.** CFRP 3 mm thick on the 1.37 mm case wall, with an epoxy bondline of
   0.2 mm, a shear modulus of 800 MPa and a 50 mm overlap. At −40 °C, the peak adhesive shear is **12.2 MPa** at each
   end. All of the shear is within approximately 15 mm of the ends. If you double the overlap, nothing changes. If you
   double the bondline to 0.4 mm, the peak decreases by √2, to approximately 8.6 MPa. Compare with the allowable of the
   adhesive at −40 °C, in cold shock.
3. **Aluminium closure plate bolted to a steel flange.** At the 140 mm bolt circle, the plates slide **0.093 mm**
   relative to each other at −40 °C. This is within the 0.5 mm hole clearance. But with close-fit holes, the outer bolts
   would bear.
4. **The grain.** Propellant at $100\times10^{-6}$/K on the steel case shrinks 2.6 mm more on diameter than the case,
   between its stress-free temperature of 60 °C and −40 °C. The [Propellant grain](../../motor/propellant-grain/) page
   changes this into bore strain and bond stress.

## Where this stops

- **Uniform temperature and room-temperature properties.** Modulus, yield and expansion all change across the range.
  Transients need the temperature field from [Transient thermal](../../motor/transient-thermal/).
- **Elastic.** Yield at a tight fit, creep and adhesive relaxation change the result after the first cycle.
- **One-dimensional bond model.** Real bond ends also have peel stress and a stress singularity. You need a
  finite-element model or a test for allowables.
- **Composites are anisotropic.** The hoop expansion and the axial expansion of a filament-wound case are different.
  Use the values of the laminate in each direction.

## On the drawing

- **Fits at a stated temperature:** the interference or clearance, and the temperature at which it applies.
- **The positive feature that carries load** (pins, keys, bolts) when the fit can become zero.
- **Slotted holes and the fixed point** for bolt patterns across dissimilar materials.
- **Bond details:** adhesive, bondline thickness (controlled with spacers or scrim), end taper and surface preparation.
- **The temperature range** for which the design was checked.
