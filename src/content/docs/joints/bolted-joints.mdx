## Why this matters

On a motor, bolts hold the closures and the nozzle on against chamber pressure, attach the igniter, skirts and mounted
units, and keep the seal glands closed. They fail in a few distinct ways:

- **Separation** — the clamped parts lift apart. On a pressure joint the seal gland opens, which is a leak path.
- **Yielding** — while tightening, from tension and thread torsion together, or later under load.
- **Fatigue** — at the first engaged thread, where the stress concentration is worst.
- **Loosening** — vibration that turns the nut back; see [Random vibration](../../verify/random-vibration/).
- **Thread stripping** — a separate check, on the [Threads](../threads/) page.

All of them depend on one number that is hard to control: the **preload**.

## Torque and preload

Tightening torque goes three ways: stretching the bolt up the thread helix, overcoming thread friction, and overcoming
friction under the head or nut:

$$
T = F_i\left[\frac{P}{2\pi} + \frac{\mu_t\,d_2}{2\cos 30^\circ} + \mu_b\,\frac{D_{km}}{2}\right] \;\approx\; K\,F_i\,d
$$

with thread pitch $P$, pitch diameter $d_2$, thread and head friction $\mu_t$, $\mu_b$, and mean bearing diameter
$D_{km}$. The short form uses a single **nut factor** $K$.

<TorqueSplit />

Friction varies from bolt to bolt, so a given torque gives a band of preloads, not one value. Torque control is the
least accurate method; turn-of-nut, measuring bolt stretch (for example ultrasonically) and direct tension indicators
are progressively tighter. The scatter for your method, and how much preload you may count on, come from your governing
standard.

## Stiffness and the joint diagram

The bolt and the clamped parts are two springs in series under the same preload: the bolt stretches by $F_i/k_b$, the
parts compress by $F_i/k_m$. The bolt's stiffness comes from its sections in series:

$$
\frac{1}{k_b} = \frac{1}{E_b}\left(\frac{0.5d}{A_d} + \frac{l_s}{A_d} + \frac{l_t}{A_{d3}} + \frac{0.5d}{A_{d3}} + \frac{0.4d}{A_d}\right)
$$

— head, plain shank, threaded length in the grip, engaged thread and nut. The clamped parts carry the load in two 30°
cones from the bearing faces:

$$
k_m = \frac{\pi E_m d \tan 30^\circ}{2 \ln\!\left[\dfrac{(l\tan 30^\circ + d_w - d)(d_w + d)}{(l\tan 30^\circ + d_w + d)(d_w - d)}\right]}
$$

An external load $P$ that pulls the parts apart stretches the bolt a little more and unloads the parts a little. Only
the **load factor** share reaches the bolt:

$$
\Phi = \frac{k_b}{k_b + k_m}, \qquad P_{sep} = \frac{F_i}{1-\Phi}
$$

$$
F_b = F_i + \Phi P, \qquad F_c = F_i - (1-\Phi)P
$$

Clamped parts are usually much stiffer than the bolt, so $\Phi$ is small — often 0.1 to 0.3. Once $P$ reaches
$P_{sep}$ the clamp force is gone, the joint opens, and the bolt carries all of the load.

<Analogy title="A tug of war on a rope already pulled tight">
  Two teams are holding a rope taut against a post between them. A child adds a small pull to one team: the rope's
  tension barely changes — the extra pull mostly just eases the force on the post. Only when the post's force reaches
  zero does every extra pull go straight into the rope. The bolt is the rope, the clamped parts are the post.
</Analogy>

## Margins

Each check uses the preload that is worst for it, and the factors and preload scatter from your programme
([Margin policy](../../verify/margin-policy/)):

- **Separation** — minimum preload (low scatter, losses, the worst temperature):
  $MS = F_{i,min} / \big(k_{sep}(1-\Phi)P\big) - 1$.
- **Yield and ultimate** — maximum preload, with the factored load: the bolt load is $F_{i,max} + \Phi\,kP$ while
  the joint stays closed, and $kP$ once it has opened.
- **Tightening** — at maximum preload the bolt carries tension and thread torsion together:
  $\sigma_{vm} = \sqrt{\sigma^2 + 3\tau^2}$, with $\tau$ from the thread torque on the stress-area diameter. VDI 2230
  commonly tightens to about 90% of yield in this combined stress.

Standards such as NASA-STD-5020 set further rules — for example how preload is treated in the ultimate check, and which
loads count for separation. Follow your governing standard where it differs.

## Temperature

When the bolt and the clamped parts expand differently, the preload changes:

$$
\Delta F_i = (\alpha_m - \alpha_b)\,\Delta T\, l\;\frac{k_b k_m}{k_b + k_m}
$$

Steel bolts in an aluminium closure lose preload when cold and gain it when hot. Titanium or composite parts reverse the
sign. A motor is assembled at room temperature and stored, transported and fired anywhere across its qualified range,
so check both ends.

## Fatigue

Because only $\Phi P$ reaches the bolt, preload protects it: a load cycle from zero to $P$ swings the bolt stress by
only $\pm\Phi P / 2A_s$. Bolts still have low fatigue strength — the first engaged thread is a sharp notch — so rolled
threads (rolled after heat treatment where possible) and a high, reliable preload matter. Assess the swing on the
[Fatigue](../../fundamentals/fatigue/) page. A joint that separates under load loses this protection completely.

## Pressure closures on a motor

The chamber pressure acts over the seal diameter, and the bolts share the result:

$$
P = \frac{p\,\pi D_s^2/4}{n}
$$

- **The seal gland must stay closed** — at proof pressure as well as MEOP, at the cold corner, with the minimum
  preload. If the parts lift, the O-ring has to follow the gap; see [O-ring design](../../seals/o-ring-design/).
- **Flanges rotate and pry.** A flange that bends adds a prying force that this page's concentric model leaves out:
  keep bolts close to the shell wall and the flange stiff, and analyse prying where the flange is thin.
- **Many smaller bolts** spread the clamp more evenly than a few large ones, and keep the gland closed between bolts.

## Worked example — the aft closure

The 300 mm case closes with a bolted aft closure. MEOP is 10.0 MPa ([MEOP build-up](../../motor/propellant/)), the seal
diameter is 260 mm, and there are 30 M10 class 12.9 bolts through a 24 mm steel grip (16 mm of plain shank), tightened to
60 N·m with a lubricant giving μ = 0.12. The preload scatter of ±25% and the factors — separation 1.2, yield 1.1,
ultimate 1.25, fitting 1.15 — are **for this example only**; press **Load worked example**.

1. **Load:** $10.0 \times \pi \times 130^2 = 531$ kN on the closure, so $P = 17.7$ kN per bolt.
2. **Preload:** 36.5 kN ($K = 0.164$). Only 15% of the torque stretches the bolt. With ±25%, between 27.4 and 45.6 kN.
3. **Stiffness:** $k_b = 362$ kN/mm and $k_m = 2097$ kN/mm, so $\Phi = 0.147$: each bolt feels 2.6 kN of its 17.7 kN.
4. **Separation:** the joint opens at $27.4/(1 - 0.147) = 32.1$ kN. Against $1.2 \times 17.7 = 21.2$ kN,
   **MS = +0.51**.
5. **Yield:** factored load $1.1 \times 1.15 \times 17.7 = 22.4$ kN; bolt load $45.6 + 0.147 \times 22.4 = 48.9$ kN
   against $1100 \times 58.0 = 63.8$ kN, **MS = +0.30**.
6. **Ultimate:** $1.25 \times 1.15 \times 17.7 = 25.4$ kN; bolt load 49.4 kN against 70.7 kN, **MS = +0.43**. The
   joint is still closed, so preload carries most of it.
7. **Tightening:** 70% of yield in combined stress at nominal preload, 87% at the top of the scatter.
8. **Fatigue:** each pressurisation swings the bolt by only ±22 MPa.

**The same closure in aluminium** (7075, same bolts, same torque) shows the trade-offs. The softer flange raises
$\Phi$ to 0.33, and a cold soak 60 °C below assembly temperature costs 4.1 kN of preload. The separation margin
actually rises, to +0.64, because less of the load comes out of the clamp — but the bolt now sees a third of every load:
the yield margin falls to +0.14 and the fatigue swing more than doubles, to ±50 MPa.

And **dry threads** at the same 60 N·m give only 25.6 kN — 30% less preload, with every margin that depends on it.

## Where this stops

- **One bolt, concentric axial load, one clamped material.** Eccentric loads, prying and flange rotation need VDI 2230's
  eccentric method or a finite-element model. Loads applied within the clamped parts, rather than under the head and
  nut, lower $\Phi$ (VDI's load-introduction factor).
- **No gaskets.** A soft gasket in the grip makes $k_m$ small and $\Phi$ large.
- **Shear joints** are a different check: friction grip from the clamp force, or bearing and bolt shear once they slip.
- [Thread stripping](../threads/) and [locking](../locking-and-retention/) are their own topics. Embedding and relaxation are entered here as a
  percentage loss.

## On the drawing

- **Torque value and tolerance, and the lubricant** — the lubricant matters as much as the torque.
- **Tightening sequence and passes** — a cross pattern in stages, so the flange pulls down evenly.
- **Fastener specification** — property class or material specification, thread class, plating and its hydrogen
  embrittlement relief, washer type and hardness.
- **Locking feature and reuse rules** — whether a fastener or locking element may be reused, and any re-torque after
  a soak or test.
