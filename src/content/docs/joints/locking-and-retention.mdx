## Why this matters

A fastener that backs off does damage twice: the joint loses its clamp — a closure leaks, a bracket rattles and
fatigues — and the loose part becomes debris that can jam a mechanism or puncture something downstream. Motors and
their hardware are shaken in transport, on the vehicle and by their own burn, and NASA-STD-5020B notes that previous
missions have been affected by fasteners that loosened and came free.

There are two ways to lose preload:

- **Non-rotational** — embedding, relaxation, creep and temperature. Covered on the
  [Bolted joints](../bolted-joints/) page.
- **Rotational self-loosening** — the nut actually turns back. This page.

## Why a tight nut turns back

<ThreadRamp />

While nothing slips, a tightened thread is self-locking: the friction on the thread and under the head resists turning
several times harder than the thread's ramp pushes the nut off. Gerhard Junker showed in 1969 that **sideways (transverse)
slip** of the joint is what breaks this. Each time the clamped parts slide relative to each other, the friction is used
up resisting the slip, and for that moment only the ramp acts on the nut:

$$
T_\text{ramp} = \frac{F_i\,P}{2\pi}
$$

A tiny rotation per cycle is enough: at vibration frequencies of tens to hundreds of hertz, it adds up quickly. Axial vibration
loosens far less readily than transverse vibration, and localised slip at the thread or under the head can start the
process before the whole joint slides.

<Analogy title="A book on a tilted board">
  A book rests on a slightly tilted board: friction holds it. Now slide the board quickly from side to side. The book
  slips sideways each time, and while it is slipping, friction can't also stop it sliding down the tilt — so it creeps
  downhill, a little with every shake. The thread's ramp is the tilt; the joint's sideways slip is the shaking.
</Analogy>

## The first lock is preload

Stop the slip and the nut cannot loosen. The joint grips sideways with the interface friction times the clamp force:

$$
F_{Q,\text{slip}} = \mu_T \; q \; \big(F_{i,min} - (1-\Phi)P\big) \;\ge\; k \, F_Q
$$

with the minimum preload (scatter, losses and temperature from the [Bolted joints](../bolted-joints/) page), the axial
load's share $(1-\Phi)P$ taken off, $q$ slip planes, and your programme's factor $k$ on the transverse load $F_Q$. This
is why NASA-STD-5020B calls preload the **primary locking feature**. When the slip check fails, a locking feature only
limits the damage:

- **Raise the clamp** — more torque, a larger or stronger bolt, more bolts.
- **Add friction or slip planes** — surface condition matters; coatings, lubricant and contamination on the faying surfaces lower the grip.
- **Take the shear elsewhere** — dowels, shoulder bolts or a spigot, so the bolts only clamp.

## What locking features actually do

<JunkerCurves />

| Feature | What it does | Without preload? | Verify by (NASA-STD-5020B Table 4) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Safety wire, cable, cotter pin | Positive: rotation only until taut, then the part cannot come off | Yes — mechanical | Visual inspection after installation |
| Adhesive thread locker, cured | Holds preload comparatively well even in severe vibration | Yes | Torque test on witness samples |
| Prevailing torque — deformed thread, insert, patch | Keeps the fastener captive; does not hold preload once the joint slips | Yes | Running torque measured during installation |
| Serrated bearing face | Works well, but only while preload lasts; can damage the bearing surface | No | — |
| Jam nut | Relies on its own preload | No | — |
| Split or tooth lock washer | Flattens at a small fraction of preload: minimal, if any, locking | No | Not to be used as a secondary lock |

A prevailing-torque feature's running torque is a fraction of a newton-metre on small fasteners, while the
self-loosening moment of a slipping joint is several times larger. The standard gives $F_i P/2$ as an estimate, after
Hess (2017) — larger than the bare ramp torque $F_i P/2\pi$. So the feature cannot stop rotation: it stops the fastener
falling out.

## Safety wire, cotter pins and staking

- **Safety wire and cable** — routed so that tension in the wire pulls each fastener in the **tightening** direction.
  Practice is set by NASM 33540 and SAE AS567. On a bolt that turns in operation, wire the nut and bolt to each other
  only, not to the structure.
- **Cotter pins** — through a castellated nut and drilled bolt; the standard choice for clevis and pivot bolts.
- **Adhesive thread lockers** — anaerobic adhesives that cure between the threads. They only perform if the process
  is right: clean, primed where needed, the correct gap, and fully cured. NASA-STD-5020B requires the process to be
  developed and validated first. Pre-applied coatings that dry without bonding the threads are not adhesives and are not
  for critical joints.
- **Epoxy staking** — a fillet bridging the fastener head and the part. It is mostly used on small fasteners, up to about
  M4 or #8, and on electronics, where NASA-STD-8739.1 applies.

## Requirements for spaceflight hardware

NASA-STD-5020B §4.6 sets the rules for NASA spaceflight hardware; other programmes have equivalents:

- **At least one locking feature that does not depend on preload**, on every threaded fastening system, regardless of
  preload (§4.6.1). Jam nuts and serrated faces may not qualify, because they depend on preload. A redundant feature may
  be advisable for some joints (FAA AC 20-71).
- **A mechanical locking feature** — safety wire or a cotter pin — on any bolt that is an axis of rotation in operation
  (§4.6.2).
- **A validated process** for adhesive lockers and staking (§4.6.3).
- **Verification of presence and performance** (§4.6.4): inspection for mechanical features, running-torque measurement
  at installation for prevailing torque, and witness samples for adhesives.
- **Running torque and preload**: unless the torque is specified "above running torque", the maximum running torque
  comes off the minimum specified torque, and the minimum breakaway torque off the maximum, before preload is
  calculated (Appendix A.2, Eqs. 27–30).
- **Thread engagement**: the fastener must be long enough for fully formed threads to engage a prevailing-torque
  feature (§4.7.4).

## Worked example — the electronics unit on the aft skirt

The 4 kg unit from the [Random vibration](../../verify/random-vibration/#worked-example--a-4-kg-unit-on-an-aft-skirt-bracket)
page sees 29.0 g at 3σ — 1137 N — in its lateral test axis. It is held by four M6 class 10.9 screws in
prevailing-torque inserts. The maximum running torque is 0.4 N·m (an example, from the insert's part standard), and
the screws are tightened to 10 ± 0.5 N·m, not specified above running torque, with friction 0.12. The preload scatter
of ±25% and the slip factor of 1.25 are **for this example only** — press **Load worked example**.

1. **Load per screw:** $1137/4 = 284$ N sideways, shared evenly, ignoring the unit's overturning moment.
2. **Effective torque:** the running torque comes off the minimum, giving 9.1–10.5 N·m.
3. **Preload:** 9.5 kN at mid-range torque, and 6.6 kN at the minimum torque with −25% scatter.
4. **Slip:** with an interface friction of 0.15, the joint grips $0.15 \times 6.6 = 1.0$ kN per screw, against
   $1.25 \times 284 = 355$ N: **MS = +1.81**. It does not slip, so it will not self-loosen — preload is doing the
   locking.
5. **The insert:** preload-independent, so it meets §4.6.1, verified by measuring running torque at installation. But
   its 0.4 N·m is well below the 1.5 N·m ramp torque (4.8 N·m by the standard's estimate): it keeps the screws captive;
   it would not hold their preload if the joint slipped.

**Snug-tight at 2 N·m** — a common shortcut on a "light" bracket — gives only 1.5 kN of minimum preload and a grip of
219 N, below the factored 355 N: **MS = −0.38**. The joint slips on every large cycle of the qualification test, the
screws walk back to the inserts' running torque, and the unit rattles: a test failure, even though no screw falls out.

## Where this stops

- **Evenly shared shear.** Overturning moments, prying and uneven bolt spacing load some bolts more than others — check
  the worst one.
- **3σ is not the largest peak.** Random vibration has occasional peaks above 3σ (see Random vibration); a slip margin at
  3σ does not rule out rare micro-slip, which is one reason the locking feature is still required.
- **Localised slip** under the head or in the threads can start loosening before the whole joint slides; bending and
  thermal cycling can drive it too.
- **Interface friction** varies with finish, coating, contamination and wear — use measured or specified values.

## On the drawing

- **The locking feature, by specification** — insert or nut part number, adhesive and its cure, or safety wire per
  NASM 33540 with wire material and diameter.
- **The torque, and how running torque is handled** — "above running torque", or a measured running-torque range to
  subtract.
- **Verification** — running torque recorded at installation, witness samples for adhesive, inspection of wire and
  pins.
- **Reuse limits** — prevailing-torque features lose torque with each use; state whether fasteners may be reused.
