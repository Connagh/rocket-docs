## Why this matters

A thread is where a bolt's load passes into the part, and it fails in a less forgiving way than the bolt. A broken bolt is
obvious, ductile, and replaced in minutes. Stripped threads in a tapped closure or skirt can show nothing until the joint
lets go, and repairing them means drilling out and fitting an insert — sometimes scrapping the part. On a motor, tapped
holes in aluminium skirts, brackets and nozzle hardware, and in high-strength steel closures, are where this matters.

## Thread forms

| Form | Profile | Where it is used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ISO metric, M | 60°, flat or rounded root | General engineering; coarse pitch by default, fine pitch called up as M10 × 1.25 |
| Unified, UNC / UNF | 60°, the same profile in inch sizes | US hardware and older aerospace parts |
| MJ and UNJ | 60°, with a large controlled root radius on the bolt | Aerospace fasteners: lower stress concentration at the root, so better fatigue life |
| Trapezoidal, Acme | 30° or 29° | Power screws and actuators — moving loads, not clamping |
| Buttress | Asymmetric | Heavy one-direction loads |
| Pipe — NPT, BSPT | Tapered | Sealing on the flanks with a sealant; not a structural fastener thread |

**Fine or coarse?** A fine pitch has a slightly larger stress area and finer adjustment, but shallower threads, so it strips
and galls more readily in soft materials and cross-threads more easily. Coarse threads are the usual choice for tapped
holes in aluminium.

**Do not mix forms.** NASA-STD-5020B §4.7.2 lists incompatible pairs:
- fine with coarse, or inch with metric;
- the same pitch at different diameters;
- an external **MJ** thread in an **M** internal thread, or **UNJ** in **UN**.

The mismatches cause interference, seizure, low strength or the wrong preload for a given torque.

## Tolerance classes

ISO 965 gives each thread a **tolerance grade** (a number: smaller is tighter) and a **tolerance position** (a letter: how
far the zone sits from basic size). Internal threads use capitals, external lower case:

- **6H / 6g** — the general-purpose fit. The "g" position leaves a small allowance below basic size on the bolt.
- **4h6h / 4H5H** — the tighter classes used for MJ aerospace threads (ISO 5855).
- **Unified: 2A / 2B** for general use, **3A / 3B** for tighter, aerospace work.

Plating has to fit in that allowance. A coating of thickness $c$ on the 60° flanks grows the pitch diameter by $4c$, so a
plated thread made to "h" with no allowance will not go into its nut.

## Rolled or cut

**Rolled** threads are formed, not machined: the grain flows round the thread form and the root is left in compression.
They are much better in fatigue than cut threads, especially when rolled **after** heat treatment, so the compressive
layer is not relieved ([Residual stress](../../materials/residual-stress/)). Aerospace bolts are rolled; a thread cut on a lathe is a weaker, rougher feature, and the root of a
cut thread is a sharp notch for [fatigue](../../fundamentals/fatigue/).

## Thread stripping

<ThreadProfile />

When threads strip, each set shears on a cylinder. In the FED-STD-H28 / VDI 2230 form, with pitch $P$ and engagement
length $L_e$:

$$
A_\text{int} = \frac{\pi L_e\, d_{min}}{P}\left[\frac{P}{2} + \tan 30^\circ (d_{min} - D_{2,max})\right]
$$

$$
A_\text{ext} = \frac{\pi L_e\, D_{1,max}}{P}\left[\frac{P}{2} + \tan 30^\circ (d_{2,min} - D_{1,max})\right]
$$

with the bolt's major diameter $d$ and pitch diameter $d_2$, and the nut's minor diameter $D_1$ and pitch diameter $D_2$, at
their minimum-material limits. At basic size they reduce to $A_\text{int} = \tfrac{7}{8}\pi d L_e$ and
$A_\text{ext} = \tfrac{3}{4}\pi D_1 L_e$. The internal threads go when their shear ultimate × $A_\text{int}$ is reached, the bolt
threads at their shear ultimate × $A_\text{ext}$. The bolt itself breaks at $R_m A_s$.

**Make the bolt the weak link.** NASA-STD-5020B §4.7.4 asks that a tapped part have enough complete threads engaged for
the bolt to fail in tension before the threads strip: a broken bolt is visible, ductile, lets load move to its neighbours,
and is easy to repair.

- **In steel**, internal and external threads are about as strong as each other, and a standard nut height (about 0.8 d)
  is enough.
- **In aluminium**, the part's shear strength is a fraction of the bolt's, so engagement has to be longer — about
  1.5–2 d in practice. Basic-size areas give a shorter length; tolerances, dilation and thread bending use up the
  difference.
- **Thread strength is not uniform.** The first engaged threads carry most of the load, so very long engagement adds
  little; the extra length mostly protects against tolerance and wear.

<Analogy title="Tearing a strip of perforated stamps">
  A row of stamps is joined by perforations. Pull a short strip and it tears along the perforations; pull a long strip
  and the stamps themselves tear first. Engaged threads are the perforations: every extra thread adds a little strength
  until the bolt, not the thread, is the weakest part.
</Analogy>

## Inserts and procured internal threads

- **Helical-coil inserts** (NASM 8846) are wire coils in a hole tapped oversize — about $d + 1.3P$. The part's threads then
  shear at that larger diameter, so a given length is stronger. The coil also gives a hard, wear-resistant thread for
  repeated assembly, and a stripped hole can be repaired.
- **Key-locked inserts** (MIL-I-45914) are solid bushes staked in with keys. Heavy-duty versions resist pull-out better
  than coils of the same length.
- **Rate a procured nut or insert by its specification**, not by a stripping analysis, because they dilate under load. For
  an insert, use the lower of its internal-thread strength and its pull-out from the part (NASA-STD-5020B §4.4.1).
  Pull-out from non-metallic or sandwich parts needs test.
- **A stainless insert in aluminium** needs a primer at the interface against galvanic corrosion.

## Plating, galling and lubrication

- **Plating** grows the pitch diameter by four times its thickness (see above). On high-strength steels, electroplating
  needs a hydrogen-embrittlement relief bake.
- **Galling** — cold welding of the flanks — hits stainless steel and titanium threads, especially like-on-like. A
  lubricant, a dissimilar mating material, or silver plating on one part prevents it.
- **The lubricant sets the preload.** Thread friction $\mu_t$ is a third of the torque budget; see the torque split on
  [Bolted joints](../bolted-joints/).

## How threads feed the bolted-joint calculation

- **Stress area** $A_s$, from the pitch and minor diameters — the bolt's tensile strength is $R_m A_s$.
- **Thread friction** and the pitch term in the [torque–preload relation](../bolted-joints/#torque-and-preload).
- **Engaged thread length**, part of the bolt's compliance.
- **Prevailing torque**, if the internal thread carries a locking feature — see
  [Locking and retention](../locking-and-retention/).

## Worked example — the bracket screws

The four M6 class 10.9 screws that hold the electronics unit on the aft skirt (from
[Locking and retention](../locking-and-retention/)) go into a 6061-T6 bracket, with a shear ultimate of about 186 MPa
(use your allowable).

1. **The bolt** breaks at $1040 \times 20.1 = 20.9$ kN.
2. **Tapped 6 mm deep (1 d):** the bracket's threads shear on $\tfrac78 \pi \times 6 \times 6 = 99$ mm², at
   $186 \times 99 = 18.4$ kN. **The threads strip first** — strength ratio 0.88.
3. **Tapped 9 mm (1.5 d):** 27.6 kN, ratio 1.32: **the bolt breaks first**, at basic size. Equal strength comes at
   6.8 mm — the 1.5 d rule leaves room for tolerance, dilation and thread bending.
4. **A 6 mm helical-coil insert instead:** the bracket shears at ⌀7.3 mm — 22.4 kN, ratio 1.07. The bolt goes first
   in a shorter hole, and the insert's own rating must also be checked.
5. **In service:** the 9.5 kN preload loads the 9 mm tapped threads to about a third of their strength.

In a steel nut, equal strength comes at only 2.9 mm, which is why a standard nut (4.8 mm for M6) never strips before
the bolt.

## Where this stops

- **Basic-size areas are an upper bound.** For a design value, use minimum-material diameters from the tolerance class.
  VDI 2230's full method adds nut dilation and thread bending.
- **Static strength only.** Thread fatigue is governed by the root of the first engaged thread — rolled threads, MJ/UNJ
  roots and the [fatigue](../../fundamentals/fatigue/) method.
- **Temperature and creep.** Aluminium threads lose strength hot; long-term clamping of soft parts can relax.

## On the drawing

- **Thread designation and class** — for example M6 × 1 – 6H, MJ6 × 1 – 4H5H, or ¼-28 UNJF-3B.
- **Full-thread depth**, separate from drill depth, for tapped holes; the minimum engagement the analysis assumed.
- **Inserts by specification and length** (NASM 8846, MIL-I-45914), with primer for stainless in aluminium.
- **Plating with its thickness and the embrittlement relief**, and the thread lubricant.
- **Fastener length**: at least two pitches past a nut or insert, and clear of incomplete run-out threads
  (NASA-STD-5020B §4.7.4–4.7.5).
