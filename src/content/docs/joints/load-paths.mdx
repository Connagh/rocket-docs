## Why this matters

A motor is a pressure vessel and a structure at the same time. The pressure loads are enormous — over 700 kN on each
end of the 300 mm case on this site — but they balance inside the case. The loads that actually leave the motor, thrust,
inertia and bending, are an order of magnitude smaller, and they travel through whatever is stiffest, not necessarily
what was designed to carry them.

Tracing the load path — what carries what, from where to where — is how you find:

- **the joints that carry the pressure**, and the ones that never see it;
- **the attachment that takes the thrust**, and the one that should be left free;
- **the redundant path that fights the case** as it grows under pressure and temperature — often a bigger load than the
  thrust itself.

## Follow the pressure

Pressure pushes on every inside surface of the case. Over a closure, the end load is the pressure times the area inside
its seal:

$$
F_{end} = p\,\frac{\pi D^2}{4}
$$

The forward dome is pushed forward by $p\,\pi D^2/4$ over the case bore, and the aft closure and nozzle are pushed back
by almost as much. The case wall holds the two ends together in tension — the axial stress
[case sizing](../../motor/case-sizing/) sizes for. Almost all of the pressure load closes inside the case and never
reaches the vehicle.

The exception is the throat. Nothing faces it, so the forward dome is pushed harder than the aft end. The difference is
the thrust:

$$
F = C_F\, p\, A_t
$$

with $C_F$ the nozzle's thrust coefficient, which also counts the pressure on the divergent cone. The ratio of end load
to thrust is $D^2 / (C_F\,d_t^2)$: 24 for the motor in the worked example.

<Analogy title="You cannot push a car from inside it">
  Sit in a car and push on the dashboard as hard as you like: it does not move. Your hands push the car forward, your
  back pushes the seat back, and the two cancel inside the car. Pressure in a motor does the same thing to every wall.
  Only where there is a hole — the throat — is there nothing to push back, and that unbalanced patch is the thrust.
</Analogy>

The same bookkeeping gives the loads on the aft end's joints:

- **The nozzle** attached over a seal diameter $d_n$ is pushed off by $p\,\pi d_n^2/4$, less the thrust it produces:
  $F_{noz} = p\,\pi d_n^2/4 - F$. That blow-off load goes into its attachment.
- **The aft closure** sealed at $D_s$ carries $p\,\pi D_s^2/4 - F$ while firing, but the full $p\,\pi D_s^2/4$ in a
  static pressure test, which has no flow and so no thrust to relieve it. Size its joint for the test as well as firing.

## Through the skirts to the vehicle

The thrust accelerates the motor and everything it pushes. With thrust alone, the acceleration is
$a = F / (m_{motor} + m_{ahead})$, and the forward skirt passes on only what the mass ahead of it needs,
$m_{ahead}\,a$:

$$
F_{skirt} = F\,\frac{m_{ahead}}{m_{motor} + m_{ahead}}
$$

The rest accelerates the motor itself and is shed along the way: the case and closures through their own walls, the
grain through its bond — a mean shear of $m_{grain}\,a / (\pi D L)$. So:

- **The skirt load rises through the burn.** As propellant burns away, the same thrust pushes less motor and more
  payload; the peak comes near burnout, not at ignition.
- **The thrust compresses the case wall** between the aft end and the forward skirt. In a firing motor that only
  reduces the pressure tension a little. In an unpressurised one — an upper stage carrying the thrust of the stage
  below, or any motor during handling and transport — the case and skirts are thin shells in compression; see
  [Shell buckling](../../verify/shell-buckling/).
- **The aft skirt** carries the motor on the ground, or ties it to an interstage, fins or steering actuators. Whichever
  skirt is the thrust takeout, the other should be designed knowing it is not.

Bending from flight loads and steering adds to the axial load. Thin shells are checked in **running load** — force per
unit length of circumference:

$$
N = \frac{P}{\pi D} \pm \frac{4M}{\pi D^2}
$$

At the attach ring, the bolts share the same loads. For $n$ equally spaced bolts on a bolt circle $D_b$, with the joint
closed and its neutral axis at the centre, the most loaded bolt carries

$$
F_{bolt} = \frac{4M}{n\,D_b} - \frac{P}{n}
$$

with $P$ the axial compression. Compression from thrust relieves the bolts, so the worst bolt tension is often with
bending and **no** thrust: coast, handling, or before ignition. That bolt load is the external load for
[Bolted joints](../bolted-joints/).

## Stiffness and load sharing

When two paths join the same two points, they share one displacement, so they share load **by stiffness** — for a bar or
tube, $k = EA/L$:

$$
P_1 = P\,\frac{k_1}{k_1 + k_2}
$$

Paths in series add their flexibilities; paths in parallel add their stiffnesses. Three consequences catch designers out:

- **Gaps decide who goes first.** A path with clearance carries nothing until the joint has moved through it. In a
  shear joint with clearance holes, the first bolt to bear takes the load; in a long bolted or riveted lap, the end
  fasteners take the most.
- **Capacities do not add.** In a bonded and bolted joint the bond is far stiffer, takes nearly all the load, and fails
  at a displacement too small for the bolts to notice. The joint fails when its first path does — the bolts then carry
  everything alone, so they are a fail-safe, not extra strength.
- **Redundant paths fight each other.** If one path would grow more than the other — pressure growth, thermal
  expansion, a tolerance — joining them forces a load through both, with nothing applied:

$$
F = \delta\,\frac{k_1 k_2}{k_1 + k_2}
$$

A motor case grows axially under pressure by $\delta = p\,r\,L\,(1 - 2\nu)/(2E\,t)$, and radially by more. Attach it
rigidly at both skirts to a structure that does not grow, and the attachments carry that growth. The cure is the
classic one: **fix one end and let the other slide** — an axial slip joint with more travel than the growth, or a
flexure — while it still carries the radial and shear loads.

Radial growth loads the skirt itself. At MEOP the 300 mm case grows 0.68 mm on radius. An integral skirt has to follow
at its root: forced to follow fully, a steel skirt there would carry the case's 0.45% hoop strain — about 930 MPa, with
no pressure inside to justify it — fading within a few times $\sqrt{rt} \approx 14$ mm of the joint. In practice the
skirt also holds the case back locally, and the junction needs a shell or finite-element analysis. Composite cases strain
several times more, so their skirts are bonded on through a rubber shear ply that lets the case grow underneath.

<Analogy title="A four-legged table">
  A three-legged stool stands evenly on any floor. A four-legged table on an uneven floor rocks until one leg is
  shimmed — and then the stiffest, longest legs carry most of the weight. Redundant load paths behave like the table:
  they share by stiffness and fit, not by intention, and a small misfit can load a leg more than the weight does.
</Analogy>

## Joint types

| Joint | Where on a motor | How it carries load | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Integral or welded skirt (Y-joint) | Skirts on metal cases | Skirt machined, forged or welded on at the dome–cylinder junction | Discontinuity stress as the case grows and the skirt does not; weld quality at the junction |
| Bonded skirt with shear ply | Composite cases | Shear through a rubber ply that lets the case grow underneath | Peel at the ends; bond verification; the temperature range — [Differential expansion](../differential-expansion/) |
| Bolted flange, axial bolts | Closures, nozzles, attach rings | Preloaded bolts in tension; flange contact takes compression | Separation, prying, flange rotation — [Bolted joints](../bolted-joints/) |
| Radial bolts or pins in shear | Skirt to vehicle; closures of small motors | Fasteners through overlapping rings, in bearing and shear | Bearing, shear-out and net-section failure; hole clearance — the first fastener to bear |
| Tang-and-clevis pinned joint | Segmented case joints | Pins in double shear through a tang and clevis | Joint rotation opening the seal gap as the case dilates (Challenger, 1986) |
| Lock ring or segmented key | Closures of metal cases | A ring seated in matching grooves, in shear and bearing | Stress concentration at the grooves; the ring rolling out; assembly clearance |
| Threaded closure | Small motors | The thread carries the whole end load | Stripping — [Threads](../threads/); galling; seal compression |
| Clamp band | Stage and payload interfaces | A wedge-section band squeezes two flanges together | Band preload; gapping under bending. Any release device is outside this site's scope |

For the shear joints, each fastener's share $V$ (the load divided among the fasteners, plus any bending) is checked
several ways against your allowables and factors:

- **Fastener shear** — $\tau = V / (m\,A)$ with $m$ shear planes: one in a lap, two in a clevis.
- **Bearing** — $\sigma_{br} = V / (d\,t)$ on the hole in each part, usually the thinnest or softest.
- **Shear-out and net section** — the part tearing out to the edge, or failing across the row of holes. Edge distance
  and pitch set these.

Every joint also needs its sealing and stiffness thought through together: a joint that is strong enough but rotates or
opens under pressure is a leak path — see [Sealing strategy](../../seals/sealing-strategy/).

## Worked example — the 300 mm motor's load path

The 300 mm D6AC case at its MEOP of 10 MPa ([case sizing](../../motor/case-sizing/#worked-example)), with an
illustrative 50 mm throat and the grain page's $C_F = 1.5$. The aft closure seals at 260 mm with 30 bolts
([Bolted joints](../bolted-joints/#worked-example--the-aft-closure)); the nozzle seals into it at an illustrative 110 mm.
Masses are from the [hardware page](../../motor/hardware/): 137.81 kg at ignition, 26.61 kg at burnout, and a 50 kg
payload on the forward skirt. Thrust is held at MEOP to burnout, which bounds the loads.

1. **Pressure.** The forward dome is pushed forward by $10 \times \pi \times 150^2 = 707$ kN, the aft end back by
   677 kN. The 29.5 kN difference is the thrust — **24 times smaller** than the end loads, which close inside the case.
2. **Nozzle and closure.** The nozzle is pushed off by $10 \times \pi \times 55^2 = 95.0$ kN, less its thrust: a
   **65.6 kN** blow-off load into its attachment. The aft closure carries 501 kN firing (16.7 kN per bolt) but 531 kN in
   a static pressure test (17.7 kN) — the value the bolted-joints example rightly used.
3. **Acceleration.** $29.5 / (137.81 + 50) = 157$ m/s², **16.0 g** at ignition, rising to **39.2 g** at burnout.
4. **Into the payload.** The forward skirt passes on $29.5 \times 50 / 187.81 = 7.84$ kN at ignition and
   **19.2 kN** at burnout. The motor's own inertia takes the rest: 21.6 kN, then 10.2 kN. The grain's 17.4 kN at
   ignition is spread over its 0.94 m² bond as 18.5 kPa of shear — tiny.
5. **Bending.** With 3 kN·m at the forward skirt (the flight load the [shell-buckling](../../verify/shell-buckling/#worked-example--the-300-mm-case-unpressurised)
   example used), the skirt's running load is $20.3 + 42.0 = 62.3$ N/mm in compression at burnout — 31 MPa in a 2 mm
   wall.
6. **Attach ring.** Sixteen bolts on a 280 mm circle each take $4M/(nD_b) = 2.68$ kN from the bending. The thrust
   relieves them: 2.19 kN at ignition, 1.48 kN at burnout. The worst case is bending **without** thrust, 2.68 kN — the
   external load per bolt to take to [Bolted joints](../bolted-joints/).
7. **A redundant attachment.** Now suppose the motor is also fixed at its aft skirt to an aluminium airframe tube
   (⌀330 × 2 mm, 1000 mm between the skirts). The case is $205\,000 \times \pi \times 301.4 \times 1.377 / 1000 =
   267$ kN/mm stiff axially, the airframe 149 kN/mm; in series against a mismatch, 95.5 kN/mm.
   - **At MEOP** the case grows $10 \times 150 \times 1000 \times (1 - 0.58) / (2 \times 205\,000 \times 1.377) =
     1.12$ mm, forcing **107 kN** through both attachments.
   - **At −40 °C**, 60 °C below assembly, the aluminium has also shrunk 0.68 mm more than the steel: 1.80 mm in all,
     **172 kN** — almost six times the thrust, and nine times what the forward skirt passes to the payload.
   - Against an aft attachment good for 120 kN, with an ultimate factor of 1.25 — both **for this example only**;
     press **Load worked example** in the second calculator — **MS = −0.44**.
   - **The fix:** let the aft skirt slide axially with 2 mm of travel. The mismatch never closes the gap and the force
     is zero; the forward skirt alone takes the thrust.

## Where this stops

- **Quasi-static, thrust only.** Drag, weight, steering side loads, the ignition transient, thrust oscillation and
  vibration ([Random vibration](../../verify/random-vibration/)) come from the vehicle's loads analysis. The thrust here
  is held at MEOP, which bounds a real thrust trace.
- **Beam theory and an elastic bolt ring.** Local load introduction, prying, flange contact and a joint that opens on
  the tension side need detail analysis.
- **Two paths.** Real structures have many, with stiffnesses that need a finite-element model to find.
- **The nozzle attach plane is taken at chamber pressure.** The flow at the nozzle entrance lowers it slightly.
- **Elastic, room temperature.** Stiffness and capacity change across the temperature range; joints with gaps also
  slip and fret.

## On the drawing

- **The interface** — hole pattern, bolt circle, datums and mating surfaces, from the vehicle's interface control
  document, and which interface is the thrust takeout.
- **The fixed end and the sliding end** — the axial travel the slip joint allows, and what it still carries.
- **Interface limit loads** — axial, shear and bending at each attachment, usually in the interface control document.
- **Fastener torques and preload** at the attach rings and closures — [Bolted joints](../bolted-joints/).
- **Bond and shear-ply details** for bonded skirts — adhesive, ply thickness, surface preparation, the inspection that
  verifies it.
