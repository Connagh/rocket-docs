## Why this matters

Seals, bondlines, adhesives, electronics and heat-treated alloys all have temperature limits. Most hardware never gets
to steady state. It heats and cools in transients. Three problems occur frequently:

- **The structure is often hottest after the motor stops.** When the gas stops, the heat stored in the hot parts
  continues to move inward, because that is the only direction that it can go. This **soak-back** has its peak minutes
  after burnout. It can damage the seals, bonds and electronics that were not damaged during the burn.
- **The outside temperature is not the inside temperature.** A thin steel case follows the weather within half an
  hour. The propellant inside takes days. Thus a thermocouple on the case tells very little about the grain, and MEOP
  depends on the grain.
- **Fast heating causes gradients.** When heat arrives faster than it can spread, the surface and the core can have a
  difference of hundreds of degrees, with the related thermal stresses and strains.

## Lumped capacitance: one temperature

If the full part is at one temperature, an energy balance gives:

$$
\rho c\,V\,\frac{dT}{dt} = h A\,(T_\infty - T)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{T - T_\infty}{T_0 - T_\infty} = e^{-t/\tau},
\qquad
\tau = \frac{\rho c\,V}{h A} = \frac{\rho c\,L_c}{h}
$$

Here $L_c = V/A$ is the **characteristic length**. It is the thickness of a plate heated on one face, $R/2$ for a long
cylinder, and $R/3$ for a sphere.

- **The part never fully gets to the final temperature.** 63% of the difference closes in one $\tau$, 95% in three,
  and 99.3% in five. Thus ask how long the part takes to get to a stated limit:
  $t = \tau \ln\!\left[(T_\infty - T_0)/(T_\infty - T_\text{lim})\right]$.
- **Conductivity is not in the equation.** In the lumped regime, the inside follows the surface. All of the resistance
  is at the surface.
- **$h$ is usually the least accurate input.** Typical film coefficients are 2–25 W/m²K for free convection in air,
  25–250 for forced air, and up to tens of thousands for liquids and boiling. Combustion gas inside a motor gets to
  thousands, with the highest values at the nozzle throat (the Bartz correlation is the usual estimate). Radiation adds
  an equivalent $h_r = \varepsilon\sigma\,(T_s^2 + T_\infty^2)(T_s + T_\infty)$.

## When one temperature is not enough: the Biot number

$$
Bi = \frac{h\,L_c}{k}
$$

This compares the resistance to heat that spreads inside ($L_c/k$) with the resistance to heat that goes in ($1/h$).
Below approximately 0.1, the inside follows the surface and the lumped model is acceptable. Above 0.1, the part has a
real gradient, and the lumped results are not correct.

## Conduction through slabs, cylinders and spheres

When the Biot number is not small, solve the heat equation inside the part:

$$
\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \alpha\,\frac{1}{r^m}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r^m\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial r}\right),
\qquad
\alpha = \frac{k}{\rho c}
$$

Here $m = 0$ for a slab, 1 for a cylinder and 2 for a sphere. The **thermal diffusivity** $\alpha$ sets the time scale.
In a time $t$, heat goes into the part by approximately $\sqrt{\alpha t}$. Thus a part of thickness $L$ takes a time of
the order of $L^2/\alpha$ to heat through.

| Part | $\alpha$, m²/s | $L^2/\alpha$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aluminium, 10 mm | $6.2 \times 10^{-5}$ | 1.6 s |
| Steel, 10 mm | $1.2 \times 10^{-5}$ | 8 s |
| Titanium alloy, 10 mm | $2.8 \times 10^{-6}$ | 35 s |
| Carbon phenolic, 10 mm | $3.7 \times 10^{-7}$ | 5 min |
| EPDM insulation, 6 mm | $1.1 \times 10^{-7}$ | 5 min |
| Propellant, 150 mm | $1.7 \times 10^{-7}$ | 36 h |

The calculator solves this equation numerically for one or two layers. It uses an implicit finite-volume scheme, which
is stable at all time steps. Its automated tests compare it with the exact series solutions for the three shapes. The
difference is less than 0.2%.

## Soak-back

During a firing, the insulation and liners take the heat and store it. At burnout the source stops, but the stored heat
stays. It flows down the temperature gradient: inward, into the structure. The back face continues to get hotter long
after the gas has stopped, and it has its peak minutes later.

<Analogy title="A pan handle off the hob">
  Take a cast-iron pan off the hob. The handle continues to get hotter for a few minutes. The flame has stopped, but the
  heat stored in the pan still spreads along it. Motor hardware does the same after burnout.
</Analogy>

These items decide the peak:

- **How much heat is stored near the surface.** A thicker and hotter heated layer has more heat to release.
- **What is behind it.** A thick metal part absorbs the heat, and its peak is lower and later. A thin wall has a high
  peak, and the peak comes sooner.
- **What occurs at the hot face after burnout.** If the face can radiate or convect heat away, less heat goes inward.
  An insulated hot face gives the upper limit. The [insulation page](../insulation/) gives the two limits.

## Worked examples

### 1: A thin case follows the weather

This is the 1.377 mm steel case from [case sizing](../case-sizing/#worked-example), alone, in still air
($h \approx 8$ W/m²K), when the air temperature increases from 20 °C to 40 °C. Use the **Thin case, weather** preset.

- $Bi = 8 \times 0.001377 / 43 = 2.6 \times 10^{-4}$. The case is fully lumped.
- $\tau = 7850 \times 480 \times 0.001377 / 8 = 649$ s, approximately **11 minutes**.
- The case is within 1 K of the air (95% of the step) after $3\tau \approx$ **33 minutes**.

### 2: Conditioning a grain

Before a test firing, a motor is kept at its hot-limit temperature, for example 60 °C, so that the firing represents
the hot-day MEOP. Assume that the 300 mm grain is a solid cylinder of propellant ($k = 0.4$ W/m·K,
$\rho = 1770$ kg/m³, $c = 1300$ J/kg·K, illustrative values). It starts at 20 °C in a conditioning chamber with forced
air at 60 °C ($h \approx 10$ W/m²K). Use the **Conditioning a grain** preset.

- $Bi = 10 \times 0.075 / 0.4 = 1.9$. This is far from lumped.
- **The lumped model** gives $\tau = 1770 \times 1300 \times 0.075 / 10 = 4.8$ h. It says that the grain gets to 58 °C
  in **14 hours**.
- **The conduction model** says that the centre gets to 58 °C after **34 hours**. After six hours, the surface is at
  50 °C, the average is at 40 °C and the centre is still at 29 °C. The lumped model gives 49 °C.

For this reason, conditioning is specified in days, not hours. If a motor is fired before the heat has gone through
it, its temperature is not known. It also has the thermal strains of a grain that is hot outside and cold inside.

### 3: Soak-back into a seal behind a boss

A seal is behind a 20 mm steel polar boss, with 6 mm of EPDM for protection. During a 20 s burn, the hot face of the
insulation is kept at 1200 °C (an effective surface temperature, as on the insulation page). After the burn, it cools
to 20 °C air at $h = 10$ W/m²K. Use the **Soak-back into a boss** preset.

- **At burnout,** the temperature of the back face of the boss has almost not changed: 20.1 °C. The heat has not
  arrived yet.
- **The peak** comes approximately **8 minutes after burnout,** at **62 °C**.
- **Behind a thin wall.** The same insulation over the 1.4 mm case wall has a peak of **225 °C**, less than four
  minutes after burnout. The heat capacity of the boss absorbs the heat. The thin wall has almost no heat capacity.

This model has no charring, pyrolysis or erosion, so it tells nothing about the liner. Use the
[insulation calculator](../insulation/#calculator) for the liner. The model shows how important the part *behind* the
liner is.

## Where this stops

- **Constant properties.** Conductivity and specific heat change with temperature, and polymers decompose.
- **Perfect contact.** Real joints between layers have a contact resistance. At bolted and pressed interfaces, this
  resistance can be the largest.
- **One dimension.** Corners, bosses, fins and fasteners spread heat in two or three dimensions. Use a finite-element
  model for these.
- **Film coefficients** are seldom known to better than a factor of two without test data. Add radiation where it is
  important.
- **No internal heat generation:** no reaction and no electrical heating.

## On the drawing and in the specifications

- **Give each temperature limit with its duration.** The maximum temperature of a seal compound for minutes is
  different from its maximum for years.
- **Specify the conditioning fully:** the temperature, the tolerance, the minimum soak time, and where to measure the
  temperature. Measure it on the grain or in a representative dummy, not on the case.
- **Put thermocouples where the peaks are** (behind the liners, at the seals and at the bondlines), and continue to
  record after burnout. The important peak can be ten minutes later.
