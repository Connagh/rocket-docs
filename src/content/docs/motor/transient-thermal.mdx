## Why this matters

Seals, bondlines, adhesives, electronics and heat-treated alloys all have temperature limits, and most hardware
never reaches steady state — it heats and cools in transients. Three things go wrong again and again:

- **The structure is often hottest after the motor stops.** When the gas stops flowing, the heat stored in the hot
  parts keeps moving inward, because that is the only direction left. That **soak-back** peaks minutes after burnout
  and cooks the seals, bonds and electronics that survived the burn itself.
- **The outside is not the inside.** A thin steel case follows the weather within half an hour. The propellant inside
  takes days, so a thermocouple on the case says very little about the grain — and MEOP depends on the grain.
- **Fast heating makes gradients.** When heat arrives faster than it can spread, the surface and the core can be
  hundreds of degrees apart, with the thermal stresses and strains that go with it.

## Lumped capacitance — one temperature

If the whole part is at one temperature, an energy balance gives

$$
\rho c\,V\,\frac{dT}{dt} = h A\,(T_\infty - T)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\frac{T - T_\infty}{T_0 - T_\infty} = e^{-t/\tau},
\qquad
\tau = \frac{\rho c\,V}{h A} = \frac{\rho c\,L_c}{h}
$$

with $L_c = V/A$ the **characteristic length** — the thickness of a plate heated on one face, $R/2$ for a long
cylinder, $R/3$ for a sphere.

- **It never quite arrives.** 63% of the gap closes in one $\tau$, 95% in three, 99.3% in five. Ask instead how long
  it takes to reach a stated limit: $t = \tau \ln\!\left[(T_\infty - T_0)/(T_\infty - T_\text{lim})\right]$.
- **Conductivity does not appear.** In the lumped regime the inside already keeps up; the bottleneck is entirely at the
  surface.
- **$h$ is usually the weakest input.** Typical film coefficients run from 2–25 W/m²K for free convection in air,
  25–250 for forced air, and up to tens of thousands for liquids and boiling. Combustion gas inside a motor reaches
  thousands, highest at the nozzle throat (Bartz's correlation is the usual estimate). Radiation adds an equivalent
  $h_r = \varepsilon\sigma\,(T_s^2 + T_\infty^2)(T_s + T_\infty)$.

## When one temperature is not enough — the Biot number

$$
Bi = \frac{h\,L_c}{k}
$$

compares the resistance to heat spreading inside ($L_c/k$) with the resistance to heat getting in ($1/h$). Below about
0.1 the inside keeps up and the lumped model is fair. Above it the part has a real gradient — and every lumped number
becomes a fiction.

  potato is soft on the outside and hard in the middle for many minutes: the water delivers heat faster than the potato
  can carry it inward. The pea has a small Biot number; the potato a large one.
</Analogy>

## Conduction through slabs, cylinders and spheres

When the Biot number is not small, solve the heat equation inside the part:

$$
\frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = \alpha\,\frac{1}{r^m}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\!\left(r^m\,\frac{\partial T}{\partial r}\right),
\qquad
\alpha = \frac{k}{\rho c}
$$

with $m = 0$ for a slab, 1 for a cylinder and 2 for a sphere. The **thermal diffusivity** $\alpha$ sets the time
scale: heat penetrates about $\sqrt{\alpha t}$ in time $t$, so a part of thickness $L$ takes of the order of
$L^2/\alpha$ to heat through.

| Part | $\alpha$, m²/s | $L^2/\alpha$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Aluminium, 10 mm | $6.2 \times 10^{-5}$ | 1.6 s |
| Steel, 10 mm | $1.2 \times 10^{-5}$ | 8 s |
| Titanium alloy, 10 mm | $2.8 \times 10^{-6}$ | 35 s |
| Carbon phenolic, 10 mm | $3.7 \times 10^{-7}$ | 5 min |
| EPDM insulation, 6 mm | $1.1 \times 10^{-7}$ | 5 min |
| Propellant, 150 mm | $1.7 \times 10^{-7}$ | 36 h |

The calculator solves this equation numerically — an implicit finite-volume scheme, stable at any time step — for one
or two layers. Its automated tests check it against the exact series solutions for all three shapes, to within 0.2%.

## Soak-back

During a firing, the insulation and liners take the heat and store it. At burnout the source disappears, but the
stored heat does not: it flows down the temperature gradient — inward, into the structure. The back face keeps
heating long after the gas has gone, and peaks minutes later.

<Analogy title="A pan handle off the hob">
  Take a cast-iron pan off the hob and the handle keeps getting hotter for a few minutes. The flame has gone, but the
  heat already stored in the pan is still spreading out along it. Motor hardware does the same after burnout.
</Analogy>

What decides the peak:

- **How much heat is stored near the surface** — the thicker and hotter the heated layer, the more there is to release.
- **What is behind it** — a thick metal part soaks the heat up and peaks lower and later; a thin wall peaks high and
  sooner.
- **What happens at the hot face after burnout** — if it can radiate or convect heat away, less goes inward. An
  insulated hot face is the upper bound; the [insulation page](../insulation/) brackets the two.

## Worked examples

### 1 — A thin case follows the weather

The 1.377 mm steel case from [case sizing](../case-sizing/#worked-example), on its own, in still air
($h \approx 8$ W/m²K) when the air warms from 20 °C to 40 °C. The **Thin case, weather** preset.

- $Bi = 8 \times 0.001377 / 45 = 2.5 \times 10^{-4}$ — about as lumped as anything gets.
- $\tau = 7850 \times 480 \times 0.001377 / 8 = 649$ s, about **11 minutes**.
- Within 1 K of the air (95% of the step): $3\tau \approx$ **33 minutes**.

### 2 — Conditioning a grain

Before a test firing, a motor is held at its hot-limit temperature, say 60 °C, so the firing represents the hot-day
MEOP. Take the 300 mm grain as a solid cylinder of propellant ($k = 0.4$ W/m·K, $\rho = 1770$ kg/m³,
$c = 1300$ J/kg·K — illustrative), starting at 20 °C in a conditioning chamber with forced air at 60 °C
($h \approx 10$ W/m²K). The **Conditioning a grain** preset.

- $Bi = 10 \times 0.075 / 0.4 = 1.9$ — far from lumped.
- **The lumped model** says $\tau = 1770 \times 1300 \times 0.075 / 10 = 4.8$ h, and that the grain reaches 58 °C
  in **14 hours**.
- **Conduction** says the centre reaches 58 °C after **34 hours**. Six hours in, the surface is at 50 °C, the
  average at 40 °C and the centre still at 29 °C — while the lumped model claims 49 °C.

Conditioning is specified in days, not hours, for this reason. A motor fired before it has soaked through is fired at
an unknown temperature — and with the thermal strains of a grain that is hot outside and cold inside.

### 3 — Soak-back into a seal behind a boss

A seal sits behind a 20 mm steel polar boss, protected by 6 mm of EPDM. During a 20 s burn the insulation's hot face
is held at 1200 °C (an effective surface temperature, as on the insulation page). Afterwards it cools to 20 °C air at
$h = 10$ W/m²K. The **Soak-back into a boss** preset.

- **At burnout** the back face of the boss has barely moved: 20.1 °C. Nothing has arrived yet.
- **The peak** comes about **8 minutes after burnout**, at **62 °C**.
- **Behind a thin wall instead.** The same insulation over the 1.4 mm case wall peaks at **225 °C**, less than four
  minutes after burnout. The boss's heat capacity soaks the heat up; the thin wall has none to spare.

This model has no charring, pyrolysis or erosion, so it says nothing about the liner itself — use the
[insulation calculator](../insulation/#calculator) for that. What it shows is how much the part *behind* the liner
matters.

## Where this stops

- **Constant properties.** Conductivity and specific heat change with temperature, and polymers decompose.
- **Perfect contact.** Real joints between layers have a contact resistance, which can dominate at bolted and pressed
  interfaces.
- **One dimension.** Corners, bosses, fins and fasteners spread heat in two or three dimensions; a finite-element model
  handles that.
- **Film coefficients** are rarely known better than a factor of two without test data, and radiation must be added
  where it matters.
- **No internal heat generation** — no reaction, no electrical heating.

## On the drawing and in the specifications

- **State every temperature limit with its duration** — a seal compound's maximum for minutes is different from its
  maximum for years.
- **Specify conditioning properly:** temperature, tolerance, minimum soak time, and where the temperature is measured
  — on the grain or in a representative dummy, not on the case.
- **Place thermocouples where the peaks are** — behind the liners, at the seals and bondlines — and keep recording
  after burnout. The peak you care about may be ten minutes later.
