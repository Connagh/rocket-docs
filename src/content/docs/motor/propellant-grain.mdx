## Why this matters

The grain is the motor's fuel tank, combustion chamber wall and throttle all at once. Its **geometry** fixes how much
burning surface is exposed at every instant, and therefore chamber pressure and thrust for the whole burn. It also
fixes how much propellant fits in the case and how high the peak pressure is — which is what the case has to be sized
for.

Its **structural integrity** is a safety issue, not a performance one. A crack is new burning surface. More surface
means more gas, higher pressure, and — because pressure amplifies changes by $1/(1-n)$ — potentially a case failure.
That is why cold-soak cracking and debonding are taken so seriously.

This page has two halves: [geometry and burn-back](#part-1--geometry-and-burn-back), then
[structural integrity](#part-2--structural-integrity).

## Part 1 — Geometry and burn-back

### The burning surface moves like a grass fire

Over most of a burn, every exposed point of propellant recedes **perpendicular to the local surface** at the same
burn rate $r$. After burning a distance $y$ (the **web burned**), the surface is simply every point that is a distance
$y$ from the original surface.

<Analogy title="A grass fire spreading from a burning outline">
  Light the edge of a field along an irregular line. With no wind, the fire front spreads into the grass at the same
  speed everywhere. Bumps that point into the grass widen into smooth arcs; narrow tongues of grass between two fronts
  burn from both sides and vanish fastest. Draw the front every minute and you get contour lines — which is exactly how
  the burn-back map in the simulator below is coloured.
</Analogy>

Two consequences follow directly:

- **Port corners that point into the propellant** (star tips, slot ends) round off into arcs as they burn.
- **Propellant ridges that point into the port** (between star points) stay sharp and recede quickly, because they burn
  from both sides. When those ridges burn out, burning area drops sharply.

### Words you need

| Term | Meaning | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Web, $w$ | Distance the flame burns before first reaching the case | Sets burn time: $t_b \approx w / \bar r$ |
| Web fraction | $w$ divided by the grain outer radius | High web fraction = long burn, more propellant, but more structural strain |
| Volumetric loading | Propellant volume ÷ volume available inside the case | How much of the case you actually filled with propellant |
| Sliver | Propellant left when the flame first touches the case | Burns in a low-pressure tail-off, contributes little useful thrust |
| $K_n$ | Burning area ÷ throat area | Sets chamber pressure |
| Port ÷ throat area | Flow area down the bore relative to the throat | Too small means fast bore flow and erosive burning |

### Shape sets the thrust curve

<GrainProfiles />

- **Tube (internal-burning cylinder).** The port only gets bigger, so burning area and thrust rise all the way:
  **progressive**. Simple and robust, but the peak pressure at the end of the burn sizes the case.
- **Star.** The points' area shrinks while the circular region grows, which can be balanced to give near-**neutral**
  thrust. The price is a **sliver** left behind the star valleys, unless the design adds fillets or sliver-filling
  inserts.
- **Core plus radial slots (wagon wheel, and the slotted section of a finocyl).** Very large initial burning area for a
  high-thrust boost, usually **regressive** as the slots open up.
- **End burner.** Burns like a cigarette, face-on: perfectly neutral, the longest burn time and the most propellant for
  the case, but low thrust — and the case wall behind the face sees hot gas for the whole burn.
- **BATES (stacked short tubes, ends burning).** The bore grows while the ends shrink inward, roughly cancelling.

That BATES balance is a good example of the geometry reasoning. For a segment of outer diameter $D$, port $d$ and
length $L$, with both ends burning, the burning area after web $y$ is:

$$
A_b(y) = \underbrace{\pi (d + 2y)(L - 2y)}_{\text{bore}} + \underbrace{2 \cdot \frac{\pi}{4}\left(D^2 - (d+2y)^2\right)}_{\text{two ends}}
$$

Setting the area at ignition equal to the area at burnout ($d + 2y = D$) gives the classic sizing rule:

$$
L = \frac{3D + d}{2}
$$

With that length the trace starts and ends at the same level, with a gentle hump in between.

### From burning area to thrust

Combine the burning area with the pressure balance from [the fundamentals page](../../fundamentals/how-a-solid-motor-works/#why-chamber-pressure-settles-at-one-value).
At every web position $y$:

$$
p_c(y) = \left(\rho_p\,a\,c^*\,\frac{A_b(y)}{A_t}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-n}}
\qquad
\frac{dt}{dy} = \frac{1}{a\,p_c^{\,n}}
\qquad
F = C_F\,p_c\,A_t
$$

March through the web, accumulate time, and you have the pressure and thrust traces. One useful check falls out:
because mass flow through the throat is $p_c A_t / c^*$, the total impulse is

$$
I_t = \int F\,dt = C_F\, c^*\, m_p
$$

whatever the grain shape. Geometry changes *when* the impulse is delivered, not how much there is.

### Simulator

The simulator finds the burn-back by computing, for every point in the cross-section, its distance to the original
port outline — the grass-fire rule, done numerically. Try the presets, then change one dimension at a time.

<BurnbackSim />

Things to try:

- **Tube → star.** Same propellant, same case: the star flattens the thrust curve but leaves a sliver. Watch the burn
  map — the sliver is the propellant left between the star valleys and the case when the flame first touches the wall.
- **Make the star points deeper** (larger tip diameter) and watch the profile tip from neutral into regressive.
- **BATES:** change the segment length away from $(3D+d)/2$ and see the trace tilt.
- **Shrink the tube port** to 50 mm and watch the port-to-throat warning appear.

### Worked example — tube grain by hand

Grain outer diameter 150 mm, port 80 mm, length 600 mm, ends inhibited. Propellant as the defaults:
$a = 4.05$ mm/s at 1 MPa, $n = 0.35$, $\rho_p = 1770$ kg/m³, $c^* = 1540$ m/s, $C_F = 1.5$. Throat sized so peak
pressure is 7 MPa.

1. **Burning area.** Start: $\pi \times 80 \times 600 = 150{,}800$ mm². End (port reaches 150 mm):
   $\pi \times 150 \times 600 = 282{,}700$ mm². Ratio 1.875 — **progressive**.
2. **Throat.** Peak pressure is at the end. $K_n = 7^{0.65} \times 10^9 / (1770 \times 4.05 \times 1540) = 320.9$,
   so $A_t = 282{,}700 / 320.9 = 881$ mm² and $D_t = 33.5$ mm.
3. **Starting pressure.** $p \propto A_b^{1/(1-n)}$, so $p_0 = 7 \times (80/150)^{1.538} = 2.66$ MPa. A 1.9× swing in
   area becomes a 2.6× swing in pressure.
4. **Burn time.** Burn rate at the end is $4.05 \times 7^{0.35} = 8.00$ mm/s, and $r \propto (\text{port radius})^{n/(1-n)}$.
   Integrating $dt = dy/r$ from a port radius of 40 mm to 75 mm gives $t_b = 5.11$ s.
5. **Mass and impulse.** $m_p = \frac{\pi}{4}(150^2 - 80^2) \times 600 \times 1770 \times 10^{-9} = 13.4$ kg, and
   $I_t = 1.5 \times 1540 \times 13.4 = 31.0$ kN·s, so the average thrust is about 6.1 kN.

The simulator's tube preset reproduces these numbers to within about 1%. The automated tests check both.

<Aside type="caution" title="Where the burn-back model stops">
  Real motors add effects this model leaves out: **erosive burning** (fast gas along the bore raises the local burn
  rate, which is why a low port-to-throat ratio gives an ignition spike), the **ignition transient**, **throat
  erosion** (pressure sags during the burn), the **burn-rate scale factor and "hump"** between small test motors and
  full-scale grains, **3-D geometry** (finocyl fins, conical ports, domed ends), and grain **deformation** under
  pressure changing the port. Production ballistics use full internal-ballistics codes anchored to motor firings.
</Aside>

## Part 2 — Structural integrity

### The material: a filled rubber with a memory

Composite propellant is around 85–90% solid particles (oxidiser and aluminium) held in a rubber binder. Mechanically it
behaves like a **viscoelastic** solid: its stiffness and strength depend on **how fast** it is loaded and **how cold**
it is. The models behind this are on the [viscoelasticity page](../../fundamentals/viscoelasticity/).

<Analogy title="Silly Putty">
  Pull Silly Putty slowly and it stretches a long way. Yank it and it snaps. Put it in the freezer and even a slow pull
  snaps it. Propellant follows the same pattern — and cold behaves like fast. That equivalence (time–temperature
  superposition) is what lets a few lab tests describe years of storage and milliseconds of ignition.
</Analogy>

Three facts drive everything below:

1. **Propellant is nearly incompressible.** It changes shape easily but barely changes volume (bulk modulus is
   thousands of times its shear modulus). Wherever its volume *wants* to change, the geometry decides where the
   movement goes.
2. **Relaxation lowers stress, not strain.** In a case-bonded grain the bore strain is set by geometry and expansion
   coefficients, not by stiffness. Slow cool-downs relax the *stress* away, but the *strain* stays.
3. **Capability depends on rate, temperature, pressure and age.** There is no single "strain to failure". Each load case
   is compared with the capability measured at its own rate and temperature, and capability falls as the propellant
   ages.

### The load cases

| Load case | What happens | Typically governs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cure cool-down and cold storage | Propellant tries to shrink far more than the case it is bonded to | Bore cracking, bond tension |
| Temperature cycling | Repeated cool-down strains | Cumulative damage over the service life |
| Ignition pressurisation | Case dilates; incompressible grain amplifies it at the bore | Bore cracking at cold firing |
| Flight acceleration, vibration | Inertial loads on a soft grain | Bond shear at the ends, slender grains |
| Long-term storage (slump) | The grain sags under its own weight for years | Large motors, bore deformation |

### Cool-down: why the bore cracks

Propellant is cured warm and bonded to the case at that temperature, where it is stress-free. Its thermal expansion
coefficient is around $10^{-4}$/K — roughly eight times steel's — so when it cools it tries to shrink far more than
the case does. The outside is glued to the case and can't follow. Because it can't change volume either, the shrinkage
has to come out of the bore, which is pulled open.

<GrainCooldown />

<Analogy title="A ring doughnut glued inside a steel bangle, put in the freezer">
  The doughnut wants to shrink, but its outer edge is stuck to the bangle, which barely shrinks at all. The doughnut
  can't get smaller from the outside, so its hole gets bigger instead — stretching the inside surface around its
  circumference. The thicker the doughnut relative to its hole, the more volume there is to accommodate, and the more
  that small hole has to stretch.
</Analogy>

For a long, case-bonded cylindrical grain with bore radius $a$ and outer radius $b$, write $m = b/a$. Incompressibility
means the area of each ring of propellant can't change except by thermal expansion, which fixes the displacement field.
Applying the case boundary condition gives the bore hoop strain on cooling by $\Delta T$ (negative for cooling):

$$
\varepsilon_{\theta}(a) = \Delta T \left[\frac{3\alpha_p - \alpha_c}{2}\,(1 - m^2) + m^2\,\alpha_c\right]
\;\approx\; \tfrac{3}{2}\,(\alpha_p - \alpha_c)\,|\Delta T|\,(m^2 - 1)
$$

The approximate form is within $\alpha_c |\Delta T|$ (about 0.1%) of the full one. The key term is $m^2$: **bore strain
grows with the square of the outer-to-bore ratio**. A grain with a small bore and a thick web — which is exactly what
you want for propellant loading — is the hardest to keep from cracking. That is the central structural trade in grain
design.

The same analysis gives the radial tension pulling the grain off the case at the bond, using the propellant's relaxed
modulus $E$ for this slow load:

$$
\sigma_r(b) = E\,(m^2 - 1)\,(\alpha_p - \alpha_c)\,|\Delta T| \;\approx\; \tfrac{2}{3}\,E\,\varepsilon_\theta(a)
$$

A soft propellant keeps bond stress low; it does nothing for bore strain.

### Ignition: the case stretches, the bore stretches more

At ignition, pressure pushes the grain against the case and the case dilates by a fraction of a percent. The
propellant can't change volume much, so that small outward movement at radius $b$ has to be matched by a much larger
movement of the bore:

$$
\varepsilon_{\theta}(a) = m^2\,\varepsilon_{\theta,\text{case}} + \frac{m^2 - 1}{2}\left(\varepsilon_{z,\text{case}} + \frac{p}{K}\right)
$$

where $K$ is the propellant bulk modulus, and for a thin closed-end case of modulus $E_c$, Poisson's ratio $\nu_c$ and
wall $h$:

$$
\varepsilon_{\theta,\text{case}} = \frac{p\,b}{E_c\,h}\left(1 - \frac{\nu_c}{2}\right)
\qquad
\varepsilon_{z,\text{case}} = \frac{p\,b}{E_c\,h}\left(\frac{1}{2} - \nu_c\right)
$$

This links the grain directly to [case sizing](../case-sizing/): a lighter, more highly stressed case stretches more,
and the grain pays for it at the bore. The worst case is usually a **cold firing**, where the cool-down strain is
already there when ignition adds its share.

### Worked example — HTPB grain in the D6AC case

The same 300 mm D6AC case as the [case-sizing worked example](../case-sizing/#worked-example) (wall 1.377 mm, MEOP
10 MPa), with a 100 mm circular bore ($m = 3$). Composite propellant with $\alpha_p = 100 \times 10^{-6}$/K, relaxed
modulus 3.6 MPa and bulk modulus 3.5 GPa; steel $\alpha_c = 12 \times 10^{-6}$/K. Stress-free at 60 °C, coldest firing
at −40 °C, so $\Delta T = -100$ K. The safety factor of 1.5 below is for this example only; press **Load worked
example** in the structural calculator to load these inputs.

1. **Cool-down strain.** $100 \times \left[\frac{300 - 12}{2} \times 8 - 9 \times 12\right] \times 10^{-6} = 10.4\%$.
2. **Case strain at MEOP.** $\frac{p b}{E_c h} = \frac{10 \times 150}{205{,}000 \times 1.377} = 0.531\%$, so hoop
   $0.452\%$ and axial $0.106\%$.
3. **Ignition strain.** $9 \times 0.452\% + 4 \times (0.106\% + 10/3500) = 4.07\% + 1.57\% = 5.6\%$.
4. **Cold ignition.** $10.4\% + 5.6\% = 16.1\%$. Against the illustrative 39.6% capability with a safety factor of 1.5:
   margin $= 39.6 / (1.5 \times 16.1) - 1 = +0.64$.
5. **Bond tension.** $3.6 \times 8 \times 88 \times 10^{-6} \times 100 = 0.25$ MPa.

Now set the **concentration factor to 2.5**, roughly what a star point might add. The cold-ignition strain becomes 40%
and the margin goes negative. The same grain that is comfortable with a circular bore fails with a star — which is why
star points get generous fillet radii and their own detailed analysis.

### Design levers

- **Larger bore / smaller web** — the most powerful lever, because strain goes as $m^2$. It costs propellant loading.
- **Stiffer or thicker case** — lowers ignition strain directly. Composite cases are light but stretch more.
- **Raise the minimum firing temperature** — every degree of $\Delta T$ removed is strain removed. Often a system-level
  negotiation.
- **Lower the stress-free temperature** — curing cooler, or curing under pressure, reduces the cool-down $\Delta T$.
- **Stress-relief flaps and boots** at the grain ends, where the bond terminates and stresses are far higher than the
  long-cylinder values above.
- **Cartridge-loaded grains** (made separately and slid into the case) avoid the bonded cool-down problem entirely,
  at the cost of support and inhibition complexity. Common for double-base grains, which go glassy at low temperature.

### Where these formulas stop

<Aside type="caution" title="Screening, not qualification">
  These are long-cylinder, elastic, closed-form results. Real grain structural analysis uses viscoelastic finite
  elements with measured relaxation data, covers the grain ends and every geometric feature, accumulates damage over
  the full thermal and handling history, accounts for ageing, and is validated against instrumented motors and
  analogue tests.
</Aside>

- **Ends and terminations.** Bond stresses at the grain ends can be several times the values above.
- **Non-circular ports.** Star points, slot tips and fin roots need FEA. The concentration factor here is a placeholder.
- **Dewetting.** At high strains the binder separates from the solid particles and voids open up. The material is then
  no longer incompressible, and its response changes.
- **Cumulative damage.** Repeated thermal cycles each use up some capability. Programmes track this with cumulative
  damage models, and through surveillance testing of aged motors.

### Differences from REF·DB v1

<Aside type="note" title="Two corrections carried into this version">
  **Pressurisation strain.** v1 used $\tfrac{p}{2G}\,\tfrac{b^2}{b^2 - a^2}$, which is the bore strain of a grain
  with *no case around it*. For a soft composite propellant that predicts strains of hundreds of percent. In a
  case-bonded grain, the case controls the bore strain — the formula above.

  **Relaxation credit.** v1 reduced the cool-down bore strain by a viscoelastic relaxation factor. The bore strain in a
  case-bonded grain is kinematically imposed, so relaxation lowers the stress but not the strain. Relaxation is now
  applied only where it belongs: the bond stress, through the relaxed modulus.
</Aside>

## On the drawing

- **Grain geometry** — port profile, fillet radii, web, and the tolerances on each. Fillet radii at star points and slot
  tips are structural features, not cosmetic ones.
- **Liner, insulation and inhibitor** — specifications, thicknesses, and which surfaces are inhibited.
- **Bond and void acceptance** — the NDT method (typically radiography or CT for voids, cracks and debonds) and its
  acceptance criteria.
- **Stress-relief features** — flap geometry and the release treatment that lets them separate.
- **Temperature limits** — storage and firing temperatures belong in the motor specification, because the structural
  margin depends on them.
