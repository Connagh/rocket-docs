<Aside type="note" title="What this page covers — and what it does not">
  Propellant here is a material with properties: how it burns, how stiff and strong it is, how it ages, and what it
  may touch. There are no formulations, proportions, processing or burn-rate tailoring. Those belong to your
  energetics and propulsion specialists and their safety authority.
</Aside>

## Why this matters

Two of the numbers that size motor hardware come from the propellant: the **MEOP** that the case, joints and proof
test are designed to, and the **strain capability** that decides whether the bore cracks on a cold night. Both move
with temperature far more than any metal property does. Between −40 °C and +60 °C, the illustrative propellant on this
page changes its chamber pressure by about 22% and its stiffness by more than ten times, depending on how quickly it
is loaded.

The hardware designer does not pick these numbers. But you do need to know where they come from, what they depend on,
and exactly which ones to ask for.

## Part 1 — Ballistic properties

### The numbers you inherit

| Property | Symbol | What it means | Where you meet it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Burn-rate coefficient | $a$ | Burn rate at unit pressure, at a reference temperature | The pressure balance ([primer](../../fundamentals/how-a-solid-motor-works/#why-chamber-pressure-settles-at-one-value)) |
| Pressure exponent | $n$ | How strongly the burn rate rises with pressure: $r = a\,p^n$ | Amplifies every variation by $1/(1-n)$ |
| Temperature sensitivity of burn rate | $\sigma_p$ | % change in burn rate per kelvin of grain temperature, at constant pressure | Hot-day MEOP — below |
| Temperature sensitivity of pressure | $\pi_K$ | % change in chamber pressure per kelvin, at constant $K_n$ | MEOP and burn time — below |
| Density, characteristic velocity | $\rho_p$, $c^*$ | Mass per volume; gas made per unit of throat flow | Pressure, thrust, mass fraction |

### Temperature: why the hot day sets MEOP

A propellant grain that starts warm burns faster. Over a firing-temperature range the effect is well described by an
exponential:

$$
a(T) = a_0\,e^{\,\sigma_p (T - T_0)}
$$

The [pressure balance](../../fundamentals/how-a-solid-motor-works/#why-chamber-pressure-settles-at-one-value) gives
$p_c \propto a^{1/(1-n)}$, so at constant $K_n$ the chamber pressure follows the same law, amplified:

$$
p_c(T) = p_c(T_0)\,e^{\,\pi_K (T - T_0)},
\qquad
\pi_K = \frac{\sigma_p}{1 - n}
$$

The burn rate at constant $K_n$ rises by the same factor, so **burn time falls** as $e^{-\pi_K (T-T_0)}$. A hot motor
pushes harder for less time. Total impulse barely changes — the same propellant is burned with nearly the same $c^*$ —
so the hot and cold pressure traces enclose almost the same area.

<Analogy title="Kindling from a sunny windowsill">
  Kindling that has been sitting in the sun catches and burns faster than kindling from a cold shed. The flame spends
  less of its heat bringing the fuel up to burning temperature, so it moves on sooner. Warm propellant is the same.
  In a motor there is a second step: faster burning raises the pressure, which raises the burn rate again — the
  $1/(1-n)$ amplification — until the throat catches up.
</Analogy>

### Building MEOP

MEOP is the nominal pressure carried up to the worst condition the motor will be fired in, plus the manufacturing
spread:

1. **Hot day.** The nominal peak pressure at the reference temperature, taken to the hottest firing temperature with
   $\pi_K$.
2. **Manufacturing variation**, each amplified by $1/(1-n)$:
   - **burn rate** — the batch-to-batch scatter in $a$;
   - **burning area** — grain dimensions and bore tolerances;
   - **throat area** — a smaller throat means a higher pressure.
3. **Combining them.** Independent variations are usually combined root-sum-square (RSS); a worst-case stack puts
   every one at its limit at once and is more conservative. Which method applies, and at what probability level, is
   set by your programme — not by this page.
4. **Transients from firings** — ignition spike, erosive burning early in the burn on long, narrow bores, and grain
   deformation under pressure. These come from test data and are added on top.

<Aside type="caution" title="Ask for the build-up, not just the number">
  A MEOP without its temperature range means little. Ask your ballistics team for the build-up: the hottest firing
  temperature, the variations included, the combination method, and whether transients are in. If the operating
  temperature range grows later in the programme, MEOP grows with it — and so does the case wall.
</Aside>

### Worked example — building the 10 MPa MEOP

Illustrative AP/HTPB-like values: nominal peak pressure 8.8 MPa at 20 °C, $n = 0.35$, $\sigma_p = 0.13$ %/K. Firing
range −40 °C to +60 °C. Variations: burn rate ±3%, burning area ±1%, throat area ±1%. These are the calculator's
starting values.

1. **Sensitivity.** $\pi_K = 0.0013 / 0.65 = 0.0020$ /K (0.20 %/K, within the 0.1–0.3 %/K the primer quotes).
   Amplification $1/(1-n) = 1.54$.
2. **Hot day.** $8.8 \times e^{\,0.0020 \times 40} = 8.8 \times 1.083 = 9.53$ MPa.
3. **Variations.** Burn rate $1.03^{1.54} - 1 = 4.65\%$; burning area $1.01^{1.54} - 1 = 1.54\%$; throat area
   $(1/0.99)^{1.54} - 1 = 1.56\%$.
4. **Combined (RSS).** $\sqrt{4.65^2 + 1.54^2 + 1.56^2} = 5.14\%$, so MEOP $= 9.53 \times 1.0514 = 10.02$ MPa — the
   10 MPa the [case-sizing example](../case-sizing/#worked-example) was designed to.
5. **Worst-case stack.** $1.0465 \times 1.0154 \times 1.0156 - 1 = 7.92\%$, giving 10.29 MPa — 2.6% higher. Which one
   applies is your programme's decision.
6. **The cold end.** $8.8 \times e^{-0.0020 \times 60} = 7.80$ MPa nominal, 7.41 MPa on the low side. The cold motor
   burns 12.7% longer and the hot one 7.7% shorter. Minimum pressure matters too: it sets the lowest thrust and must
   stay above the pressure the propellant needs to burn steadily — another number for your ballistics team.

Of the 1.22 MPa between nominal and MEOP, the hot day is 0.73 MPa — 60%. Temperature, not manufacturing, is usually
the biggest single term.

## Part 2 — Mechanical properties

### Stiffness depends on the clock and the thermometer

Composite propellant is a highly filled rubber, and like [Silly Putty](../propellant-grain/#the-material-a-filled-rubber-with-a-memory)
its stiffness depends on how fast it is loaded and how cold it is. **Cold behaves like fast.** That equivalence —
time–temperature superposition — means one set of laboratory tests can describe both years of storage and milliseconds
of ignition.

The link between the two is the **shift factor** $a_T$: how many times slower every relaxation process runs at
temperature $T$ than at a reference temperature $T_s$. For many polymers, propellant binders included, it follows the
Williams–Landel–Ferry (WLF) equation:

$$
\log_{10} a_T = \frac{-C_1\,(T - T_s)}{C_2 + T - T_s}
\qquad\qquad
\xi = \frac{t}{a_T}
$$

A load applied for time $t$ at temperature $T$ acts like a load applied for the **reduced time** $\xi$ at $T_s$. Read
the stiffness for that $\xi$ off the propellant's **master curve** — its relaxation modulus measured over many decades
of time, shifted onto one temperature.

### What the explorer shows

With the illustrative constants ($T_s = -20$ °C, the general WLF constants, a power-law master curve):

- **Cool-down to −40 °C over 8 hours.** $a_T \approx 148$, so 8 hours at −40 °C acts like 194 s at −20 °C. The
  propellant is almost fully relaxed: $E \approx 4.0$ MPa.
- **Ignition at −40 °C**, pressurising in about 50 ms. The same $a_T$ turns 50 ms into 0.34 ms. $E \approx 58$ MPa —
  **14 times stiffer**, in the same grain at the same temperature.
- **Storage for 10 years at 20 °C** is far out on the relaxed end of the curve: about 3 MPa, the equilibrium value.

Three consequences for hardware:

1. **Thermal strain analyses use the relaxed modulus**; ignition analyses use the stiff, short-time modulus. The
   [grain page](../propellant-grain/) does exactly this.
2. **Bond stress at a cold ignition** is high, because the grain is stiff just when the case stretches.
3. **Capability must be measured at the rate and temperature of each load case** — there is no single strength.

### Strength and strain capability

Strength is measured on dog-bone tensile specimens across a matrix of temperatures and strain rates. Each test gives
the **maximum stress** $\sigma_m$, the **strain at maximum stress** $\varepsilon_m$ and the **strain at break**
$\varepsilon_b$. Design margins usually use $\varepsilon_m$ or $\varepsilon_b$, depending on the programme.

- **Dewetting.** At higher strains the binder pulls away from the filler particles, small voids open, the material's
  volume grows and the stress–strain curve bends over. That is where $\varepsilon_m$ comes from.
- **Pressure helps.** A superimposed pressure — as at ignition — suppresses dewetting and raises strain capability.
  Capability measured at atmospheric pressure is therefore conservative for ignition. Programmes either test under
  pressure or accept the conservatism.
- **Biaxial is worse than uniaxial.** The bore surface is strained in two directions at once, and biaxial capability is
  usually lower than uniaxial. It is measured on biaxial specimens or covered by the programme's analysis rules.
- **Rate and temperature collapse onto one curve.** Plotted as stress at break against strain at break, results from
  all rates and temperatures fall on a single **failure envelope** (Smith, 1963) — the strength counterpart of the
  master curve.
- **Damage accumulates.** Each thermal cycle uses up part of the capability. Long-life motors are assessed with
  cumulative-damage methods, not one load case at a time.

### Ageing

Propellant properties keep changing in storage, and the hardware margins have to hold at **end of life**, not on
delivery day.

- **Composite propellants** typically harden: the binder keeps cross-linking, so the modulus rises and strain
  capability falls. Plasticiser can migrate into the liner and insulation, and moisture is absorbed where the oxidiser
  is hygroscopic.
- **Nitrate-ester propellants** also have a chemical clock: the stabiliser is used up over time, which sets a safe life
  separate from the mechanical one.
- **What it means for hardware.** Grain and bondline margins are computed with aged properties. The ageing evidence
  comes from accelerated and real-time ageing and from surveillance motors taken from the stockpile — see
  [Storage & ageing (v1)](../../legacy/?z=ageing).

## Propellant families as engineering materials

The family matters to hardware because stiffness, temperature range, ageing and — above all — compatibility differ
between families.

| Family | Mechanically | Temperature range | Exhaust and performance | Ages mainly by | Hardware watch-points |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Double-base (DB) | Stiff, glassy-polymer-like; one phase | Narrow; brittle when cold | Low smoke; lower $I_{sp}$ than composites | Stabiliser depletion; plasticiser migration | The copper rule; cold cracking |
| Elastomer-modified cast double-base (EMCDB) | Tougher than DB | Wider than DB | Low smoke | As DB, plus elastomer migration | Copper rule in full |
| Composite-modified double-base (CMDB) | Filled; partly composite-like | Moderate | Higher $I_{sp}$ than DB; smoky once metallised | Both families' mechanisms; filler debonding | Both sets of compatibility rules |
| AP composite | Highly filled rubber, viscoelastic | Wide | High $I_{sp}$; visible exhaust when metallised | Binder hardening; migration; bondline ageing; moisture | Bondline integrity; humidity control |
| AN composite | Filled rubber | Limited by oxidiser phase changes on cycling | Lower $I_{sp}$; low signature, chlorine-free | Moisture; phase changes | Moisture ingress and corrosion |

## What may touch it — materials compatibility

A material is not "compatible with propellant" in the abstract. Nitrate-ester and composite propellants degrade by
different mechanisms, so the same metal can be excluded next to one and merely controlled next to the other. **Read
across the row.** Compatibility is shown by test, not asserted from a datasheet: STANAG 4147 is the governing
agreement, using methods such as vacuum stability, heat-flow calorimetry and — for nitrocellulose-based propellants
only — stabiliser depletion.

<Aside type="caution" title="Two rules worth remembering">
  **The copper rule.** Copper and copper alloys are excluded around nitrate-ester (double-base) propellant: no brass
  fittings, bronze bushes, beryllium-copper tools, copper-bearing solder or copper plating — in the hardware, the
  tooling, the handling equipment or the store. It is easy to break through a bought-in part.

  **The silicone rule.** Silicone is banned from most propellant and bond areas — not because it reacts, but because it
  migrates, transfers on contact and destroys adhesive bonds. Bondlines are structure, so a silicone fingerprint can
  fail a motor. That includes release agents, sealants, lubricants and hand creams.
</Aside>

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

| Lead and lead alloys | **Excluded** | Approval | Approval | Often hidden in free-machining brass and steel, and in solders |
| Silver (plating, brazing) | **Excluded** | Approval | Approval | Same class of concern as copper near nitrate esters |
| Zinc and cadmium (plating, galvanising) | **Excluded** | Approval | Approval | Corrosion products at bondlines; cadmium is being phased out anyway |

| Stainless steel | OK | OK | OK | The usual contact choice; passivate after machining |
| Carbon and low-alloy steel | Approval | Approval | Approval | Corrodes; the finish is where incompatibilities hide |
| Titanium | OK | OK | OK | Do not carry over liquid-oxidiser assumptions |

approved list governs either way.

## The data to ask for

| Property | Usually measured by | You use it for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Burn-rate law $a$, $n$, over the pressure range | Strand burners and small ballistic test motors | Pressure balance, $K_n$ |
| Temperature sensitivity $\sigma_p$, $\pi_K$ | The same, at several conditioning temperatures | Hot-day MEOP, burn time |
| Batch-to-batch burn-rate scatter | Acceptance tests on every batch | MEOP build-up |
| Density $\rho_p$, characteristic velocity $c^*$ | Measurement; firing data | Mass, pressure, thrust |
| Thermal expansion $\alpha_p$ | Dilatometry | Cool-down strain ([grain](../propellant-grain/)) |
| Relaxation modulus $E(t, T)$ and shift factors | Stress-relaxation tests across temperatures | Grain and bondline stress |
| Bulk modulus, Poisson's ratio | Dilatation (pressure–volume) tests | Ignition strain; near-incompressibility |
| Strength and strain capability $\sigma_m$, $\varepsilon_m$, $\varepsilon_b$ | Uniaxial, biaxial and pressurised tensile tests across rates and temperatures | Grain margins |
| Bond strength, propellant–liner–insulation | Bond tension, peel and shear specimens | Bondline margin |
| Thermal conductivity, specific heat | Laboratory measurement | Thermal analysis ([Transient thermal, v1](../../legacy/?z=thermal)) |
| Glass transition temperature $T_g$ | Dynamic mechanical analysis, dilatometry | The lowest usable temperature |
| How all of these change with age | Accelerated and real-time ageing; surveillance motors | End-of-life margins |

## Where this stops

- $r = a\,p^n$ is a fit over a pressure range. Some propellants change exponent within their operating range; use the
  law only over the range it was fitted.
- The exponential temperature law is also a fit, valid over the conditioning range it came from.
- The MEOP build-up shows the mechanism. The real derivation follows your programme's statistical method and includes
  transients measured on firings.
- The master curve and its constants are illustrative. Real analyses use measured relaxation data and fitted shift
  factors, and treat dewetting, pressure and ageing separately.
- What is in the propellant and how it is made are out of scope here.

## On the drawing

- **Call the propellant up by its specification or designation**, never by a description of what is in it. Batch
  acceptance — burn rate at reference conditions within a band, density, mechanical properties — belongs to the
  propellant specification, which the motor drawing references.
- **State the operating and storage temperature limits** on the motor drawing or interface document. MEOP and every
  grain margin are valid only inside them.
- **Name every material that may touch propellant** — metal, finish, seal, adhesive, release agent, even temporary
  covers — from the approved list. Never "or equivalent", and never the shop's choice.
- **Define the bondlines** — surface preparation, liner, cure and inspection — as structure. See
  [Insulation](../insulation/#on-the-drawing).
