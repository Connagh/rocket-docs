## Why this matters

A solid motor has few parts: a case with two closures, insulation, the propellant grain, a nozzle, an igniter, and the
joints and seals between them. When a motor fails, the cause is usually one of these hardware parts, or the interface
between two of them. It is usually not the propellant chemistry. The Space Shuttle *Challenger* was lost in January
1986 because the seals of a booster field joint could not follow the joint as it opened under pressure (see
[O-ring design](../../seals/o-ring-design/)). Later that year, a Titan 34D was lost after insulation separated from the
case of one of its solid boosters and let the flame get to the steel.

After the propellant has burned, all of these parts are dead weight. Hardware design must balance these two
requirements: sufficient strength, temperature capability and margin to survive, and no more mass than necessary.

## The number every part answers to: mass fraction

The **propellant mass fraction** of the motor is the fraction of its mass that is propellant:

$$
\zeta = \frac{m_p}{m_p + m_\text{inert}}
$$

The rocket equation tells what this gives. $m_0$ is the mass at ignition and $m_f$ is the mass at burnout:

$$
\Delta v = I_{sp}\,g_0 \ln\frac{m_0}{m_f}
\qquad
m_0 = m_p + m_\text{inert} + m_\text{payload},
\quad
m_f = m_\text{inert} + m_\text{payload}
$$

Here $I_{sp}\,g_0$ is the effective exhaust velocity. For a solid motor, it is $C_F\,c^*$ (see the
[primer](../../fundamentals/how-a-solid-motor-works/#from-pressure-to-thrust)). The inert mass is in the denominator, so
it has two bad effects: the motor pushes it for the full burn, and it is still there at the end. Two results follow
directly:

- **The cost of one inert kilogram.** By differentiation:
  $$
  \frac{\partial\,\Delta v}{\partial m_\text{inert}} = -\,I_{sp}\,g_0\,\frac{m_p}{m_0\,m_f}
  $$
- **The exchange ratio.** To keep the same $\Delta v$ when the inert mass increases by $\delta$, the mass ratio
  $m_0/m_f$ must stay the same. Thus the propellant must increase by $(m_0/m_f - 1)\,\delta$. With a mass ratio of
  2.5, each additional kilogram of hardware needs 1.5 kg more propellant. This needs a longer case, more insulation and
  a larger nozzle. This chain is why it is very difficult to correct an overweight motor late in a design.

<Analogy title="Packing for a long hike">
  Each gram of tent, stove and pack frame is a gram of food that you cannot carry. You also carry the tent all the way
  to the end, long after you have eaten the food. Ultralight hikers do not eat less. They weigh their equipment. A motor
  designer does the same with the case, the insulation and the nozzle.
</Analogy>

Large motors with composite cases get mass fractions of more than 0.9. Small motors have much lower values, because
the igniter, the nozzle throat, the bosses and the seals do not become smaller in proportion to the propellant.

<Aside type="tip" title="A design sequence that works">
  First set the MEOP and the proof and burst factors. Then select the class of case material, size the wall and add the
  insulation. Only then check the remaining mass fraction. If you select the material last, after the geometry is
  frozen, the motor can be overweight with no remaining options.
</Aside>

### Worked example: the 300 mm motor from the other pages

This is the same motor as in the [case-sizing](../case-sizing/#worked-example),
[propellant-grain](../propellant-grain/) and [insulation](../insulation/) examples. These are the starting values of
the calculator.

- **Case:** 11.77 kg. This is the 1.377 mm D6AC cylinder with hemispherical domes, from the case-sizing example.
- **Propellant:** a 300 mm grain with a 100 mm bore, 1000 mm long, in the cylinder only:
  $\frac{\pi}{4}(0.300^2 - 0.100^2) \times 1.000 \times 1770 = 111.2$ kg.
- **Insulation:** 2 mm of EPDM along the cylinder, where the grain covers it until tail-off
  ($0.942\ \text{m}^2 \times 2\ \text{mm} \times 1100 = 2.07$ kg), and the 8.9 mm that the insulation example needed in
  the domes ($0.283\ \text{m}^2 \times 8.9\ \text{mm} \times 1100 = 2.77$ kg). The total is 4.84 kg.
- **Nozzle 6 kg and other hardware 4 kg:** round illustrative values. **Payload** 50 kg.
- **Exhaust velocity** from the illustrative values of the grain page: $C_F\,c^* = 1.5 \times 1540 = 2310$ m/s, which
  is $I_{sp} = 235.55$ s.

1. **Inert mass** $= 11.77 + 4.84 + 6 + 4 = 26.61$ kg, so $\zeta = 111.2 / 137.81 = 0.807$.
2. **Mass ratio:** $m_0 = 187.81$ kg and $m_f = 76.61$ kg, so $m_0/m_f = 2.452$.
3. **Ideal $\Delta v$** $= 2310 \times \ln 2.452 = 2310 \times 0.897 = 2071$ m/s.
4. **Cost of one inert kilogram** $= 2310 \times 111.2 / (187.81 \times 76.61) = 17.9$ m/s, or
   $2.452 - 1 = 1.45$ kg of additional propellant to keep the same $\Delta v$.
5. **The cost of the dome insulation.** The 2.77 kg that the bondline limit needed costs approximately **51 m/s**. The
   insulation example found that the bondline governs, not the char. This is the cost of that result, and the reason to
   examine a case with more heat capacity or a better insulator.

Assume that a composite case could do the work of the case with 6 kg less mass (an assumption, not a design). Then
$\Delta v$ would increase by approximately 113 m/s. Try it in the calculator. Then try the same 6 kg on the nozzle. A
kilogram has the same effect in all locations.

## Then and now: why the materials changed

| Part | Then | Now | Cause of the change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Case | Welded high-strength steel (D6AC, maraging); titanium on some upper stages | Filament-wound carbon/epoxy (aramid/epoxy before it) | Fibre put exactly along the load paths: much higher strength for each unit of mass |
| Insulation | Nitrile rubber filled with asbestos or silica | EPDM filled with aramid or silica | Health (asbestos), lower density, better ageing |
| Throat | Bulk and pyrolytic graphite; tungsten on small motors | Carbon–carbon, often 3-D woven | Toughness under thermal shock, at lower mass |
| Exit cone | Tape-wrapped carbon phenolic and silica phenolic over a metal shell | Thin radiation-cooled carbon–carbon; extendible cones on upper stages | Mass; more expansion ratio in the same stowed length |
| Steering | Jet vanes, jetavators, liquid injection | Movable nozzles on flexible bearings, more and more with electromechanical actuators | No thrust lost in the exhaust; no injectant tank or hydraulics |
| Nozzle housing | Steel | Aluminium, titanium, composite | Mass |

Look at the last column again. Most of these changes came from a **manufacturing process** (filament winding, 3-D
weaving, tape wrapping and laminated bearings) as much as from a new material. When you select a material, ask which
process it makes possible. That is usually where the real improvement is.

## Designing the case to fail gently

If a motor is in a fuel fire, or if a fragment hits it, it can ignite whatever the designer does. The hardware decides
**what occurs next**. If the case continues to hold pressure, the burning propellant pressurises it until it bursts.
This is an explosion. If the case opens early and stays open, the propellant burns at low pressure. This is a fire.
From the hardware side, insensitive-munitions (IM) design makes sure of the second result.

<ImResponse />

A design is assessed against these threats: **fast heating** (a fuel fire around the item), **slow heating** (a slow
increase, for example from a fire in an adjacent store), **bullet impact**, **fragment impact**, **sympathetic
reaction** (a reaction of an adjacent munition) and **shaped-charge jet**. The NATO policy is STANAG 4439. AOP-39 gives
the assessment method and the necessary response for each threat. As with all factors on this site, get these
requirements from the current editions, not from a page such as this one.

What the hardware designer controls:

- **Composite cases usually vent themselves in a fast fire.** The resin becomes soft and the case loses strength
  early.
- **Metal cases need a special feature.** The feature makes the case or a closure weaker, or releases it, at a
  temperature below the temperature where the propellant reacts violently.
- **Slow heating is the difficult case.** Fast heating heats the case before the propellant. Slow heating heats the
  full item together. Thus the propellant can get to its reaction temperature while the case is still cool, strong and
  intact. Most venting features exist because of slow heating.
- **A venting feature is structure with two functions.** It must hold MEOP with margin at all storage temperatures and
  for the full service life, and it must still open when necessary. It needs the same proof, ageing and qualification
  evidence as the remainder of the case.

<Aside type="note">
  IM qualification is a specialist activity and a safety-authority activity. This section gives the view of the
  hardware designer. The propellant selection and the reaction behaviour are the responsibility of the energetics
  specialists.
</Aside>

## Defining motor hardware on the drawing

- **Give more than the alloy.** Give the alloy, condition, product form, applicable specification and melt practice.
  See the [specification builder](../../materials/specification/). *"D6AC bar per AMS 6431, VAR, heat treated to
  1520 MPa min UTS, double temper"* is a specification. *"D6AC"* is not.
- **Special processes get their own callouts.** Heat treatment (with pyrometry to AMS 2750), welding procedure, NDT
  method and acceptance class, coatings and cleanliness each need a note and a specification. If you cannot verify a
  property by a measurement of the finished part, it needs an approved process and a certificate.
- **Fracture-critical parts have different rules.** When a part is classified as fracture-critical, its drawing gives
  the flaw-size and NDE requirements, and its material comes from a controlled quality class. Agree the classification
  before you finalise the tolerances, because it controls surface finish, radii and inspection access.
- **Traceability is a drawing requirement.** Give the batch or heat marking, its location and its method. Stamping is
  a stress raiser and is not permitted on many fracture-critical surfaces. Etching usually is permitted.
- **Dissimilar metals.** A steel case with an aluminium closure, or carbon fibre against any metal, makes a galvanic
  cell. Specify isolation (sealant, primer, isolating film) *and* an electrical bonding path on purpose. These two
  requirements are in opposite directions.
- **Differential expansion.** A carbon throat insert in a metal housing has large temperature changes across materials
  with very different expansion. If a fit depends on temperature, give the assembly temperature on the drawing.
- **Seals in hot environments.** Confirm the seal compound against the real fluids and temperatures. Check if your
  programme requires approved seal materials. Many programmes do.
- **Bondlines are structure.** Bonds between insulation and case, and liner bonds, carry load and are the usual failure
  path. Define the surface preparation, adhesive, bondline thickness, cure and the NDT that verifies the bond. A bond
  without an inspection requirement is a joint that nobody has verified.
