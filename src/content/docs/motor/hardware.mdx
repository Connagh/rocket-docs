## Why this matters

A solid motor is a short list of parts: a case with two closures, insulation, the propellant grain, a nozzle, an
igniter, and the joints and seals between them. When motors fail, it is usually one of those hardware parts — or the
interface between two of them — not the propellant chemistry. The Space Shuttle *Challenger* was lost in January 1986
to a booster field joint whose seals could not follow the joint as it opened under pressure (see
[O-ring design](../../seals/o-ring-design/)). A Titan 34D was lost later that year after insulation came away from the
case of one of its solid boosters and let the flame reach the steel.

And every one of those parts is dead weight once the propellant has burned. Hardware design is that tension: enough
strength, temperature capability and margin to survive — and not a gram more.

## The number every part answers to: mass fraction

The motor's **propellant mass fraction** is the share of its mass that is propellant:

$$
\zeta = \frac{m_p}{m_p + m_\text{inert}}
$$

What that buys is set by the rocket equation. With $m_0$ the mass at ignition and $m_f$ the mass at burnout,

$$
\Delta v = I_{sp}\,g_0 \ln\frac{m_0}{m_f}
\qquad
m_0 = m_p + m_\text{inert} + m_\text{payload},
\quad
m_f = m_\text{inert} + m_\text{payload}
$$

where $I_{sp}\,g_0$ is the effective exhaust velocity (for a solid motor, $C_F\,c^*$ — see the
[primer](../../fundamentals/how-a-solid-motor-works/#from-pressure-to-thrust)). Inert mass sits in the denominator, so
it hurts twice: it is pushed for the whole burn and it is still there at the end. Two results fall straight out:

- **What one inert kilogram costs.** Differentiating,
  $$
  \frac{\partial\,\Delta v}{\partial m_\text{inert}} = -\,I_{sp}\,g_0\,\frac{m_p}{m_0\,m_f}
  $$
- **The exchange ratio.** To hold $\Delta v$ when inert mass grows by $\delta$, the mass ratio $m_0/m_f$ must stay the
  same, so the propellant must grow by $(m_0/m_f - 1)\,\delta$. With a mass ratio of 2.5, each extra kilogram of
  hardware needs 1.5 kg more propellant — which needs a longer case, more insulation, a bigger nozzle. That spiral is
  why overweight motors are so hard to recover late in a design.

<Analogy title="Packing for a long hike">
  Every gram of tent, stove and pack frame is a gram of food you cannot carry, and you carry the tent all the way to
  the end — long after the food is eaten. Ultralight hikers do not eat less; they weigh their hardware. A motor
  designer does the same with the case, insulation and nozzle.
</Analogy>

Large motors with composite cases reach mass fractions above 0.9. Small motors fall well below that, because the
igniter, nozzle throat, bosses and seals do not shrink in proportion to the propellant.

<Aside type="tip" title="A design order that works">
  Fix MEOP and the proof and burst factors first. Then choose the case material class, size the wall, add the
  insulation — and only then check what mass fraction is left. Choosing the material last, after the geometry is
  frozen, is how motors end up overweight with no options remaining.
</Aside>

### Worked example — the 300 mm motor from the other pages

The same motor as the [case-sizing](../case-sizing/#worked-example),
[propellant-grain](../propellant-grain/) and [insulation](../insulation/) examples. These are the calculator's
starting values.

- **Case:** 11.77 kg — the 1.377 mm D6AC cylinder with hemispherical domes, from the case-sizing example.
- **Propellant:** a 300 mm grain with a 100 mm bore, 1000 mm long, in the cylinder only:
  $\frac{\pi}{4}(0.300^2 - 0.100^2) \times 1.000 \times 1770 = 111.2$ kg.
- **Insulation:** 2 mm of EPDM along the cylinder, where the grain covers it until tail-off
  ($0.942\ \text{m}^2 \times 2\ \text{mm} \times 1100 = 2.07$ kg), and the 8.9 mm the insulation example needed in the
  domes ($0.283\ \text{m}^2 \times 8.9\ \text{mm} \times 1100 = 2.77$ kg): 4.84 kg in all.
- **Nozzle 6 kg and other hardware 4 kg** — round illustrative figures. **Payload** 50 kg.
- **Exhaust velocity** from the grain page's illustrative values: $C_F\,c^* = 1.5 \times 1540 = 2310$ m/s, which is
  $I_{sp} = 235.55$ s.

1. **Inert mass** $= 11.77 + 4.84 + 6 + 4 = 26.61$ kg, so $\zeta = 111.2 / 137.81 = 0.807$.
2. **Mass ratio:** $m_0 = 187.81$ kg, $m_f = 76.61$ kg, so $m_0/m_f = 2.452$.
3. **Ideal $\Delta v$** $= 2310 \times \ln 2.452 = 2310 \times 0.897 = 2071$ m/s.
4. **Cost of one inert kilogram** $= 2310 \times 111.2 / (187.81 \times 76.61) = 17.9$ m/s, or
   $2.452 - 1 = 1.45$ kg of extra propellant to keep the same $\Delta v$.
5. **What the dome insulation costs.** The 2.77 kg the bondline limit demanded costs about **51 m/s**. That is the
   price of the insulation example's finding that the bondline, not the char, governs — and the reason a case with
   more heat capacity or a better insulator is worth a look.

If a composite case did the case's job for 6 kg less — an assumption here, not a design — $\Delta v$ would rise by
about 113 m/s. Try it in the calculator, then try the same 6 kg on the nozzle: a kilogram is a kilogram wherever it is.

## Then and now — why the materials changed

| Part | Then | Now | What drove the change |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Case | Welded high-strength steel (D6AC, maraging); titanium on some upper stages | Filament-wound carbon/epoxy (aramid/epoxy before it) | Fibre laid exactly along the load paths: far higher strength per unit mass |
| Insulation | Asbestos- and silica-filled nitrile rubber | Aramid- or silica-filled EPDM | Health (asbestos), lower density, better ageing |
| Throat | Bulk and pyrolytic graphite; tungsten on small motors | Carbon–carbon, often 3-D woven | Toughness under thermal shock, at lower mass |
| Exit cone | Tape-wrapped carbon- and silica-phenolic over a metal shell | Thin radiation-cooled carbon–carbon; extendible cones on upper stages | Mass; more expansion ratio in the same stowed length |
| Steering | Jet vanes, jetavators, liquid injection | Flexible-bearing movable nozzles, increasingly with electromechanical actuators | No thrust lost in the exhaust; no injectant tank or hydraulics |
| Nozzle housing | Steel | Aluminium, titanium, composite | Mass |

Read the last column again. Most of these changes rode on a **manufacturing process** — filament winding, 3-D weaving,
tape wrapping and laminated bearings — as much as on a new material. When you select a material, ask what process it
makes possible; that is usually where the real gain is.

## Designing the case to fail gently

A motor caught in a fuel fire, or hit by a fragment, may well ignite whatever the designer does. What the hardware
decides is **what happens next**. If the case keeps holding pressure, the burning propellant pressurises it until it
bursts — an explosion. If the case opens early and stays open, the propellant burns at low pressure — a fire.
Insensitive-munitions (IM) design, from the hardware side, is about guaranteeing the second outcome.

<ImResponse />

The threats a design is assessed against are **fast heating** (a fuel fire engulfing the item), **slow heating** (a
gradual rise, such as a fire in a neighbouring store), **bullet impact**, **fragment impact**, **sympathetic reaction**
(a neighbouring munition reacting) and **shaped-charge jet**. NATO policy is STANAG 4439; AOP-39 gives the assessment
approach and the required response for each threat. As with every factor on this site, take those requirements from
the current editions, not from a page like this.

What the hardware designer controls:

- **Composite cases tend to vent themselves in a fast fire** — the resin softens and the case loses strength early.
- **Metal cases need help** — a feature that weakens or releases the case or a closure at a temperature below the
  point where the propellant reacts violently.
- **Slow heating is the hard case.** Fast heating cooks the case before the propellant; slow heating brings the whole
  item up together, so the propellant can reach its reaction temperature while the case is still cold-strong and
  intact. Most deliberate venting features exist because of slow heating.
- **A venting feature is structure with two jobs.** It must hold MEOP with margin through every storage temperature
  and the whole service life, and still open when it should. It goes through the same proof, ageing and
  qualification evidence as the rest of the case.

## Defining motor hardware on the drawing

- **Say more than the alloy.** Alloy, condition, product form, governing specification and melt practice — see the
  [specification builder](../../materials/specification/). *"D6AC bar per AMS 6431, VAR, heat treated to 1520 MPa min
  UTS, double temper"* is a specification; *"D6AC"* is a wish.
- **Special processes get their own callouts.** Heat treatment (with pyrometry to AMS 2750), welding procedure, NDT
  method and acceptance class, coatings and cleanliness each need a note and a specification. If it cannot be verified
  by measuring the finished part, it needs an approved process and a certificate.
- **Fracture-critical parts change the rules.** Once a part is classified fracture-critical, its drawing carries the
  flaw-size and NDE requirements, and its material comes from a controlled quality class. Agree the classification
  before finalising tolerances — it drives surface finish, radii and inspection access.
- **Traceability is a drawing requirement.** State the batch or heat marking, its location and its method. Stamping is
  a stress raiser and is banned on many fracture-critical surfaces; etching usually is not.
- **Dissimilar metals.** A steel case with an aluminium closure, or carbon fibre against any metal, makes a galvanic
  cell. Specify isolation (sealant, primer, isolating film) *and* an electrical bonding path deliberately — they pull in
  opposite directions.
- **Differential expansion.** A carbon throat insert in a metal housing sees large temperature swings across materials
  with very different expansion. If a fit depends on temperature, state the assembly temperature on the drawing.
- **Seals in hot environments.** Confirm the seal compound against the real fluids and temperatures, and check whether
  your programme mandates approved seal materials — many do.
- **Bondlines are structure.** Insulation-to-case and liner bonds carry load and are the usual failure path. Define
  surface preparation, adhesive, bondline thickness, cure and the NDT that verifies the bond. A bond with no
  inspection requirement is an unverified joint.
