## Why this matters

Combustion gas is around 3000 K. High-strength case steels lose strength quickly above a few hundred °C, composite
case resins soften at around 120–200 °C, and the adhesive bonding insulation to case may be limited lower still. The
insulation is all that stands between them. Too thin, and the case or bond overheats or burns through — a
catastrophic failure. Too thick, and it is inert mass, which costs performance just like case mass. Insulation can be a
significant share of a motor's inert mass, so it is sized region by region, not with one blanket thickness.

## Two jobs at once

<CharLayers />

An ablative insulator protects the structure in two ways:

1. **It gives up its surface on purpose.** Heat decomposes the rubber or resin (pyrolysis), leaving a porous carbon
   **char**. The decomposition absorbs heat, and the gases it releases percolate out through the char and thicken the
   boundary layer, blocking some of the incoming heat ("blowing"). The flow scours the char away slowly — **erosion**.
2. **The virgin material behind it insulates.** Low conductivity keeps the heat near the surface, so the bondline and
   case stay cool.

<Analogy title="A log in a campfire">
  A thick log burns for hours because its outside turns to charcoal, and charcoal is a good insulator: the wood inside
  stays brown and cool while the surface glows. Poke the log and knock the char off, and it burns much faster. Motor
  insulation is the same: the char layer protects what is behind it, and anything that strips the char — fast gas,
  impinging aluminium-oxide droplets — speeds up the attack.
</Analogy>

**Material affected depth (MAD)** is everything the flame has used up: eroded material plus char. It is the number
measured by sectioning insulation after a firing, and the basis of most insulation design rules.

<Analogy title="An oven glove versus a cast-iron pan">
  A cast-iron pan doesn't burn on the hob, but its handle gets too hot to hold: it resists the flame and passes the
  heat on. An oven glove chars if you hold it in the flame, but your hand stays cool. Graphite and carbon-carbon
  behave like the pan — superb surface resistance, poor insulation — and rubbers and phenolics like the glove. That is
  why a graphite throat insert always has an insulator behind it.
</Analogy>

## Exposure time sets the thickness

Insulation only works while hot gas touches it, so the first question for any location is **how long is it
exposed?** That comes straight from the grain design.

<InsulationZones />

- **Under the grain**, the cylinder insulation is covered by propellant until the web burns through. Exposure is only
  the tail-off, so this is usually the thinnest insulation in the motor.
- **Domes and grain ends** are exposed from ignition, so their exposure is close to the full burn time.
- **The aft dome and nozzle entrance** add the fastest, turning flow and aluminium-oxide particles hitting the
  surface, so erosion dominates. This is where the thickest, toughest grades go.

With a star or slotted grain, the flame reaches the case under the star tips long before it reaches the case under
the valleys. Exposure — and the thickness needed — therefore varies **around the circumference**, not just along the
motor:

## The physics of charring

### Heat soaks in as the square root of time

Treat the insulation as a thick slab whose surface is suddenly held at a high temperature. Heat spreads in by
conduction, and the temperature rise at depth $x$ after time $t$ is

$$
\frac{T - T_0}{T_s - T_0} = \operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}\right),
\qquad \alpha = \frac{k}{\rho\,c_p}
$$

where $\alpha$ is thermal diffusivity. Every depth scales with $\sqrt{\alpha t}$: **double the time and the heat
reaches only about 1.4 times deeper**.

<Analogy title="Roasting a joint of meat">
  Cooking time goes with the square of thickness, which is why a joint twice as thick takes about four times as long,
  not twice. The same √t law sets how far heat reaches into insulation. For insulation it is good news: a long burn is
  less punishing per second than simple arithmetic suggests.
</Analogy>

### Erosion changes the shape of the problem

If the surface also recedes at a steady rate $v$, the exact solution for a semi-infinite solid with its surface held
at $T_s$ is:

$$
\theta(\xi, t) = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\frac{\xi + v t}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}\right)
+ e^{-v\xi/\alpha}\,\operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\frac{\xi - v t}{2\sqrt{\alpha t}}\right)\right]
$$

where $\xi$ is depth below the *current* surface. The char front is where $\theta$ falls to the pyrolysis level
$\theta_p = (T_p - T_0)/(T_s - T_0)$. Two limits make it easy to read:

- **No erosion ($v = 0$):** char grows as $\sqrt{t}$ indefinitely.
- **Long times:** the profile settles into $\theta = e^{-v\xi/\alpha}$, which travels with the surface. The char layer
  stops growing at a **steady thickness**

$$
\delta_{\text{char}} = \frac{\alpha}{v}\,\ln\frac{1}{\theta_p}
$$

  and the affected depth then grows linearly with time, $\text{MAD} \approx v\,t + \delta_\text{char}$.

So **short burns are char-dominated and long burns are erosion-dominated.** That is why the erosion rate — which
depends on the local flow, not just the material — is the number insulation designers argue about most.

### After burnout: soak-back

When the motor stops, the hot char and insulation still hold a lot of heat. With no gas flow to carry it away, much
of it flows **inward**, the only way left to go. The bondline often reaches its peak temperature **minutes after
burnout**, long after the insulation surface has started to cool.

<Analogy title="A stone pizza oven">
  Rake the fire out of a stone oven and it keeps cooking for hours, because the heat stored in the stone keeps flowing
  into the bread. The insulation layer is that stone; the bondline and case are the bread.
</Analogy>

How much of the stored heat goes inward depends on how the motor cools after burnout: radiation to the cold nozzle
opening, venting, and heat absorbed by continuing pyrolysis. The calculator brackets it with two bounds:
**insulated face** (all stored heat soaks inward — conservative) and **radiating face** (the surface radiates to cold
surroundings — optimistic for most of a closed case).

## Sizing rule

Two requirements, and the thicker wins:

$$
t \;\ge\; SF \times \text{MAD}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
T_\text{bond,peak}(t) \;\le\; T_\text{limit}
$$

plus a **minimum gauge** for manufacture and handling. Safety factors of around 1.5–2.0 on measured affected depth
are common in the open literature. Your programme sets its own, often higher in the aft dome and other regions with
large uncertainty.

## Worked example — EPDM over a thin steel case

Aramid-filled EPDM ($k = 0.25$ W/m·K, $\rho = 1100$ kg/m³, $c_p = 2000$ J/kg·K) on the 1.4 mm D6AC case from the
[case-sizing example](../case-sizing/#worked-example), in a low-velocity region exposed for 20 s. Effective surface
temperature 1200 °C, char front 450 °C, erosion 0.025 mm/s, bondline limit 150 °C, safety factor 1.5. These are the
calculator's defaults.

1. **Diffusivity.** $\alpha = 0.25 / (1100 \times 2000) = 1.14 \times 10^{-7}$ m²/s, so $\sqrt{\alpha t} = 1.51$ mm
   at 20 s.
2. **Char front level.** $\theta_p = (450 - 20)/(1200 - 20) = 0.364$.
3. **Without erosion** the char would reach $2\sqrt{\alpha t}\;\operatorname{erfc}^{-1}(0.364) = 3.02 \times 0.64 =
   1.93$ mm.
4. **With erosion**, 0.5 mm of surface is removed, and the moving-surface solution puts the char front 1.64 mm below
   the new surface. Affected depth at burnout is $0.5 + 1.64 = 2.14$ mm. The calculator's transient model gets the same
   1.64 mm, and the automated tests check that agreement.
5. **Soak-back** pushes the char front a little deeper after burnout: MAD ≈ 2.4 mm, so $1.5 \times \text{MAD} =
   3.6$ mm.
6. **Bondline.** Keeping the peak bondline temperature below 150 °C needs about **8.9 mm** with the insulated-face
   assumption (peak about 6 minutes after burnout), or 6.7 mm with the radiating face.

**The bondline governs, by a wide margin.** The affected depth alone suggests 3.6 mm, but a 1.4 mm steel case has very
little heat capacity to absorb soak-back. Try the carbon/epoxy case, a 60 s exposure, or the high-velocity regime, and
watch which requirement takes over. The steady char thickness, $(\alpha/v)\ln(1/\theta_p) = 4.6$ mm, shows that at this
erosion rate the char layer would never exceed about 4.6 mm, however long the burn.

## Choosing the material

| Material | Where it's used | Strengths | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| EPDM, aramid-fibre filled | Case insulation, most regions | Lowest density and conductivity, good ageing, good bond | Lower erosion resistance in high-velocity, particle-laden flow |
| EPDM, carbon-fibre filled | Aft domes, high-flow regions | Tougher, more adherent char | Higher conductivity than aramid grades |
| NBR, silica filled | Case insulation (older and some current designs) | Good propellant compatibility, tough char | Denser; historically asbestos filled — phased out |
| Silicone, silica filled | Flexible joints, wide-temperature service | Silica-rich char, flexible when cold | Bonding needs special primers |
| Carbon phenolic | Nozzle throat region, entrance (large motors) | High erosion resistance, good insulation | Precursor supply; delamination and pocketing if made badly |
| Silica / glass phenolic | Exit cones, insulation behind inserts | Cheaper, lower conductivity than carbon phenolic | Lower erosion resistance; silica melts at high heat flux |
| Carbon-carbon | Throats and exit cones | Very low erosion, light, strong when hot | Conducts heat — needs backing insulation; cost; oxidation |
| Bulk graphite | Throat inserts in small motors | Cheap, low erosion | Thermal shock cracking; very conductive |

How to choose, in order:

1. **Exposure and flow first.** Long exposure in fast, particle-laden flow needs an erosion-resistant grade. Short,
   gentle exposure is driven by conductivity and density.
2. **Then mass.** Density × thickness is the cost. A slightly less erosion-resistant but much lighter insulator often
   wins in low-velocity regions.
3. **Compatibility with the propellant.** Plasticiser and burn-rate catalysts can migrate from the propellant into the
   liner and insulation over years of storage. That weakens the bond and changes the propellant next to it. The liner
   is formulated partly as a barrier against this migration.
4. **Bond and processing.** How the insulation is laid up (calendered sheet, cured in place or co-cured with a
   composite case), and how it bonds to the case and the propellant liner, are as important as its thermal numbers.
5. **Ageing.** Insulation has to keep its properties and its bonds for the motor's whole service life.

## Where this model stops

<Aside type="caution" title="Screening model, not a qualified thermal analysis">
  The calculator holds the surface at one effective temperature, uses constant virgin properties throughout, and
  ignores pyrolysis heat absorption, gas blowing and the char's different properties. That makes the bondline result
  conservative in some ways and optimistic in others. Real insulation is sized with charring-ablation codes calibrated
  against material-affected depths from sectioned motors, and bondline thermocouple data from firings.
</Aside>

- **Erosion is an input, not a prediction.** It depends on local velocity, particle impingement, pressure, propellant
  aluminium content and the material's char strength. Use measured rates.
- **2-D effects** at joints, flaps, grain terminations and the nozzle entrance can dominate locally.
- **Nozzle throats** erode much faster than case insulation and change chamber pressure as they do — covered on the
  planned nozzle page.

## On the drawing

- **Thickness map** — insulation thickness by zone, with tolerances. Show where it is measured and with what method.
- **Material and process specifications** — compound, lay-up and cure, bond surface preparation and primers.
- **Bond inspection** — NDT of the case-to-insulation and insulation-to-liner bonds, with acceptance criteria.
- **Stress-relief flaps** — geometry, and the release agent that lets them open.
