## Why this matters

The case is usually the heaviest inert part of a solid motor, and every kilogram of case is a kilogram of propellant
you are not carrying. **Propellant mass fraction** — propellant mass over total motor mass — is the single biggest
hardware lever on delivered performance. Case sizing is therefore a balance between being light enough to perform and
strong enough to never fail, with no leak warning and an energetic payload inside.

## The intuition: why cylinders split lengthwise

<Analogy title="A sausage in a hot pan">
  Overcooked sausages and frozen water pipes always split **along their length**, never around their circumference.
  That is not a weakness in the skin — it is geometry. The force trying to open a lengthwise seam grows with the
  *diameter*, but the force trying to pull the end off grows with the end *area* and is resisted by the whole
  circumference. Work it through and the lengthwise seam carries exactly twice the stress.
</Analogy>

<HoopVsAxial />

For a thin-walled cylinder of inner radius $r$ and wall $t$ at internal pressure $p$:

$$
\sigma_h = \frac{p\,r}{t} \qquad\qquad \sigma_a = \frac{p\,r}{2t} = \frac{\sigma_h}{2}
$$

A **sphere** has no "long direction" — every cut is the same as cut B — so its wall stress is $p\,r/2t$ in every
direction. That is why a hemispherical dome needs only **half the wall** of the cylinder it closes, and why the
sphere is the lightest possible pressure vessel. Domes are bubbles; flat closures are plates in bending and are heavy.

## The three pressures

A case is not designed to the operating pressure. It is judged against a ladder of pressures, every one referenced to
the **maximum expected operating pressure (MEOP)**:

<PressureLadder />

- **MEOP** already contains the hot-day grain temperature, manufacturing spread, ignition spike and erosive burning.
  See [why pressure is amplified](../../fundamentals/how-a-solid-motor-works/#the-consequence-for-hardware-small-changes-are-amplified).
- **Proof** is applied to every case (or a defined sample) to show no yield and no leak. It is also your best
  **flaw screen** — any crack big enough to fail at proof fails on the test stand, not in service.
- **Design burst** is the minimum ultimate capability the design must have, shown by analysis and by test to failure
  on qualification articles.

That gives two sizing requirements, and the thicker wall wins:

$$
t_{\text{proof}}:\;\; \sigma_h(p_{\text{proof}}) \le \eta\,\sigma_y
\qquad\qquad
t_{\text{burst}}:\;\; \sigma_h(p_{\text{burst}}) \le \eta\,\sigma_{\text{UTS}}
$$

where $\eta$ is the weld (joint) efficiency. High-strength case steels have yield close to UTS, so burst usually
governs; for lower yield-to-tensile ratios, proof can.

## When "thin" stops being thin

The thin-wall formula assumes the stress is uniform through the wall. It isn't — it is highest at the bore. Lamé's
thick-cylinder solution gives the exact hoop stress at the inner surface:

$$
\sigma_{h,\text{bore}} = p\,\frac{r_o^2 + r_i^2}{r_o^2 - r_i^2}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
t = r_i\left(\sqrt{\frac{\sigma + p}{\sigma - p}} - 1\right)
$$

Below about $t/r = 0.1$ the two agree within a few percent. Motor cases are typically far thinner than that, but
closures, bosses and high-pressure gas generators are not. The calculator below always uses Lamé and shows the
thin-wall value alongside so you can see the difference.

## Leak before break

A case can fail two ways. In **leak-before-break**, a crack grows through the wall, vents gas and is noticed. In
**break-before-leak**, a crack reaches critical size while still buried in the wall and the case ruptures without
warning. The simplest screen (Irwin's) asks whether a crack that has just broken through — about as long as the wall
is thick on each side, half-length $c \approx t$ — would be stable:

$$
c_{\text{crit}} = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{K_{IC}}{\sigma_h}\right)^2
\qquad\text{leak before break if}\qquad c_{\text{crit}} \ge t
$$

The full treatment — growing a surface flaw through the wall and checking the through crack it becomes, with bulging
and plastic collapse — is on [Leak before break](../../verify/leak-before-break/).

<Analogy title="A balloon versus a football">
  Prick a balloon and it bursts — the material is highly stressed and has little toughness, so the hole runs. Prick a
  football and it just goes flat — the hole stays a hole. You want your case to behave like the football. Higher
  strength steels let you thin the wall, but their toughness usually falls as strength rises, pushing you towards the
  balloon.
</Analogy>

This is a screening check only. Real fracture-control plans use surface and embedded crack solutions, measured
toughness at the right orientation, temperature and thickness, and crack-growth analysis over the motor's pressure
history.

## Worked example

A welded D6AC case: internal diameter 300 mm, cylinder length 1000 mm, MEOP 10 MPa (100 bar), proof factor 1.10, burst
factor 1.25, full-penetration welds fully radiographed ($\eta = 0.90$). Material values from the database: UTS 1520 MPa,
yield 1450 MPa, $K_{IC} \approx 70$ MPa√m, density 7.85 g/cm³. The factors are for this example only; press **Load
worked example** in the calculator to load these inputs.

1. **Design pressures.** $p_{\text{proof}} = 1.10 \times 10 = 11.0$ MPa; $p_{\text{burst}} = 1.25 \times 10 = 12.5$ MPa.
2. **Allowables with weld efficiency.** $0.9 \times 1520 = 1368$ MPa ultimate; $0.9 \times 1450 = 1305$ MPa yield.
3. **Thin-wall thicknesses.**
   Burst: $t = 12.5 \times 150 / 1368 = 1.371$ mm. Proof: $t = 11.0 \times 150 / 1305 = 1.264$ mm.
   **Burst governs.**
4. **Lamé check.** $t = 150\left(\sqrt{1380.5/1355.5} - 1\right) = 1.377$ mm — within 0.5% of thin-wall, as
   expected for $t/r \approx 0.009$.
5. **Stress at MEOP.** $\sigma_h \approx 1094$ MPa hoop, about 547 MPa axial.
6. **Leak before break.** $c_{\text{crit}} = \frac{1}{\pi}(70/1094)^2 = 1.30$ mm, which is **less than** the 1.38 mm
   wall. A through-crack would not be stable: this case would be expected to **burst without leaking first**.
7. **Mass.** Cylinder $\approx 10.2$ kg of steel holding about 85 litres (including hemispherical domes) at 10 MPa.

<Aside type="tip" title="What the example teaches">
  The membrane sizing is easy and the answer is startlingly thin: relative to its size, this wall is thinner than an
  eggshell. The hard part is step 6. At this strength level the case is not leak-before-break, so its safety rests on
  **NDT that reliably finds small flaws** and on the **proof test** screening anything larger. Load the calculator
  with these inputs and try 15CDV6 or maraging 18Ni-250 instead to see how the trade moves.
</Aside>

## Where this tool stops

<Aside type="caution" title="Membrane stress is the easy part">
  The calculator sizes the **cylinder away from its ends**. Real cases fail at the discontinuities it does not model.
</Aside>

- **Dome-to-cylinder junctions.** A cylinder and a dome under the same pressure want to grow by different amounts.
  Forcing them to match creates local bending that can raise stress well above membrane values. A 2:1 ellipsoidal
  head also carries **compressive hoop stress** in its knuckle, which can buckle thin heads.
- **Closures, bosses and joints.** Polar bosses, bolted or pinned closure joints, clevis joints and skirt attachments
  are where load paths concentrate. These need their own analysis (and usually FEA) — see
  [Load paths & joints](../../joints/load-paths/).
- **External and flight loads.** Axial thrust, bending from flight loads, and external pressure or handling loads add
  to pressure stresses. Unpressurised thin cases can be governed by **buckling**, not burst.
- **Temperature.** Allowables must be at the case's temperature during and after the burn, including heat soak-back
  from the insulation after burnout.
- **Composite cases.** Filament-wound cases are sized by netting analysis on fibre strength and wind angle, not by
  isotropic membrane theory. They are covered on a separate (planned) page.

## Defining it on the drawing

- **Say more than the alloy.** Alloy, condition/temper, product form, governing specification and melt practice:
  *"D6AC bar per AMS 6431, VAR, heat treated to 1520 MPa min UTS, double temper"* is a specification; *"D6AC"* is a
  wish.
- **Special processes get their own notes.** Heat treatment (with pyrometry to AMS 2750), welding procedure, NDT
  method and acceptance class, coatings and cleanliness are each separate callouts with their own specification.
- **Fracture-critical parts change the rules.** Once classified fracture-critical, the drawing carries the flaw-size
  and NDE requirements, and material comes from a controlled quality class.
- **Proof test is a drawing or acceptance requirement**, with pressure, hold time, medium, temperature and acceptance
  criteria (no leak, permanent set limits) stated.
