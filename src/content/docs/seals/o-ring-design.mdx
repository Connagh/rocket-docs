## Why this matters

O-rings are the cheapest parts on a motor and some of the most safety-critical. Case joints, the nozzle-to-case joint,
igniter bosses and closures all rely on them to hold hot, high-pressure gas. A leak path past a seal lets combustion
gas cut through metal in seconds. The worst-known example is the loss of the Space Shuttle *Challenger* in 1986, traced
to a booster field-joint O-ring that could not seal on a cold morning.

## How an O-ring seals

<ORingStates />

<Analogy title="A plug-type aircraft door">
  An airliner's passenger door is slightly bigger than its frame and fits from the inside, so cabin pressure pushes it
  harder into the frame the higher the aircraft climbs. An O-ring works the same way: the squeeze you build in gives it
  a starting seal, and system pressure then pushes it harder into the low-pressure corner of the groove. The seal gets
  tighter as the pressure rises — until the pressure is high enough to push rubber through the clearance gap.
</Analogy>

Rubber behaves like a very stiff liquid in bulk and a very soft solid in shape: it changes shape easily but hardly
changes volume.

<Analogy title="A water balloon in a box">
  Push down on a water balloon in a box and it bulges sideways — its volume can't change, only its shape. If the box is
  too small it has nowhere to go and the pressure inside climbs enormously. That is why groove *volume* (gland fill)
  matters as much as depth: the ring must have room to change shape, plus room to grow when it is hot or swells in
  fluid.
</Analogy>

## The four numbers

For a ring of cross-section $d$ in a gland of depth $g$ (the radial or axial space it is squeezed into) and width $w$:

| Quantity | Definition | What goes wrong |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Squeeze** | $s = \dfrac{d - g}{d}$ | Too little: no initial seal, especially cold. Too much: overstress, set, high assembly force |
| **Gland fill** | $\phi = \dfrac{\pi d^2 / 4}{w\,g}$ | Over ~90%: no room to expand — hydraulic lock, cracked groove lands, extrusion |
| **Stretch** | $\varepsilon = \dfrac{D_\text{groove} - ID}{ID}$ | Too much thins the section and shortens life; negative leaves the ring loose to roll |
| **Extrusion gap** | Diametral clearance at the low-pressure side | Too wide for the pressure and hardness: rubber is nibbled into the gap |

Stretching a ring onto a groove thins its cross-section. Assuming constant volume, the stretched section is

$$
d' = \frac{d}{\sqrt{1 + \varepsilon}}
$$

so 2% stretch loses about 1% of the section — and about 1% of squeeze with it.

### Nominal is not enough

Every one of those numbers changes across the tolerance band, and the extremes are what fail. A 5.33 mm section has a
tolerance of about ±0.13 mm. On its own, that moves squeeze by about ±2.5 percentage points. Add the groove, bore and
piston tolerances, then let the parts sit off-centre (the gland is shallow on one side and deep on the other), and a
nominal 19% squeeze can range from about 13% to 25%.

Then add the things that move after assembly:

- **Temperature.** Elastomers expand about ten times more than steel (around $1.6\times10^{-4}$/K). Cold rings shrink and
  lose squeeze; hot rings grow and fill the groove.
- **Fluid swell.** A few percent of volume growth in service is normal and must fit in the groove.
- **Compression set.** Over time and temperature the ring takes a permanent set and stops pushing back as hard.
  Budget it into minimum squeeze.
- **Joint opening.** Under pressure, case joints rotate, bores dilate and flanges lift. The gap the ring has to fill
  gets bigger exactly when the pressure arrives. Bolt preload is what keeps a flanged gland shut — see
  [Bolted joints](../../joints/bolted-joints/). Where to seat the seal so the gap barely moves, and how to back it up and leak-check it, is on
  [Sealing strategy](../sealing-strategy/).

## The Challenger lesson: the seal must follow the joint

<Aside type="caution" title="A seal is a spring, and cold springs are slow">
  In a Shuttle booster field joint, ignition pressure made the joint rotate slightly and open the gap the O-rings
  sealed. A warm O-ring springs out fast enough to follow. On the cold morning of 28 January 1986 (around 2 °C
  ambient, with the joint colder still) the fluoroelastomer rings recovered too slowly. Hot gas got past before they
  sealed, and the joint failed about a minute into flight.
</Aside>

During the Rogers Commission hearings, Richard Feynman showed the effect with a sample of the O-ring rubber clamped in
iced water: released from the clamp, it stayed squashed instead of springing back. Two design rules come straight from
that:

1. **Size the minimum squeeze with the joint opened under pressure, at the coldest temperature** — the calculator's
   "joint opening" input does exactly this.
2. **Do not rely on rubber resilience to follow motion near its low-temperature limit.** Specify the compound by its
   low-temperature recovery (TR-10 and resilience tests), heat the joint, or design the joint so it does not open. The
   redesigned booster joint added a capture feature to limit rotation, a third O-ring and joint heaters. The
   [viscoelasticity page](../../fundamentals/viscoelasticity/#worked-example--a-fluoroelastomer-seal) shows why: cold,
   every recovery process in the rubber runs thousands of times slower.

Two more joint lessons:

- **Redundancy only counts if both seals work.** A primary and secondary O-ring with a leak-check port between them is
  standard. Show that the secondary can still seal when the joint is opened and the primary has already let gas past.
- **A leak check pushes the primary O-ring the wrong way.** Pressurising between two rings drives the primary towards
  the motor side of its groove, so at ignition it must first travel across the groove before it can seal. Leak-check
  pressure and procedure are part of the seal design.

## Worked example — a nozzle-to-case radial seal

The calculator's defaults: a static radial seal in a piston-type closure, 150 mm bore at 10 MPa, steel housing,
service range −40 to +60 °C. Ring: 5.33 mm section, 139.29 mm stock ID. Groove root Ø141.45–141.55, width 7.0–7.2 mm;
bore Ø150.000–150.063, plug Ø149.860–149.900; parts free to sit off-centre.

1. **Nominal.** Gland depth $(150.03 - 141.50)/2 = 4.27$ mm. Stretch onto the root is $(141.5 - 139.29)/139.29 = 1.6\%$,
   which thins the 5.33 section to 5.29 mm. Squeeze $= (5.29 - 4.27)/5.29 = 19.3\%$; fill 73%. Comfortably in band.
2. **Worst corners.** Fully off-centre, the gland ranges from 4.16 to 4.41 mm. Maximum squeeze — largest ring, hot,
   swollen 5% — is 25%. Minimum — smallest ring, most stretch, −40 °C — is 13.5%, and **10.8% after 20% compression
   set**. That is right at the edge of the 10% screening floor.
3. **Extrusion.** The clearance can reach 0.203 mm. At 10 MPa a 70 Shore ring wants about 0.08 mm, and even 90 Shore
   only about 0.15 mm. **A back-up ring is needed**, or a tighter fit and a harder compound.
4. **Compound.** Standard FKM is typically limited to about −20 °C. At −40 °C it fails the temperature check — use a
   low-temperature fluoroelastomer grade or another compound, and confirm the TR-10.
5. **Joint opening.** Enter 0.25 mm of radial opening under pressure: minimum squeeze falls to **6.9%** and the
   cold-resilience check fails. That is the Challenger situation in miniature — fix the joint stiffness, not the seal.

## Choosing the compound

Pick the compound for the coldest, hottest and chemically worst moment — in that order. The table in the calculator
checks every compound against your temperature range and medium. The traps that catch experienced engineers:

- **EPDM with petroleum grease.** EPDM is excellent in water, steam and phosphate-ester fluids, but swells and fails in
  mineral oil — including the assembly lubricant. Specify the lubricant on the drawing next to the compound.
- **Standard FKM when cold.** Superb hot and in fuels, but standard grades stiffen around −20 °C. Low-temperature grades
  exist; specify one by its TR-10.
- **Silicone in dynamic duty.** Wide temperature range, but poor tear and abrasion strength. Static seals only.
- **Oxygen service.** Ordinary elastomers can ignite in oxygen. LOX and high-pressure oxygen seals use specifically
  tested materials and controlled cleanliness.
- **Brittle point is not the limit.** Sealing is lost far above the brittle point. Use TR-10 or resilience data.

## Groove details that decide the outcome

- **Surface finish.** Static sealing faces typically around Ra 0.8 µm (finer for gases and vacuum); dynamic
  counterfaces Ra 0.2–0.4 µm; groove walls about Ra 1.6–3.2 µm. The machining lay should not cross the seal.
- **Lead-in chamfers** of 15–20°, deburred and blended, on every edge the ring passes over. More O-rings are cut on
  assembly than fail in service, and a cut ring cannot be seen once installed.
- **Groove corner radii** small enough for the ring to seat, never sharp.
- **Back-up rings** on the low-pressure side, or on both sides if pressure reverses. Add their width to the groove.

## Where this stops

<Aside type="caution" title="Screening, not a seal qualification">
  The squeeze bands, fill limit and extrusion table are typical guidance, not ISO 3601-2 dimensions. Real joint seals
  are qualified by test at temperature, with the joint deflecting as it does in service, and the compound's
  low-temperature recovery measured directly.
</Aside>
