## Why this matters

The same part, measured by two inspectors with different datum set-ups, gives two different answers — and both can be
measured correctly. The datums decide what the tolerances mean. Choose them from the machining set-up and the drawing
controls what was easy to measure; choose them from the assembly and it controls what makes the part work.

Most disputes between a gauge and a coordinate-measuring machine (CMM), or between a supplier and a customer, come back
to datums: their order, how they are simulated, and whether a datum feature at maximum material boundary was allowed to
shift. Getting them right on the drawing, and making sure inspection does what the drawing says, is cheaper than any
re-make.

## Building a datum reference frame from function

A rigid part has six degrees of freedom: three translations and three rotations. A datum reference frame removes them in
order of precedence, and the order should follow the way the part is assembled.

<DatumDof />

| Datum | Closure feature | Simulated by | Removes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A, primary | Seating face on the case flange | A surface plate, contacting the high points | Tz, Rx, Ry |
| B, secondary | Pilot that centres the closure in the case bore | A ring at the pilot's maximum material boundary | Tx, Ty |
| C, tertiary | Dowel hole that clocks it | A pin in the hole | Rz |

- **Follow the assembly.** What touches first and holds most is primary; what centres is secondary; what clocks is
  tertiary.
- **Precedence changes the answer.** With the pilot primary and the face secondary, a face that is not square to the
  pilot would make the part tilt on the gauge, and everything measured from it would move.
- **Control the datum features themselves.** Flatness on A, perpendicularity of B to A, position of C to A and B. A
  datum is only as good as the feature that makes it.
- **Mirror the mating part.** The case flange's datums should correspond: its face, its bore, its clocking hole. Then
  tolerances on the two parts can be compared directly in a [tolerance stack](../tolerance-stack/).

## Datum targets

Some surfaces cannot be datums as a whole: a cast or forged surface is too rough, a large one too uneven, a thin one too
flexible. **Datum targets** — points, lines or small areas at basic locations — say exactly where the part is supported.

- **Castings and forgings.** Targets on the raw surfaces hold the part for the first machining operation; the machined
  features then become the datums for everything after. The drawing relates the two so that machined features end up
  where the raw part has material.
- **Thin and non-rigid parts.** A 1.4 mm case wall deflects under its own weight and under a gauge. Put targets at the
  stiff places — flanges, skirt ends, bosses — and say how the part is restrained, so it is measured in the shape it has
  in service.
- **Movable targets** follow a feature that varies, such as a conical or curved surface, by sliding along a defined
  direction until they touch.
- **Target areas** should match the size of the contact the fixture really makes; a point target on a rough surface
  measures the roughness.

## Pattern and composite position

A pattern of holes has two jobs that may need different tolerances: the **pattern as a whole** must sit in the right
place relative to the datums, and the **holes relative to each other** must match the mating pattern.

- **Single-segment position** controls both at once with one tolerance.
- **Simultaneous requirement.** Patterns referenced to the same datums, in the same order and with the same modifiers,
  are one pattern by default in ASME Y14.5 — they are gauged together.
- **Composite position** — a frame with two rows. The upper row locates the pattern to the datums with a larger
  tolerance; the lower row holds the holes to each other with a smaller one, and its datums control only orientation.
- **Two single-segment frames** — the lower row's datums also control location, so the pattern may move only as far as
  the lower tolerance allows.

<CompositeZones />

Composite tolerancing suits a pattern whose position matters less than its internal spacing — two mating parts both
located by their own pilots and dowels, for example. It does **not** suit a bolted closure whose threaded holes are fixed
to the case bore: there the pattern-to-pilot location is part of the fastener clearance (see the
[fastener formulas](../gdt/#mmc-bonus-tolerance-and-virtual-condition)).

## Datum shift at MMB

When a datum feature of size is referenced at **maximum material boundary (MMB)**, the datum is simulated at that fixed
boundary, and as the actual feature departs from it, the part can move on the simulator. Everything referenced to it may
move with it — as a group:

$$
\text{shift available} = MMB - d_{actual}
$$

for an external feature such as a pilot, with $MMB = MMC +$ the feature's own orientation or position tolerance at MMC.
The shift is diametral: the pattern may move half of it in any direction.

- **It moves the whole pattern, not each hole.** It is not added to each hole's tolerance like a bonus.
- **It is free tolerance only if the assembly has it too.** If the pilot sits in its mating bore with at least that
  much clearance, the assembled part can shift as far as the gauged one. If the fit is tight, reference the pilot
  regardless of material boundary (RMB) instead.
- **A functional gauge applies it automatically**; a CMM applies it only if it is programmed to.

## Checking it: functional gauges and CMMs

A **functional gauge** is the assembly in hardware: a plate for A, a ring at the pilot's MMB for B, a pin for C, and
pins at the holes' virtual condition on the basic pattern. If the part fits, it conforms — bonus and datum shift
included. It is fast and unambiguous, but gives no numbers, cannot check anything at RFS, and its own tolerance and wear
come out of the part's under an absolute policy (ASME Y14.43).

A **CMM** measures points and computes. Two things decide whether it agrees with the gauge:

- **How it builds the datums.** ASME Y14.5 datums come from the high points — a tangent plane, the largest inscribed or
  smallest circumscribed cylinder. A least-squares fit sits inside the material and can pass or fail the same part
  differently.
- **Whether it uses the shift.** With a datum at MMB, the pattern must be fitted within the shift available. A program
  that holds the pattern to the datum feature's actual axis rejects parts the drawing accepts.

| Disagreement | Usual cause |
| --- | --- |
| Gauge accepts, CMM rejects | Datum shift or bonus not applied; least-squares datums; the CMM evaluating to a different datum order |
| CMM accepts, gauge rejects | Gauge tolerance or wear; burrs; form error the CMM fit ignored; datum not simulated at MMB |
| Two CMMs disagree | Different datum fits, probing strategies or point densities — the method belongs in the report |

## Worked example — the closure the CMM rejected

The aft closure from the [GD&T page](../gdt/#worked-example--the-aft-closure-its-bolts-and-the-nozzle-seal-groove),
with illustrative inspection data — the calculator's starting values. The gauge-tolerance policy of 5% is **for this
example only**; press **Load worked example** to apply it in the gauge tab.

1. **The frame.** A, the seating face; B, the pilot ⌀255.80–255.90, perpendicular to A within ⌀0.02 at MMC; C, the
   dowel hole. Thirty ⌀11.0–11.2 holes on a basic ⌀285 circle: position ⌀0.5 at MMC to A, B at MMB, C.
2. **The CMM report.** Holes 8, 10 and 27 measure ⌀0.529, 0.525 and 0.521 against the ⌀0.52, 0.51 and 0.52 their
   sizes allow, bonus included. Rejected. The functional gauge accepts the same part.
3. **Why.** The CMM program held the pattern to the pilot's actual axis. But B is at MMB: its boundary is
   $255.90 + 0.02 = 255.92$, the pilot measures ⌀255.83, so the pattern may shift by ⌀0.09 — 0.045 mm in any
   direction. Fitted within that, the best move is (0.042, −0.015) mm and the worst hole uses **87%** of its zone. The
   part conforms. The gauge found the same answer by letting the pilot settle in its ⌀255.92 ring.
4. **Is the shift honest?** The flange bore the pilot fits in service is ⌀256.00–256.05 (illustrative): at least 0.10
   of clearance, more than the 0.09 shift. The closure moves as far on the case as on the gauge, and the threaded holes
   are located to that bore, so the bolts still start. Had the pilot been a close fit, MMB would have granted tolerance
   the assembly cannot use.
5. **The gauge.** Pins ⌀10.500 at the holes' virtual condition; a ring ⌀255.920 at the pilot's MMB; a plate for A. C
   at RFS cannot be gauged with a fixed pin — use an expanding pin, or reference C at MMB if its fit allows. With 5%
   gauge tolerance, the pins are ⌀10.500–10.535: the gauge never accepts a bad part, and takes up to 0.035 mm from each
   hole to guarantee it.
6. **The fix is in the CMM program, not the part.** Construct A from the high points, simulate B at MMB with the shift
   allowed, and fit the pattern within it. Report the method with the result.

## Where this stops

- **Translation-only datum shift** in the calculator, with C at RFS. A tertiary datum at MMB adds a rotational shift.
- **Equally spaced patterns** for the composite rotation fit; uneven patterns need their own coordinates.
- **A minimax search, not a certified CMM algorithm.** Inspection software must follow ASME Y14.5.1 and be verified
  (ISO 10360 for the machine).
- **ASME conventions.** ISO's pattern and combined zones (ISO 5458) and datum rules (ISO 5459) differ in detail.

## On the drawing

- **Datums from the assembly**, in its order, each datum feature with its own form or orientation control.
- **MMB only where the mating fit gives the same freedom** — otherwise RMB.
- **Datum targets** for cast, forged and thin parts, with restraint notes for non-rigid ones.
- **Composite frames** only where pattern location and spacing really differ in importance.
- **Gauge and CMM methods** agreed with inspection: datum simulation, fitting, and whether shift is applied.
