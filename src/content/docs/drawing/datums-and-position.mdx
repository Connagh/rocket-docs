## Why this matters

Two inspectors can measure the same part with different datum set-ups and get two different results. The two
measurements can both be correct. The datums decide what the tolerances mean. If you select the datums from the
machining set-up, the drawing controls what was easy to measure. If you select them from the assembly, the drawing
controls what makes the part operate.

Most disagreements between a gauge and a coordinate-measuring machine (CMM), or between a supplier and a customer, come
from the datums. The causes are their order, how they are simulated, and whether a datum feature at maximum material
boundary could shift. It costs less to get the datums correct on the drawing, and to make sure that inspection does
what the drawing says, than to make the part again.

## Building a datum reference frame from function

A rigid part has six degrees of freedom: three translations and three rotations. A datum reference frame removes them
in order of precedence. This order must follow the sequence in which the part is assembled.

<DatumDof />

| Datum | Closure feature | Simulated by | Removes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A, primary | Seating face on the case flange | A surface plate that touches the high points | Tz, Rx, Ry |
| B, secondary | Pilot that centres the closure in the case bore | A ring at the maximum material boundary of the pilot | Tx, Ty |
| C, tertiary | Dowel hole that clocks it | A pin in the hole | Rz |

- **Follow the assembly.** The feature that touches first and holds most is primary. The feature that centres is
  secondary. The feature that clocks is tertiary.
- **Precedence changes the result.** If the pilot is primary and the face is secondary, a face that is not square to
  the pilot makes the part tilt on the gauge. All the measurements from it then move.
- **Control the datum features.** Put flatness on A, perpendicularity of B to A, and position of C to A and B. A datum
  is only as good as the feature that makes it.
- **Use the same datums as the mating part.** The datums of the case flange must agree: its face, its bore and its
  clocking hole. You can then compare the tolerances on the two parts directly in a
  [tolerance stack](../tolerance-stack/).

## Datum targets

Some surfaces cannot be datums as a whole. A cast or forged surface is too rough, a large surface is not sufficiently
even, and a thin surface is too flexible. **Datum targets** are points, lines or small areas at basic locations. They
tell exactly where the part is supported.

- **Castings and forgings.** Targets on the raw surfaces hold the part for the first machining operation. The machined
  features then become the datums for all subsequent operations. The drawing relates the two, so that the machined
  features are where the raw part has material.
- **Thin and non-rigid parts.** A 1.4 mm case wall bends under its own weight and under a gauge. Put targets at the
  stiff locations (flanges, skirt ends and bosses). Tell how to restrain the part, so that you measure it in the shape
  that it has in service.
- **Movable targets** follow a feature that changes, such as a conical or curved surface. They slide along a defined
  direction until they touch.
- **Target areas** must have the same size as the real contact of the fixture. A point target on a rough surface
  measures the roughness.

## Pattern and composite position

A pattern of holes has two functions, which can need different tolerances. The **pattern as a whole** must be in the
correct position relative to the datums. The **holes relative to each other** must agree with the mating pattern.

- **Single-segment position** controls the two functions with one tolerance.
- **Simultaneous requirement.** In ASME Y14.5, patterns that refer to the same datums, in the same order and with the
  same modifiers, are one pattern by default. You gauge them together.
- **Composite position.** The frame has two rows. The upper row locates the pattern to the datums with a larger
  tolerance. The lower row holds the holes to each other with a smaller tolerance, and its datums control only
  orientation.
- **Two single-segment frames.** The datums of the lower row also control location. Thus the pattern can move only as
  far as the lower tolerance permits.

<CompositeZones />

Composite tolerancing is applicable to a pattern whose position is less important than its internal spacing. An
example is two mating parts that each have their own pilots and dowels. It is **not** applicable to a bolted closure
whose threaded holes are fixed to the case bore. There, the location of the pattern to the pilot is part of the
fastener clearance (see the [fastener formulas](../gdt/#mmc-bonus-tolerance-and-virtual-condition)).

## Datum shift at MMB

When a datum feature of size is at **maximum material boundary (MMB)**, the simulator is at that fixed boundary. As the
actual feature moves away from the boundary, the part can move on the simulator. All the features that refer to it can
move with it, as a group:

$$
\text{shift available} = MMB - d_{actual}
$$

This is for an external feature such as a pilot, where $MMB = MMC +$ the orientation or position tolerance of the
feature at MMC. The shift is diametral: the pattern can move half of it in any direction.

- **The shift moves the whole pattern, not each hole.** Do not add it to the tolerance of each hole as a bonus.
- **The shift is only free tolerance if the assembly also has it.** If the pilot sits in its mating bore with at least
  that clearance, the assembled part can shift as far as the gauged part. If the fit is tight, refer to the pilot
  regardless of material boundary (RMB).
- **A functional gauge applies the shift automatically.** A CMM applies it only if its program does so.

## Checking it: functional gauges and CMMs

A **functional gauge** is the assembly in hardware: a plate for A, a ring at the MMB of the pilot for B, a pin for C,
and pins at the virtual condition of the holes on the basic pattern. If the part fits, it conforms, with the bonus and
the datum shift included. The gauge is fast and its result is clear. But it gives no numbers and it cannot check a
feature at RFS. Under an absolute policy, the tolerance and wear of the gauge come out of the tolerance of the part
(ASME Y14.43).

A **CMM** measures points and calculates. Two things decide if it agrees with the gauge:

- **How it makes the datums.** ASME Y14.5 datums come from the high points: a tangent plane, or the largest inscribed
  or smallest circumscribed cylinder. A least-squares fit is inside the material, and it can give a different result
  for the same part.
- **If it uses the shift.** With a datum at MMB, the pattern must be fitted within the available shift. A program that
  holds the pattern to the actual axis of the datum feature rejects parts that the drawing accepts.

| Disagreement | Usual cause |
| --- | --- |
| Gauge accepts, CMM rejects | Datum shift or bonus not applied; least-squares datums; the CMM uses a different datum order |
| CMM accepts, gauge rejects | Gauge tolerance or wear; burrs; form error that the CMM fit ignored; datum not simulated at MMB |
| Two CMMs disagree | Different datum fits, probing methods or point densities. Put the method in the report |

## Worked example: the closure the CMM rejected

This is the aft closure from the [GD&T page](../gdt/#worked-example-the-aft-closure-its-bolts-and-the-nozzle-seal-groove),
with illustrative inspection data. These are the starting values of the calculator. The gauge-tolerance policy of 5% is
**for this example only**. Push **Load worked example** to apply it in the gauge tab.

1. **The frame.** A is the seating face. B is the pilot, ⌀255.80–255.90, perpendicular to A within ⌀0.02 at MMC. C is
   the dowel hole. There are thirty ⌀11.0–11.2 holes on a basic ⌀285 circle, with position ⌀0.5 at MMC to A, B at
   MMB, C.
2. **The CMM report.** Holes 8, 10 and 27 measure ⌀0.529, 0.525 and 0.521. Their sizes permit ⌀0.52, 0.51 and 0.52,
   with the bonus included. The CMM rejects the part. The functional gauge accepts the same part.
3. **The cause.** The CMM program held the pattern to the actual axis of the pilot. But B is at MMB. Its boundary is
   $255.90 + 0.02 = 255.92$ and the pilot measures ⌀255.83, so the pattern can shift by ⌀0.09. This is 0.045 mm in any
   direction. With the pattern fitted within this shift, the best movement is (0.042, −0.015) mm, and the worst hole
   uses **87%** of its zone. The part conforms. The gauge got the same result, because the pilot moved into its
   correct position in the ⌀255.92 ring.
4. **Is the shift real?** In service, the pilot fits in a flange bore of ⌀256.00–256.05 (illustrative). This gives a
   clearance of at least 0.10, which is more than the 0.09 shift. The closure moves as far on the case as on the gauge,
   and the threaded holes are located to that bore, so the bolts still start. If the pilot were a close fit, MMB would
   give tolerance that the assembly cannot use.
5. **The gauge.** It has pins of ⌀10.500 at the virtual condition of the holes, a ring of ⌀255.920 at the MMB of the
   pilot, and a plate for A. You cannot gauge C at RFS with a fixed pin. Use an expanding pin, or refer to C at MMB if
   its fit permits. With a gauge tolerance of 5%, the pins are ⌀10.500–10.535. The gauge never accepts a bad part. To
   make sure of this, it takes up to 0.035 mm from each hole.
6. **Correct the CMM program, not the part.** Make A from the high points, simulate B at MMB with the shift permitted,
   and fit the pattern within it. Put the method in the report with the result.

## Where this stops

- **Translation-only datum shift** in the calculator, with C at RFS. A tertiary datum at MMB adds a rotational shift.
- **Equally spaced patterns** for the composite rotation fit. Patterns with unequal spacing need their own coordinates.
- **A minimax search, not a certified CMM algorithm.** Inspection software must follow ASME Y14.5.1 and must be verified
  (ISO 10360 for the machine).
- **ASME conventions.** The ISO rules for patterns and combined zones (ISO 5458) and datums (ISO 5459) are different in
  some details.

## On the drawing

- **Datums from the assembly,** in the assembly sequence, each datum feature with its own form or orientation control.
- **MMB only where the mating fit gives the same freedom.** If it does not, use RMB.
- **Datum targets** for cast, forged and thin parts, with restraint notes for non-rigid parts.
- **Composite frames** only where the location of the pattern and its spacing really have different importance.
- **Gauge and CMM methods** agreed with inspection: datum simulation, fitting, and if the shift is applied.
