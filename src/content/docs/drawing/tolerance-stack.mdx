## Why this matters

Every drawing on a motor can be right and the assembly still wrong. An O-ring groove, a bore and a pilot can each be in
tolerance and leave the ring with too little squeeze. A case, a closure and a nozzle can each pass inspection and still
point the thrust off the centre of gravity. The individual drawings cannot show this. The stack — the chain of
dimensions that decides one gap — is where it shows.

A stack answers three questions: what the gap can be, which tolerances decide it, and whether the answer must hold for
every assembly or only for nearly all of them.

<Analogy title="Books on a shelf">
  Each book on a shelf is a little thicker or thinner than the catalogue says. Pick them at random and the errors mostly
  cancel: the row ends close to where you expect. It overflows only if every book happens to be a fat one — possible,
  but rare. Unless someone has sorted them, or they all came from one printing run: then the errors line up, and "rare"
  becomes "every time". That is the whole of tolerance stacking: adding the worst case, adding what usually happens, and
  knowing which one your parts allow.
</Analogy>

## The loop diagram

1. **Name the result and its requirement** — a gap, a squeeze, an offset, and the limit it must meet.
2. **Start at one side of the gap** and walk to the other through the parts that touch, one arrow per dimension. Pick
   a positive direction; arrows the other way are negative.
3. **Go through datums and contact points.** Each part's dimensions run from its datums, and parts meet where they touch,
   so the loop goes datum to datum. If the drawing dimensions a feature from somewhere else, the loop has to go the long
   way round, collecting more tolerance — usually a sign the datums are wrong
   ([Datums & position](../datums-and-position/)).
4. **Decide where the clearances sit.** Where a fit lets a part move, push it the way that is worst for this result.
   The loop goes through the contact point.
5. **Convert every dimension to mean ± equal half-tolerance**, and give it a sensitivity: ±1 for a length in the loop,
   ±½ for a diameter whose radius is in it, $\cos\theta$ for one at an angle.

For the nozzle seal on the [O-ring page](../../seals/o-ring-design/#worked-example--a-nozzle-to-case-radial-seal) —
the plug pushed across onto the far wall of the bore, the groove offset by its runout $e$ — the deepest gland is:

$$
g = D_{bore} - \tfrac{1}{2}D_{pilot} - \tfrac{1}{2}D_{root} + e
$$

The squeeze is the ring's section less $g$. On the tight side the bore drops out entirely: the plug sits on it.

## Worst case and RSS

With the result $y = \sum s_i x_i$, each contributor's half-tolerance $t_i$ and sensitivity $s_i$:

$$
t_{WC} = \sum |s_i|\,t_i
$$

$$
t_{RSS} = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum (s_i t_i)^2}
$$

- **Worst case** holds for every assembly made of in-tolerance parts, whatever their distributions. It grows in
  proportion to the number of contributors.
- **RSS** assumes every part is independent, normal, centred, and holds its tolerance at ±3σ. It grows only as the square
  root of the number of contributors, so it is much tighter on long stacks — and about 0.27% of assemblies fall outside
  its band even when every assumption holds.
- **Which one** is a decision. Use the worst case where a single failure is unacceptable and would not be found at
  assembly, where few parts are made, or where the statistics cannot be backed by process data. A statistical stack suits
  many contributors, quantity production and failures that are caught or tolerable at the predicted rate.

## GD&T and fits in a stack

| On the drawing | In the stack |
| --- | --- |
| Size ⌀a–b, radius in the loop | mean $(a+b)/4$ ± $(b-a)/4$; sensitivity ½ on the diameter |
| Position ⌀t at RFS, coaxiality ⌀t, runout t | an offset of 0 ± t/2 in the loop's direction |
| Position ⌀t at MMC, to a hole's surface | size and position together: the surface lies between the virtual and resultant conditions, so radius $(VC + RC)/4$ ± $(RC - VC)/4$ |
| Profile of a surface, total width t | 0 ± t/2 (equal-bilateral), or the zone's own offset if unequal |
| Flatness, parallelism or perpendicularity t | 0 to t in a gap; as a tilt, t over the length it acts on, carried to the lever arm |
| A clearance fit | an assembly shift: either the loop goes through the contact point (diameters at ±½), or a shift up to $c_{max}/2$ |
| A datum feature at MMB | an extra shift, up to half the difference between its boundary and its actual size |
| Temperature | each part's growth $\alpha L \Delta T$ as an extra arrow — check every temperature, not just 20 °C |

The size and geometric tolerances of one feature are not independent when a material condition modifier links them;
use the boundaries ([GD&T](../gdt/#mmc-bonus-tolerance-and-virtual-condition)), not both rows.

## Statistical stacks: Cpk and Monte Carlo

**Cpk-based.** Real processes are not all at Cpk 1. A part made at capability $C_{pk}$ has, conservatively,
$\sigma_i = t_i / (3\,C_{pk,i})$, and the assembly's σ and capability follow, with μ its mean:

$$
\sigma_y = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum (s_i \sigma_i)^2}
$$

$$
C_{pk,y} = \frac{\min(USL - \mu,\ \mu - LSL)}{3\,\sigma_y}
$$

Plain RSS is the case where every $C_{pk} = 1$. Treating a process that is off-centre ($C_{pk} < C_p$) this way spreads
its shift as if it were random; if the shift is the same on every part, add it worst case instead. A part that is
uniform across its tolerance — tool wear between resets, a feature nobody measures — has $\sigma = t/\sqrt{3}$, 1.7 times
the normal value. Where the parts' capability is unknown, a traditional allowance multiplies RSS by 1.5 (Bender).

**Monte Carlo.** Draw every part from its distribution, compute the result through the real function — nonlinear if it
is, a squeeze as a percentage of the drawn section, a vector sum — and count the failures. It needs no normal
assumption, but it needs enough samples: to see a one-in-10,000 failure, run hundreds of thousands. Its answer is only
as good as the distributions put in.

**Correlation.** RSS and Monte Carlo assume independent parts. Two features turned in one setup, cut by one tool or
taken from one batch move together; group them and add them worst case.

## Radial stacks

<StackRadial />

Coaxiality, position and clearance shifts are offsets in a plane, in any direction. They add as vectors:

- **The worst case** is $\sum r_i$, every offset in line.
- **The RSS of the radii** $\sqrt{\sum r_i^2}$ is not a bound. It treats each offset as a ±3σ dimension, but a
  clearance taken up fully in a random direction is nothing like that: with two equal ones, half of all assemblies exceed
  it.
- **Many random offsets** tend to a Rayleigh distribution, with $\sigma^2 = \sum \sigma_i^2$ in each direction —
  $r^2/4$ for an offset anywhere in a zone, $r^2/2$ for a clearance fully taken up — so the radius covering a fraction
  $p$ is $\sigma\sqrt{-2\ln(1-p)}$. With few offsets, use Monte Carlo.
- **A common direction** turns random offsets into arithmetic ones. Gravity on a horizontal assembly stand takes every
  clearance up downwards; so does a side load while the bolts are tightened.
- **Tilts** are offsets too: a tolerance $t$ over a length $l$ tilts an axis by $t/l$, which moves it $tL/l$ at a
  lever arm $L$.

For a nozzle, the result that matters is the **thrust line's offset from the centre of gravity**, $d$. It gives a
disturbing moment $M = F d$ that the vehicle's control must trim, and folds the nozzle's lateral offset and its tilt into
one number.

## Worked example — seal squeeze and nozzle alignment

### The nozzle seal

The [O-ring page's](../../seals/o-ring-design/#worked-example--a-nozzle-to-case-radial-seal) radial seal, with the groove
runout the [GD&T page](../gdt/#worked-example--the-aft-closure-its-bolts-and-the-nozzle-seal-groove) put on the drawing:

| Arrow | Limits (mm) | Sensitivity | Mean ± half |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| O-ring section | 5.20–5.46 | +1 | 5.330 ± 0.130 |
| Case bore ⌀ (H8) | 150.000–150.063 | −1 | 150.0315 ± 0.0315 |
| Plug pilot ⌀ | 149.860–149.900 | +½ | 149.880 ± 0.020 |
| Groove root ⌀ | 141.45–141.55 | +½ | 141.500 ± 0.050 |
| Root runout 0.05 to the pilot | — | −1 | 0 ± 0.025 |

1. **Nominal and worst case.** The squeeze is 0.989 mm nominal, and **0.767 mm** at worst — 14.75% of the smallest
   ring, the GD&T page's figure. The requirement here is 0.74 mm, the level at which the O-ring page's stretch, −40 °C and
   20% compression set leave its 10% floor. The worst case passes, by 0.027 mm.
2. **RSS.** ±0.139 mm, so **0.850 mm** at the low end — and the O-ring's own section is **88% of the variance**. Halving
   all four machined tolerances moves that by less than 0.01 mm; a ring held to ±0.08 on its section would move it
   about 0.05 mm. The machining is not where this stack is decided.
3. **Cpk.** With every part at Cpk 1.33, σ = 0.035 mm and the assembly's Cpk against the 0.74 mm floor is 2.38. None of
   the Monte Carlo's 100,000 assemblies falls below it.
4. **The tempting saving.** Drop the runout to the general 0.2 and the worst case becomes **0.692 mm** — 9.2% at end of
   life, below the floor. RSS says 0.819 mm and passes. Nobody measures a general tolerance, so model the runout as
   uniform (“-0.1, 0.1, u”): it becomes 74% of the variance, the assembly's Cpk falls to 1.23, and the Monte Carlo
   finds one assembly in 100,000 below the floor. (The normal estimate says one in 10,000; a uniform part has no tails,
   which the normal approximation cannot see.)
5. **Decision: worst case, and keep the 0.05.** A seal below its floor cannot be seen at assembly, and its failure is a
   burn-through, on a product made in tens. Statistics would accept a risk the programme should not. The runout costs
   nothing if the groove and the pilot are turned in one setup.

### The nozzle's alignment

The thrust line's offset at the vehicle's centre of gravity, 700 mm forward of the aft joint at ignition (illustrative),
with the [load-path page's](../../joints/load-paths/#worked-example--the-300-mm-motors-load-path) 29.5 kN of thrust. The
closure pilot fit is the [datums page's](../datums-and-position/#worked-example--the-closure-the-cmm-rejected); the other
tolerances are illustrative. The requirement: 99.73% of motors within 0.45 mm.

| Offset | Tolerance | Kind | At the CG (mm) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Case flange bore to the case axis | coaxiality ⌀0.10 | zone | 0.050 |
| Case flange face | perpendicularity 0.05 over ⌀300 | zone, tilt | 0.117 |
| Closure pilot in the flange bore | 0.10–0.25 clearance | shift | 0.125 |
| Nozzle bore in the closure | position ⌀0.10 | zone | 0.050 |
| Nozzle seat face | perpendicularity 0.03 over ⌀180 | zone, tilt | 0.117 |
| Nozzle pilot in its bore | 0.100–0.203 clearance | shift | 0.102 |
| Throat to the nozzle pilot | coaxiality ⌀0.10 | zone | 0.050 |

1. **Worst case: 0.610 mm** — 18.0 N·m, the same as tilting the thrust 0.050°. It fails 0.45 mm. The two squareness
   tilts and the closure clearance are 58% of it.
2. **RSS of the radii: 0.246 mm.** It looks like an easy pass — and **26% of motors exceed it**. Do not use it.
3. **Monte Carlo, assembled vertically,** with both clearance shifts in random directions: 99.73% of motors lie within
   **0.442 mm** (13.1 N·m). It passes, just: 0.20% exceed 0.45.
4. **Assembled horizontally,** the nozzle and the closure settle onto the bottom of their clearances before the bolts are
   torqued — change both shifts to “fixed”. The 99.73% value grows to **0.474 mm** and 0.81% of motors exceed the limit.
   It fails, though no part has changed.
5. **The fix.** Tighten the nozzle pilot to H7/g6 ([Fits & limits](../fits-and-limits/)), a 0.0395 mm shift: even
   horizontal, 99.73% are within **0.415 mm**. Or centre the nozzle in a fixture while torquing. Then re-run the seal
   stack, because the plug pilot is one of its arrows, and measure each motor's nozzle alignment anyway — a statistical
   requirement is best closed by inspection.

## Where this stops

- **Independent contributors.** Correlated features — one setup, one tool, one batch — must be grouped by hand.
- **Linear sensitivities** in the linear tab; nonlinear results need their own function, as the library's Monte Carlo
  allows.
- **One lever arm for every tilt** in the radial tab. Tilts that pivot far apart need their own, and the centre of
  gravity moves as propellant burns.
- **Geometry only.** Loads, pressure, deflection and temperature change gaps too — the joint opening on the O-ring page,
  [Differential expansion](../../joints/differential-expansion/) for temperature. Thrust misalignment also comes from
  the flow and the nozzle's erosion, which tolerances do not capture.
- **Normal and uniform parts.** Real distributions — skewed, truncated by inspection, bimodal from two machines — belong
  in a Monte Carlo of their own, from measured data.

## On the drawing

- **Datums chosen so the loop is short,** each critical dimension from the datum it stacks from.
- **The tolerances the stack depends on,** and none left to the general tolerances by accident — like the groove runout.
- **Assembly instructions** where the stack assumes a position: centring, orientation, the order of tightening.
- **The stack itself** in the design record: the loop, the method, the distributions assumed and the data behind them.
- **Inspection** of the assembled result where the requirement is statistical — alignment, a gap, a squeeze measured or
  gauged.
