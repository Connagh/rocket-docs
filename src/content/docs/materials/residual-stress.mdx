## Why this matters

Every weld, every machined or ground surface, every quenched or straightened part carries stresses that no load
analysis shows. They can reach yield. They add to the service stress at every point, and they never switch off:

- **Fatigue** — residual tension shortens life; residual compression from peening or rolling lengthens it, often
  several times.
- **Fracture** — residual tension shrinks the flaw a part can tolerate.
- **Stress-corrosion cracking** — it needs only sustained tension, and residual stress is sustained by definition. A
  high-strength steel part can crack in storage with no load on it.
- **Distortion** — machine away a stressed layer and the part moves.

On a solid motor: the welds in a high-strength steel case, the ground surfaces near them, rolled threads, peened
fillets, quenched aluminium skirts and closures — and the thermal residual stresses in composite cases, bonded joints
and the propellant grain itself.

## What it is

A residual stress is the elastic response to a **misfit**. Some region has been yielded, transformed or shrunk so that
it no longer fits the material around it, and the surroundings force it to fit. With no external load, the stresses
must balance across any cut through the part — no net force and no net moment:

$$
\int_A \sigma_{res}\,dA = 0
$$

$$
\int_A \sigma_{res}\,y\,dA = 0
$$

So tension somewhere always means compression somewhere else — and when machining removes part of the section, the
remainder rebalances and the part distorts.

<Analogy title="Toughened glass">
  A glass panel is heated and its surfaces are blasted with cold air. The surfaces set first; the hot core then cools
  and shrinks, pulling them into compression and leaving itself in tension. A scratch in the surface now has to fight
  that compression before it can grow, so the glass is several times stronger. But the stored energy is still there:
  break through to the core and the whole panel shatters into pebbles. Prince Rupert's drop is the same trick, in one
  drop of glass.
</Analogy>

## Where it comes from

| Process | Where | Sign and size | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Welding | Weld and heat-affected zone | Tension near yield along the weld; compression away from it | Lower across the weld. Phase changes in steels alter the pattern |
| Machining | A thin surface layer, up to a few tenths of a millimetre | Compression from sharp tools and light cuts; tension from heat | Abusive grinding leaves tension and a burned, re-hardened layer |
| Quenching | Through the section | Non-transforming alloys: surface compression, core tension | Thick aluminium sections distort when machined; transforming steels can reverse the pattern |
| Shot peening, deep rolling, thread rolling, cold expansion of holes | Surface, roughly 0.1–1 mm deep | Compression, a large fraction of yield | Balanced by a small tension below; lost if machined off afterwards |
| Straightening and forming | Where it bent | Tension on one side | Straightening hardened high-strength steel is restricted for this reason |
| Autofrettage | Bore of a thick cylinder | Hoop compression at the bore | Yield the inner wall once, then unload |
| Thermal mismatch | Composites at cure, bonded joints, the propellant grain | Either sign | See [Differential expansion](../../joints/differential-expansion/) and [Propellant grain](../../motor/propellant-grain/) |

Assembly stresses act the same way: an interference-fit bush or an over-tightened fitting holds a sustained tension that
no service load explains.

## How it adds to service stress

While everything is elastic, residual and service stresses simply add. Three consequences follow.

**Static strength of a ductile part barely changes.** The residual stress balances itself, so as the part approaches
collapse it yields away. Brittle parts, buckling and distortion are different: there it stays.

**Yield caps it.** If service stress plus residual stress would pass yield, the excess yields away, and less residual
stress is left afterwards:

$$
\sigma'_{res} = \min\left(\sigma_{res},\; F_{ty} - \sigma_{max}\right)
$$

with the matching limit in compression. One large load — a proof test, an overload — is therefore a mechanical stress
relief, and it can leave compression at notches that yielded. It also means large fatigue cycles wash residual stress
out: it does most for high-cycle fatigue.

**In fatigue it is a mean stress.** At the surface where cracks start, the residual stress adds to the service mean:

$$
\sigma_{m,eff} = \sigma_m + \sigma'_{res}
$$

then Goodman or another mean-stress rule, as on the [Fatigue](../../fundamentals/fatigue/) page. Tension pushes the
cycle towards failure. Compression pulls it away — this page, like the fatigue page, credits compression only as far
as it cancels tensile mean stress.

**In fracture it is crack-driving stress, load or no load:**

$$
K = Y\,(\sigma + \sigma_{res})\sqrt{\pi a}
$$

The critical flaw depth goes as $1/(\sigma + \sigma_{res})^2$, so even a modest residual stress costs a lot of
tolerable flaw size ([Leak before break](../../verify/leak-before-break/)). Fitness-for-service procedures such as
BS 7910 and API 579-1 prescribe residual stresses where they are not measured — up to yield in as-welded joints, a
fraction of it after post-weld heat treatment.

**In stress corrosion it is often the whole story.** Stress-corrosion cracking needs a susceptible material, an
environment and sustained tension. Residual tension supplies the last at all times: a flaw grows once $K$ from the
residual stress alone exceeds $K_{ISCC}$. High-strength steels and 7075-T6 aluminium loaded across its short-transverse
grain are the classic cases — the reason for over-aged tempers such as T73 and T7351.

## Autofrettage

A thick cylinder pressurised beyond its first-yield pressure yields from the bore outwards to a radius $c$. On
unloading, the elastic outer wall squeezes the yielded inner wall, leaving the bore in hoop compression. For a bore of
radius $a$ and outside radius $b$ (Hill, Tresca):

$$
p_A = F_{ty}\left[\ln\frac{c}{a} + \frac{b^2 - c^2}{2b^2}\right]
$$

from first yield at $F_{ty}(b^2-a^2)/2b^2$ to full overstrain at $F_{ty}\ln(b/a)$. In service, pressure cycles the
bore around a compressive mean, so bore cracks barely open, and the peak stress moves out to the edge of the yielded
zone. Too much overstrain on a thick wall yields the bore in **reverse** on unloading — sooner in real steels, whose
reverse yield strength falls after overstrain (the Bauschinger effect). It is used on gun tubes, high-pressure pumps
and tubing, and on the metal liners of composite-overwrapped pressure vessels: the liner is yielded once so that it
works elastically, in compression at zero pressure, ever after.

## Measuring it

| Method | Depth | Part survives? | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| X-ray diffraction | Tens of micrometres | Yes | Surface lattice strain; profiles by electropolishing layers off (EN 15305) |
| Hole drilling | To about 1–2 mm | Small hole | Strain-gauge rosette around a drilled hole (ASTM E837) |
| Neutron diffraction | Centimetres | Yes | At a neutron source; through-section maps (ISO 21432) |
| Contour method | Whole cut plane | No | Cut the part, measure how the cut face deforms |
| Slitting, ring-core, deep-hole drilling | Through thickness | Partly or no | Strain released by a cut or core |
| Barkhausen noise, temper etch | Surface | Yes | Screening for grinding burns, not values |

Almen strips control shot-peening intensity (SAE J442 and J443); they measure the process, not the part. Every
measurement averages over its own gauge volume, and steep gradients near the surface or a weld need the method chosen
for them.

## Relieving and controlling it

- **Thermal stress relief.** At temperature the yield strength falls and creep relaxes the stress; slow cooling keeps
  it from coming back. Stay below the tempering temperature of quenched-and-tempered steels, or they soften. The
  temperature and time come from the material specification.
- **Weld, then heat-treat.** High-strength steel cases are commonly welded before their final hardening and
  tempering, which also relieves the welds. A repair weld after heat treatment brings back near-yield tension and an
  untempered heat-affected zone, so it needs the full heat treatment again — or is not allowed.
- **Mechanical relief.** Aluminium plate and forgings are stretched or compressed a few per cent after quenching (the
  T651, T7351 and T652 tempers) because heating them would over-age the alloy. A proof test relieves tensile residual
  stress where it yields.
- **Process control.** Welding sequence and heat input; sharp tools and light finishing cuts; grinding with controlled
  feeds and coolant, inspected for burns. For distortion-prone parts: rough, stress-relieve, then finish.
- **Put compression where cracks start.** Shot peening, laser peening, deep rolling, threads rolled after heat
  treatment, cold-expanded holes. Peen after the last machining, and check at temperature — peened layers relax when
  hot.

## Worked example — residual stress in the 300 mm motor's hardware

The starting values in each tab of the calculator are this example's. The residual stresses here are **assumed for
the example**; on real hardware they are measured or set by your assessment procedure.

1. **The case weld.** At MEOP the D6AC case wall carries 1094 MPa of hoop stress, and $K_{IC} = 70$ MPa√m
   ([Leak before break](../../verify/leak-before-break/)). A long, shallow surface flaw along a longitudinal weld
   ($Y = 1.12$) goes critical at $\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{70}{1.12 \times 1094}\right)^2 = 1.04$ mm deep. Assume
   290 MPa of residual stress — 20% of yield — survives the final heat treatment across the weld. The critical depth
   falls to **0.65 mm**. The 11 MPa proof test helps: proof stress plus residual, $1204 + 290$, passes the 1450 MPa
   yield, so the residual stress relaxes to $1450 - 1204 = 246$ MPa and the critical depth recovers to **0.69 mm**.
   A repair weld after heat treatment would bring back near-yield tension in an untempered heat-affected zone.
2. **The case in storage.** With the case unpressurised, the 246 MPa still acts. At a 0.5 mm flaw it sustains
   $K = 1.12 \times 246 \times \sqrt{\pi \times 0.0005} = 10.9$ MPa√m — permanently. For high-strength steels in
   moist or salty air, $K_{ISCC}$ can be a small fraction of $K_{IC}$. If it is below 10.9 MPa√m for this steel, heat
   treatment and storage environment, that flaw grows with no pressure in the case. Enter your $K_{ISCC}$ in the
   **Fracture & SCC** tab to see the deepest flaw that is safe in storage.
3. **The actuator bracket.** The 4340 bracket from the [Fatigue](../../fundamentals/fatigue/#worked-example--an-actuator-bracket)
   page lasts 6.2 service lives as machined. Its fillet sees local cycles of 444 on 444 MPa in handling and 320 on
   107 MPa in firings.
   - **Shot peened**, with −400 MPa at the surface: the handling mean drops to 44 MPa, the equivalent amplitude from
     680 to 460 MPa, and the handling life rises from 16,000 to 126,000 cycles. Firings gain less: their mean goes
     compressive, and compression gets no credit. The bracket lasts **25 service lives — 4.1 times longer**.
   - **Abusively ground**, leaving +300 MPa: the first handling cycle takes the surface to yield, so the residual
     relaxes to 292 MPa — and stays there. Handling life falls to 1,670 cycles, and the bracket lasts **0.73 service
     lives**. It fails in service, from a surface treatment no drawing shows.
4. **Autofrettage.** Motor cases at 10 MPa never need it, but high-pressure test equipment does. A tube with a 10 mm
   bore and 25 mm outside diameter, of steel with $F_{ty} = 1000$ MPa, works at 300 MPa. Plain, its bore carries
   714 MPa of Tresca stress. Autofrettaged at 650 MPa, it yields the inner 23% of the wall and leaves **−548 MPa** of
   hoop stress at the bore. At working pressure the bore is still in compression (−133 MPa), and the peak stress is
   537 MPa at the edge of the yielded zone — **25% lower**. Pressure cycles at the bore now swing between −548 and
   −133 MPa, so a crack there barely opens. 650 MPa is close to the best for this tube: more overstrain raises the
   peak again.

## Where this stops

- **Elastic–perfectly plastic, uniaxial.** Real relaxation depends on the cyclic stress–strain curve, multiaxial
  stress and the number of cycles; residual stresses also fade at temperature.
- **Uniform residual stress across a flaw.** Real profiles vary steeply with depth, and fitness-for-service procedures
  integrate them through the crack.
- **Autofrettage without the Bauschinger effect**, Tresca and elastic unloading. Codes such as ASME VIII Division 3
  use corrected methods.
- **Values come from measurement or your assessment procedure** — not from typical numbers like the ones in this
  example.

## On the drawing

- **Stress relief** — heat treatment after welding, forming or straightening, with the material specification's
  temperature and time; whether straightening or repair welding is allowed at all.
- **Shot peening** — areas and masking, intensity (Almen), coverage and media to the governing specification (for
  example SAE AMS 2430), and that it follows the last machining.
- **Grinding** — inspection for grinding burns on high-strength steel, and limits on stock removal.
- **Thread rolling after heat treatment** for fatigue-critical fasteners — see [Threads](../../joints/threads/).
- **Stress-relieved tempers** (T651, T7351) for aluminium parts machined from thick stock.
- **Autofrettage pressure** and the verification that follows it, for autofrettaged parts.
