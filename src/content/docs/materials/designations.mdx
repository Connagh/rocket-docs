## Why this matters

A heritage drawing calls up HE30, the supplier quotes EN AW-6082, the US partner says 6061, and the certificate carries
a UNS number. They are related, not identical. Knowing how to read each system lets you follow a material across
drawings, suppliers and countries; knowing where they differ stops a "nearest equivalent" quietly becoming a
non-conformance.

<Analogy title="A person's names on different documents">
  Your passport, your driving licence and your employee badge all identify you, but each carries different details and
  is accepted for different things. A material's designations are the same: EN, UNS and AMS all point at broadly the
  same alloy, but the *specification* — the document — is what sets the limits, the tests and what is accepted.
</Analogy>

## How the numbering systems read

**EN aluminium (EN 573).** `EN AW-6082`: **AW** is aluminium wrought (AC is cast); the four digits follow the
international series (1xxx pure, 2xxx Cu, 5xxx Mg, 6xxx Mg–Si, 7xxx Zn). The temper follows the designation
(`EN AW-6082-T6`) and is defined by EN 515. Composition is EN 573-3; properties come from the product standard —
EN 485 sheet and plate, EN 755 extrusions, EN 754 drawn bar and tube.

**EN steels (EN 10027).** Two parallel systems. The *name* encodes composition: `34CrNiMo6` is about 0.34% carbon with
chromium, nickel and molybdenum, the last number giving the main alloying element's content multiplied by a factor. The
*number* (`1.6582`) is a catalogue entry: 1.xxxx for steels.

**EN stainless (EN 10088).** `X2CrNiMo17-12-2`: **X** marks a high-alloy steel, then carbon ×100, the elements, and their
percentages — this one is 316L, number `1.4404`. Part 2 covers sheet and plate, part 3 bar, rod and sections.

**UNS.** One letter and five digits across all metals: A aluminium, C copper, G carbon and alloy steels, K
miscellaneous steels (D6AC is K24728), N nickel, R reactive and refractory metals including titanium, S stainless.
Often the cleanest way to pin down exactly which grade someone means.

**Aerospace specifications (AMS, BS L-series, AIR).** These are *specifications*, not just names: they fix chemistry,
product form, condition, testing and acceptance. Two L-series specs can be the same alloy and temper with different
tolerances, and stock certified to one does not satisfy a drawing calling the other.

<Aside type="tip" title="Which one goes on the drawing?">
  In the UK, working to BS 8888, the BS EN designation plus the governing product standard is the usual callout — for
  example `EN AW-6082-T6 TO BS EN 755-2`. Add the UNS or AMS reference only where the supply chain genuinely needs it.
  Legacy BS names (HE30, 817M40, 304S11) are for reading old drawings, not writing new ones.
</Aside>
