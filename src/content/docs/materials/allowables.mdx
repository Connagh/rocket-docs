## Why this matters

A datasheet says the alloy has a yield strength of 460 MPa. That is a **typical** value — the middle of the test
results — and about half of all production material is weaker. Put it into a margin calculation and the margin is
fiction. A **design allowable** is a lower bound on the strength of the material that will actually be built, at the
condition it will actually see. Quoting a typical value into a margin is the quiet way to lose a design review.

## The bases

| Basis | Means | Used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A-basis** | 99% of the population exceeds it, with 95% confidence | Single load paths, where one failure is not contained |
| **B-basis** | 90% of the population exceeds it, with 95% confidence | Redundant structure, where load redistributes — if your rules allow it |
| **S-basis** | The specification minimum, with no statistical backing of its own | Often treated like A-basis — check your programme's rules |
| **Typical** | The mean of the test data | Comparing materials. Never a margin |

The A- and B-basis definitions come from aerospace practice (MMPDS for metals, CMH-17 for composites), and civil
aircraft rules (14 CFR 25.613) set when each may be used. Most space and defence programmes follow the same logic.

## The statistics behind them

A basis value is a **one-sided tolerance limit**: the mean of the tests, less a multiple of their standard deviation,

$$
x_\text{basis} = \bar{x} - k\,s
$$

There are two percentages in the definition. The **proportion** (99% or 90%) is how much of the population must lie
above the value. The **confidence** (95%) covers the fact that $\bar{x}$ and $s$ are only estimates from a sample.
With infinite data $k$ would just be the normal-distribution value — 2.326 for 99%, 1.282 for 90%. With a finite
sample it is larger, and it comes from the noncentral $t$-distribution:

$$
k = \frac{t'_{0.95}\!\left(n-1,\; z_p\sqrt{n}\right)}{\sqrt{n}}
$$

| Tests, $n$ | 5 | 10 | 20 | 30 | 100 | ∞ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $k$, B-basis | 3.407 | 2.355 | 1.926 | 1.778 | 1.527 | 1.282 |
| $k$, A-basis | 5.741 | 3.981 | 3.295 | 3.064 | 2.684 | 2.326 |

The calculator computes these exactly; its tests check them against the published tables. CMH-17 also gives
closed-form approximations that agree to within 1% for ten or more tests.

<Analogy title="Sizing a doorway for a whole town">
  You measure twenty people and want a doorway that 99 of every 100 townspeople fit through. The tallest of your
  twenty is not enough — the town holds taller people you did not measure. So you add an allowance, and the fewer people
  you measured, the bigger it must be. A basis value is the same idea turned upside down: a strength that almost all of
  the material you have not tested will still exceed.
</Analogy>

What the statistics assume, and where they go wrong:

- **A normal distribution.** Real data can be skewed. Handbook practice tests the fit and uses Weibull, lognormal or a
  non-parametric method when normal does not fit.
- **One population.** Strength varies between material lots (heats, batches) more than within one. Allowables come
  from several lots, and data are pooled only where statistics allow. A single lot underestimates the scatter.
- **Data that represent production** — the same specification, process, supplier, thickness and orientation as the
  parts that will fly.

## Knockdowns — before the allowable is yours

A basis value applies to the condition it was tested at. To reach the design condition, it is multiplied by
**knockdowns** — property ratios from data, not safety factors:

| Knockdown | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Temperature, and time at temperature | Strength falls with temperature; short-time and long-exposure allowables are different curves |
| Direction | In thick wrought products, short-transverse (through-thickness) strength and ductility can be well below longitudinal |
| Thickness and product form | Quench rate falls with thickness, so heavy sections do not reach thin-section properties; plate, forging and extrusion differ |
| Environment | Moisture and heat ("hot-wet") for composites; corrosion and stress corrosion for metals |
| Joints and welds | Weld efficiency comes from the code and the procedure qualification, not the parent metal |
| Process | Castings and additively manufactured parts carry allowables tied to the qualified process and supplier |

## How the allowable feeds the margin

$$
MS = \frac{\text{allowable}}{FS \times \text{applied stress}} - 1
$$

with the allowable on the right basis, at the design temperature, in the loaded direction, for the product form used.
Yield and ultimate are checked separately, each against its own load level and factor. The factor of safety comes
from your programme's requirements. Every margin report should say **which allowable, from which source, on which
basis, at which condition** — "σ = 880 MPa" on its own will be queried at every review until someone finds where it
came from.

## Worked example — a plate lot on a single load path

Illustrative data: twenty yield-strength tests from one lot of aluminium plate, longitudinal, at room temperature.
Mean 460.0 MPa, standard deviation 9.0 MPa (2.0% scatter), lowest result 444 MPa. These are the calculator's starting
values.

1. **Factors for $n = 20$:** $k_B = 1.926$, $k_A = 3.295$.
2. **Basis values:** B-basis $= 460.0 - 1.926 \times 9.0 = 442.6$ MPa; A-basis $= 460.0 - 3.295 \times 9.0 = 430.3$ MPa.
   Both are below the lowest test — they bound the population, not the sample.
3. **To the design condition:** 100 °C (0.90 from the aluminium screening curve) and loading in the short-transverse
   direction, which in this example your own tests put at 88% of longitudinal. A-basis:
   $430.3 \times 0.90 \times 0.88 = 340.8$ MPa. The same knockdowns give 350.6 MPa on B-basis and 364.3 MPa on the
   typical value.
4. **The check.** A single load path needs A-basis: against 300 MPa applied, the reserve is $340.8/300 = 1.14$. The
   typical value would have shown 1.21. Any factor of safety between 1.14 and 1.21 passes on the typical value and
   fails on the real allowable.
5. **More tests buy allowable.** With only the first five of these tests, $k_A = 5.741$: the A-basis value falls to
   412.6 MPa, the design allowable to 326.8 MPa, and the reserve to 1.09.

## Where this stops

- **Normal data from one population.** Real allowables use several material lots and test the distribution; the
  calculator assumes a normal distribution and one sample.
- **Knockdowns are inputs.** The temperature curve is a family-level screening estimate. Use handbook or test data for
  the alloy, temper, product form and exposure time.
- **Composites** follow CMH-17 practice, with environmental conditioning, lay-up and damage built in. Their allowables
  are generated very differently.
- **The factor of safety** comes from your programme, not from this page.

## On the drawing and in the reports

- **Specify the material so the allowable applies:** specification, temper, product form and thickness range on the
  drawing — see [Specification](../specification/). Change any of them and the allowable may no longer apply.
- **Lot acceptance tests** prove each delivery meets its specification. They do not make an allowable.
- **In every margin table:** allowable value, source, basis, temperature, direction and product form, next to the
  margin.
