## Why this matters

The material block is the part of a drawing that is easiest to write badly and most expensive to get wrong. "6082-T6"
or "D6AC" tells a supplier the chemistry and nothing about what you will receive: which product form, what tolerance
class, what melt practice, what strength, what certificate. The strength you designed to only arrives if the drawing
asks for it in terms an inspector can check.

## The three-part rule

<Analogy title="Ordering a coffee">
  Ask for "a coffee" and you will get *a* coffee. Ask for "a large flat white with oat milk and an extra shot" and you
  get what you meant — and if it arrives wrong, you can say exactly how. A material callout works the same way: the
  alloy is "coffee"; the form, standard, condition and finish are everything that makes the order checkable.
</Analogy>

Every metallic callout needs three things, and most drawings carry only one or two:

1. **The alloy and its condition** — temper or heat treatment, stated so it can be tested (for example a minimum UTS),
   with the process specification that delivers it.
2. **The product form and its standard** — properties and tolerances differ between bar, plate, extrusion and forging,
   so the standard for *that form* governs. Many forms have a separate tolerance standard.
3. **The finish, with its own specification** — anodise, plating, passivation, primer, peening. Never "to suit".

## Product form and grain direction

<GrainFlow />

Rolled and extruded metal is anisotropic. In thick plate the **short-transverse** direction, through the thickness,
typically has the lowest elongation and fracture toughness and the highest sensitivity to stress-corrosion cracking —
the classic failure path for high-strength aluminium and steel fittings loaded through their thickness. Two
consequences for the drawing:

- **Choose the form for the load path.** A closure, boss or Y-ring that carries pressure load around a contour is
  usually forged so the grain follows the shape, rather than machined from bar or plate.
- **Say which way the grain runs** when it matters. A grain-direction note on the drawing and on the forging drawing
  removes the guesswork.

## Worked example — a D6AC motor case component

The builder's default is the D6AC sheet, one of the two case-steel sheets added in v2.

1. **Alloy and form.** Bar to **AMS 6431**, the material specification for D6AC bar, forgings and tubing, which also
   fixes vacuum-arc remelting (VAR). Sheet and plate for a rolled-and-welded cylinder come under **AMS 6439**.
2. **Condition.** The case strength — around 1520 MPa minimum UTS in v1's example — comes from hardening and double
   tempering, invoked through the heat-treatment specification (AMS 2759/2 for low-alloy steels at this strength) with
   furnace pyrometry to AMS 2750. Write the strength you designed to on the drawing, not just the process.
3. **Finish.** Paint over a phosphate or primer base. Electroplating ultra-high-strength steel risks hydrogen
   embrittlement, so it is used only where justified and always followed by an embrittlement-relief bake.
4. **Extras that matter for a pressure part:** an inspection certificate (BS EN 10204 type 3.1 in the UK), batch or heat
   marking by a method that doesn't create stress raisers, and NDT after heat treatment — the v1 example shows magnetic
   particle inspection to ASTM E1444.

<Aside type="tip" title="Special processes get their own lines">
  Heat treatment, welding, NDT, coating and cleanliness are each separate notes with their own specification. If a
  requirement can't be verified by measuring the finished part, it needs an approved process and a certificate.
</Aside>

## Where this stops

<Aside type="caution" title="Check the current issue">
  Standards are revised, superseded and sometimes withdrawn. The sheets here are a starting point carried over from
  REF·DB v1 plus two v2 additions; work to the issue your programme invokes, and use your organisation's preferred
  specifications where they exist.
</Aside>

- **Equivalent is not identical.** Two specifications for "the same" alloy can differ in impurity limits, melt practice,
  testing and acceptance. See [designations](../designations/) before substituting across systems.
- **Fracture-critical parts** add flaw-size and NDE requirements and controlled quality classes to the drawing.
- **Composites and non-metallics** are specified quite differently: the lay-up, process and cure *are* the material.
