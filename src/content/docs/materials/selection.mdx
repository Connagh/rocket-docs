## Why this matters

Material choice is usually the largest single lever on mass, cost and risk, and it is made early, when changing your mind
is cheap. It is also where designs most often go wrong for a simple reason: they get sized on the wrong number. A
datasheet's room-temperature *typical* yield is not the *minimum* strength at the service temperature, in the load
direction, for the product form you can actually buy. This page is about making the right comparison first; the
[specification page](../specification/) is about pinning the result down on a drawing.

## The idea: a performance index

<Analogy title="Choosing a minibus by fuel used per passenger">
  If you need to move eight people, the car with the best miles per gallon is not the answer — a two-seater sports car
  would win that. What matters is miles per gallon *per seat*: the combination of properties that serves the job. A
  performance index is the same idea for materials. For a light, strong pressure vessel it is strength divided by
  density, not strength alone.
</Analogy>

Ashby's method turns a design requirement into that index in four steps:

1. **Function** — what does the part do? (Contain pressure, carry a bending load, conduct heat.)
2. **Objective** — what are you minimising or maximising? (Usually mass, sometimes cost or deflection.)
3. **Constraints** — what must it achieve no matter what? (Not yield at proof, not burst, survive 150 °C, be weldable.)
4. **Free variables** — what are you allowed to change? (Wall thickness, section size, the material itself.)

Write the objective, use the constraint to eliminate the free variable, and what remains is a group of material
properties. Maximise that group.

### Worked derivation — a light motor case

A thin cylinder of radius $r$ and length $L$ holds pressure $p$. Its mass is $m = 2\pi r L\,t\,\rho$, and the wall must
carry the hoop stress without exceeding the allowable $\sigma$: $t = p\,r/\sigma$. Substituting the free variable $t$:

$$
m = 2\pi r^2 L\,p\;\cdot\;\frac{\rho}{\sigma}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
\text{maximise}\;\; M = \frac{\sigma}{\rho}
$$

Everything before the dot is fixed by the design; only $\rho/\sigma$ belongs to the material. That is the specific
strength — the same index as a light tie in tension — and it is exactly why the [case-sizing page](../../motor/case-sizing/)
reports $p_bV/W$.

### Shape changes the index

<IndexShapes />

For a beam of square section with a stiffness target, deflection goes as $1/(E\,I)$ with $I \propto A^2$; eliminating the
area gives $m \propto \rho/E^{1/2}$. For a panel of fixed width, $I \propto t^3$, so $m \propto \rho/E^{1/3}$. Taking logs
turns each index into a straight line on a log–log property chart:

$$
M = \frac{E^{1/2}}{\rho} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \log E = 2\log\rho + 2\log M
$$

— a line of slope 2. Every material on that line performs identically for the job; every material above it does
better. That is the dashed guideline on the chart below.

<Analogy title="Contour lines on a map">
  A property chart is a map of the material world. The families are countries — metals over here, polymers down there,
  composites in a long thin island. A performance index is a set of contour lines drawn across the whole map: walking
  along one changes the material but not the performance; walking uphill, perpendicular to them, makes the part better.
</Analogy>

### Fracture needs its own index

Strength-per-weight rewards the strongest alloys — but toughness generally falls as strength rises. Two fracture
indices capture the other side:

- **Yield before break,** $K_{IC}/\sigma_y$ — the critical crack size at yield scales with its square:
  $a_c = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{K_{IC}}{Y\sigma_y}\right)^2$. Large is forgiving.
- **Leak before break,** $K_{IC}^2/\sigma_y$ — the largest wall a pressure vessel can have and still leak before it
  breaks (see the [leak-before-break screen](../../motor/case-sizing/#leak-before-break)).

On their own these pick soft, tough metals that are hopeless for a light case. Used **together** with the mass index
they expose the real trade: how much toughness you give up for each kilogram saved.

## Worked example — choosing a case material

**Function:** contain chamber pressure. **Objective:** minimum mass. **Constraints:** weldable, service temperature at
least 150 °C, metallic (for this example). **Free variable:** wall thickness.

1. **Rank by** $\sigma_y/\rho$ (the chart's default). The top eight, in kN·m/kg:

   | # | Material | $\sigma_y/\rho$ | $K_{IC}/\sigma_y$ (√m) | Crack at yield, $Y = 1.12$ |
   | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
   | 1 | Maraging 18Ni-300 | 233 | 0.043 | 0.47 mm |
   | 2 | Ti-6Al-2Sn-4Zr-2Mo | 218 | 0.066 | 1.09 mm |
   | 3 | AerMet 100 | 218 | 0.067 | 1.13 mm |
   | 4 | Maraging 18Ni-250 | 211 | 0.059 | 0.89 mm |
   | 5 | Ti-6Al-4V | 199 | 0.085 | 1.84 mm |
   | 6 | Al-Li 2195 | 194 | 0.057 | 0.83 mm |
   | 7 | Ti-6Al-4V ELI | 187 | 0.120 | 3.68 mm |
   | 8 | D6AC | 185 | 0.048 | 0.59 mm |

2. **Check fracture.** The lightest choice, maraging 300, tolerates a crack of only about half a millimetre at yield —
   close to the limit of reliable inspection. Maraging 250 gives up 10% of the mass index for nearly twice the crack
   size. Ti-6Al-4V ELI costs 20% of the mass index against maraging 300 but tolerates a crack nearly eight times
   deeper. That is the yield-before-break trade in numbers.
3. **Check what the chart can't see.** D6AC ranks eighth on mass and low on toughness, yet it has flown on many large
   motors. The reasons are off the chart: large forgings and plate are available, heat treatment and welding are well
   understood, the indicative price in the database is roughly a quarter to a third of maraging steel's, and decades of test data back it.
   Titanium brings cost and availability in large sections; maraging steels carry cobalt price and supply risk.
4. **Composites.** Switch on the composites family and filament-wound carbon/epoxy lands far above every metal — but on
   *fibre-direction* strength. A real filament-wound case is a laminate whose wall strength is a fraction of that
   number, and it brings its own failure modes (matrix cracking, impact damage, stress rupture). It still usually wins
   on mass, which is why so many modern cases are composite.

<Aside type="tip" title="What the example teaches">
  An index ranks; it doesn't decide. Use the mass index to find the candidates, a second index to expose what they cost
  you, and judgement for everything the chart can't plot — supply, process, cost, compatibility and heritage.
</Aside>

## Where this stops

<Aside type="caution" title="Screening data, not allowables">
  The chart uses typical room-temperature values shared with REF·DB v1. Designs are sized on statistically based
  allowables (for example MMPDS A- or B-basis) for the specific product form, thickness, grain direction and
  temperature, from your organisation's approved data.
</Aside>

- **Temperature.** Strength and toughness at the service temperature, including after any thermal exposure, decide the
  case. The max-temperature filter only screens.
- **Product form and direction.** Plate, forging and extrusion differ, and the short-transverse direction of thick plate
  can be much weaker and less tough than the rolling direction. See [specifying materials](../specification/#product-form-and-grain-direction).
- **Environment.** Stress-corrosion cracking, hydrogen embrittlement and compatibility with propellant, fluids and
  coatings remove candidates that the chart ranks highly.
- **Cost and availability** are real constraints, not afterthoughts — and they change.
