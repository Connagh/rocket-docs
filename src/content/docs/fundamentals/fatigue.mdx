## Why this matters

A static margin cannot see fatigue coming. A bracket that carries ten times its working load once can crack after a
few million cycles at a third of it. Fatigue is the most common way that mechanical hardware fails in service.

A solid motor case sees only a handful of pressure cycles — proof, leak check, one firing — so it is rarely
fatigue-critical. Much of the hardware around it is:

- handling and transport equipment, lifting points and the motor's own attachment points;
- thrust-vector actuator brackets and linkages, and the bolts in every joint;
- brackets and mounts loaded by vibration in transport and in flight;
- test-stand hardware and reusable test cases, cycled many times.

<Analogy title="Bending a paperclip">
  Bend a paperclip once and nothing happens. Bend it back and forth twenty times and it snaps — at a load it had
  already survived nineteen times. Each cycle did a little damage you could not see. Hardware fatigue works the same
  way, with smaller strains and millions of cycles.
</Analogy>

This page covers **initiation** — stress-life, for a part with no known flaw. Once a crack exists, switch to fracture
mechanics: [Proof testing](../../verify/proof-testing/) grows a known flaw with the Paris law. Using the wrong one is
a common and expensive mistake.

## The S-N curve

Fatigue strength is measured on polished coupons cycled at constant amplitude, fully reversed, and plotted as stress
against cycles to failure on log scales.

- **Steels and titanium have an endurance limit** at around $10^6$–$10^7$ cycles: below it, clean coupons at room
  temperature do not fail. For steels, a common estimate is $S'_e \approx 0.5\,S_{ut}$, levelling off at about
  700 MPa above $S_{ut} = 1400$ MPa.
- **Aluminium alloys have no endurance limit.** Their fatigue strength is quoted at $5 \times 10^8$ cycles —
  roughly $0.4\,S_{ut}$, but no more than about 130 MPa — and the curve keeps falling.
- **Between $10^3$ cycles and the endurance limit** the curve is close to a straight line on log scales (Basquin's
  law), from about $0.9\,S_{ut}$ at $10^3$ down to $S_e$.
- **A life is a distribution, not a number.** At one stress, coupon lives typically scatter by a factor of ten.

## From a laboratory coupon to your part

The coupon is small, polished, bent and tested in a laboratory. Your part is none of those. Marin's factors correct
for the differences:

$$
S_e = k_a\,k_b\,k_c\,k_d\,k_e\;S'_e
$$

| Factor | Corrects for | Typical effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $k_a$ surface | Roughness — cracks start at the surface | Machined 1280 MPa steel 0.68; ground 0.86; as-forged 0.22 |
| $k_b$ size | A bigger section has more surface and more inclusions to start from | 0.88 at 25 mm in bending; 1 in axial loading |
| $k_c$ loading | Axial loading has no gradient to slow an early crack | 1 bending, 0.85 axial |
| $k_d$ temperature | Strength loss and oxidation when hot | 1 at room temperature |
| $k_e$ reliability | From the median coupon life to a survival probability | 0.81 for 99%, 0.75 for 99.9% |

The surface factor falls fastest for **high-strength steels**: the stronger the steel, the more a rough surface costs
it. That is why fatigue-critical features on strong parts are ground, polished or peened.

## Notches

A hole, fillet or thread raises the local stress by the elastic stress-concentration factor $K_t$, read from charts
such as Peterson's. Fatigue sees a little less than $K_t$, because a small notch also has a steep stress gradient.
Peterson's notch sensitivity gives the fatigue factor:

$$
K_f = 1 + q\,(K_t - 1),
\qquad
q = \frac{1}{1 + a/r}
$$

with $r$ the notch radius and $a$ a material length: about $0.0254\,(2070/S_{ut})^{1.8}$ mm for steels, about 0.64 mm
for aluminium alloys. Stronger steels have a smaller $a$, so they feel almost the full $K_t$. The design rules
follow: generous radii, notches kept apart, and nothing stacked on a fillet — no thread run-out, cross-hole or
marking.

## Mean stress

A tensile mean stress shortens life; a compressive one helps. The common criteria convert a cycle with amplitude
$\sigma_a$ and mean $\sigma_m$ into an equivalent fully reversed amplitude $\sigma_{ar}$:

| Criterion | $\sigma_{ar}$ | Character |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goodman | $\sigma_a / (1 - \sigma_m/S_{ut})$ | Standard; conservative for ductile metals |
| Gerber | $\sigma_a / \left(1 - (\sigma_m/S_{ut})^2\right)$ | A parabola, closer to test data for ductile metals |
| Soderberg | $\sigma_a / (1 - \sigma_m/S_y)$ | To yield; the most conservative |
| Smith–Watson–Topper | $\sqrt{\sigma_\text{max}\,\sigma_a}$ | Widely used with strain-life methods and for aluminium |

Common practice gives **no credit for compressive mean stress**. **Residual stress acts as a mean stress**: shot
peening and rolling leave the surface in compression and extend life, while welding leaves it in tension and shortens
it.

## Variable loads: Miner's rule

Real parts see a spectrum. Count the cycles into blocks of similar amplitude and mean — rainflow counting
(ASTM E1049) is the standard way to do this from a measured load history — then add up the fraction of life each
block uses:

$$
D = \sum_i \frac{n_i}{N_i},
\qquad
\text{failure predicted at } D = 1
$$

It is simple and widely used, but measured damage sums at failure scatter roughly between 0.3 and 3, and the order of
loading matters, which the rule ignores. Programmes apply a factor on life for this reason. With an endurance limit,
blocks below $S_e$ do no damage in the rule, but occasional large cycles can lower the endurance limit in reality.
Haibach's modification continues the S-N line below $S_e$ at a shallower slope to cover this.

## Worked example — an actuator bracket

A machined 4340 steel bracket for a thrust-vector actuator, 25 mm section in bending, with a fillet of $K_t = 1.8$ and
radius 2 mm, at 99% reliability. Per service life it sees transport vibration (90 MPa amplitude on a 50 MPa mean,
5 million cycles), handling (zero to 500 MPa, so 250 on 250, 2000 times) and firings (180 on 60 MPa, 20,000 cycles).
These are the calculator's starting values.

1. **Coupon strength.** $S'_e = 0.5 \times 1280 = 640$ MPa.
2. **To the part.** $k_a = 4.51 \times 1280^{-0.265} = 0.677$, $k_b = 1.24 \times 25^{-0.107} = 0.879$,
   $k_e = 0.814$, so $S_e = 640 \times 0.677 \times 0.879 \times 0.814 = 310$ MPa — under half the coupon's
   strength.
3. **The notch.** $a = 0.0254 \times (2070/1280)^{1.8} = 0.060$ mm, so $q = 1/(1 + 0.060/2) = 0.971$ and
   $K_f = 1 + 0.971 \times 0.8 = 1.78$.
4. **The S-N line** runs from $0.9 \times 1280 = 1152$ MPa at $10^3$ cycles to 310 MPa at $10^6$: slope
   $b = \log(310/1152)/3 = -0.190$.
5. **Each block** (Goodman, with $K_f$ on both amplitude and mean):
   - Transport: local 160 on 89 MPa → $\sigma_{ar} = 172$ MPa, below $S_e$: **no damage**.
   - Handling: local 444 on 444 → $\sigma_{ar} = 444/(1 - 444/1280) = 680$ MPa →
     $N = 10^3 (680/1152)^{-1/0.190} = 16{,}000$ → $D = 0.125$.
   - Firings: local 320 on 107 → $\sigma_{ar} = 349$ MPa → $N = 538{,}000$ → $D = 0.037$.
6. **Total** $D = 0.162$: the bracket lasts about **6.2 service lives** in the median — before your programme's factor
   on life.

Two lessons come out of it. The 2,000 handling cycles do **77%** of the damage; the 5 million vibration cycles do
none. And **grinding the fillet** raises $k_a$ to 0.86 and $S_e$ to 394 MPa: the firing loads drop below the endurance
limit, and the life rises to **14.8 service lives** — 2.4 times, from the surface alone.

To see the other side, try 7075-T6 with every stress divided by three (an aluminium bracket would be sized for lower
stresses). With no endurance limit, the 5 million vibration cycles now do about a quarter of the damage.

## What makes fatigue worse — and better

| Worse | Better |
| --- | --- |
| Tensile residual stress — welds, abusive grinding | Compressive residual stress — shot peening, cold rolling |
| Corrosion and corrosion pits; corrosive environments remove the endurance limit | Protective finishes chosen for fatigue as well as corrosion |
| Fretting at clamped and bolted interfaces | Preloaded bolts — the bolt then sees only part of the alternating load |
| Hard chromium plating on steel; hard anodising on aluminium | Generous radii, notches kept apart |
| Handling damage, transverse tool marks, stamped markings | Fine finish along the load direction; etched, not stamped, marks |
| High temperature, hydrogen embrittlement | Welds kept out of the highest-stress regions |

## Where this stops

- **High-cycle, stress-life** only. Below about $10^3$–$10^4$ cycles, or wherever the notch yields, use strain-life
  (Coffin–Manson) methods — see [stress- and strain-based failure](../../verify/random-vibration/#stress-based-and-strain-based-failure).
- **Once there is a crack**, use crack growth — the Paris law on the [proof-testing page](../../verify/proof-testing/).
- **Multiaxial and non-proportional loading** need dedicated criteria; this page uses one equivalent stress.
- **Random vibration** is turned into cycles through a spectral method; see [Random vibration](../../verify/random-vibration/).
- **Welded joints** are assessed with detail categories (for example BS 7608), not with Marin factors.
- **Everything here is an estimate.** Fatigue punishes optimism: a fatigue-critical part is substantiated by coupon and
  full-scale testing.

## On the drawing

- **Surface finish on fatigue-critical features**, as an Ra limit and, where it matters, a lay direction along the
  load.
- **Minimum fillet radii**, dimensioned — not left to "break sharp edges".
- **Shot peening** to a specification (for example AMS 2430 or AMS 2432), with intensity and coverage stated.
- **Bolt preload** specified as a tension or a controlled torque, not left to the fitter.
- **Fatigue-critical features identified**, with their inspection (penetrant or magnetic particle) and no marking or
  stamping on them.
