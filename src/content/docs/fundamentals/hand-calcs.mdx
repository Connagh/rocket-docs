## Why this matters

A finite-element model gives an answer for whatever you build into it — wrong loads, wrong units, wrong boundary
conditions and all. A hand calc is how you know what answer to expect. It is used three times:

- **Before the model, to size the part.** Thickness, section and pin size come from closed-form solutions; the model
  then refines a design that is already close.
- **To find what governs.** Five quick checks often show that four failure modes do not matter and one does — and that
  one tells you where the model needs its detail.
- **After the model, to check it.** Reactions, far-field stresses and deflections should match the hand calc where its
  idealisation holds. When they do not, something is wrong, and it is cheaper to find out now.

Reviewers expect it too: a margin that rests only on a contour plot is hard to check, and a hand calc is something
another engineer can follow line by line.

## How to make one

1. **Draw the free body.** Every load, every reaction, and check that they balance.
2. **Decide what the part is** — a beam, a plate, a shell, a lug or a column — and which part of it the idealisation
   describes. Most real parts are several of these at once.
3. **Bound the boundary conditions.** A bolted edge is neither simply supported nor clamped, so run both. If the
   design passes both, the edge does not matter; if not, it needs a better model.
4. **Calculate in one consistent set of units** — N, mm and MPa on this site — and compare with the
   [allowables](../../materials/allowables/) and your programme's factors ([Margin policy](../../verify/margin-policy/)).
5. **Sanity-check.** Order of magnitude, limiting cases, the direction things should move when you change an input.
6. **Write down the assumptions.** Each one is either a requirement on the drawing or a question for the detailed model.

## Beams

Bending stress and peak shear stress, with $Z = I/c$ the section modulus and $Q$ the first moment of area above the
neutral axis:

$$
\sigma = \frac{M}{Z}, \qquad \tau_{max} = \frac{VQ}{Ib}
$$

The peak shear is 1.5 times the mean shear $V/A$ for a rectangle, 4/3 for a solid round and about 2 for a thin tube.
For a load $W$ (a point load, or the total of a uniform load) on a span $L$:

| Supports and load | Peak moment | Peak deflection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cantilever, end load | $WL$ | $WL^3/3EI$ |
| Cantilever, uniform load | $WL/2$ | $WL^3/8EI$ |
| Simply supported, central load | $WL/4$ | $WL^3/48EI$ |
| Simply supported, uniform load | $WL/8$ | $5WL^3/384EI$ |
| Fixed both ends, central load | $WL/8$ | $WL^3/192EI$ |
| Fixed both ends, uniform load | $WL/12$ | $WL^3/384EI$ |

- **Deflection goes as $L^3$**, so span matters far more than section: halve the span and the deflection falls
  eightfold.
- **Short, deep beams** (span under about five depths) also deflect in shear, and beam theory becomes approximate.
- **Thin-walled sections** fail locally — flanges and walls buckle or cripple — before the beam's bending stress
  reaches yield. A thin tube in bending is a [shell-buckling](../../verify/shell-buckling/) problem.

## Flat plates

A plate carries pressure by bending alone, which makes it far less efficient than a shell. For a solid circular plate of
radius $a$ and thickness $t$ under uniform pressure $q$ (Roark):

$$
\sigma_{SS} = \frac{3(3+\nu)\,q a^2}{8t^2} \;\text{at the centre}
$$

$$
\sigma_{clamped} = \frac{3\,q a^2}{4t^2} \;\text{at the edge}
$$

with centre deflections $\frac{(5+\nu)\,q a^4}{64(1+\nu)D}$ and $\frac{q a^4}{64D}$, and $D = Et^3/12(1-\nu^2)$. A
rectangular plate with short side $b$ follows the same pattern, $\sigma = \beta\,q b^2/t^2$ and
$w = \alpha\,q b^4/Et^3$:

| Long side / short side | 1.0 | 1.5 | 2.0 | 3.0 | ∞ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\beta$, simply supported (centre) | 0.287 | 0.487 | 0.610 | 0.713 | 0.750 |
| $\beta$, clamped (mid long edge) | 0.308 | 0.453 | 0.497 | 0.498 | 0.500 |

- **Stress goes as $1/t^2$** and deflection as $1/t^3$. A flat closure needs ten or twenty times the thickness of a
  dome doing the same job — which is why pressure vessels have domed heads.
- **These are small-deflection solutions**, good while the deflection is under about half the thickness. Beyond that
  the plate also stretches like a membrane, gets stiffer, and these formulas over-predict the stress.
- **A long plate is a beam strip**: by an aspect ratio of three it is within a few per cent of the strip.

## Thin shells

A thin shell carries pressure in membrane tension, and the familiar results follow from equilibrium alone:

$$
\sigma_\theta = \frac{pr}{t}, \qquad \sigma_x = \frac{pr}{2t}
$$

with $r$ the mean radius; a sphere carries $pr/2t$ in every direction. The cylinder grows radially by $\frac{pr^2}{Et}\left(1 - \tfrac{\nu}{2}\right)$ with closed
ends. These hold **away from edges**. Where something stops the wall growing — a stiff flange, a ring, a closure of
different stiffness — the wall bends, over an edge zone set by

$$
\beta = \frac{\left[3(1-\nu^2)\right]^{1/4}}{\sqrt{rt}}
$$

The bending dies away within about $\pi/\beta \approx 2.4\sqrt{rt}$ — 35 mm on the 300 mm case. At a fully clamped
edge the axial bending stress at the surface is

$$
\sigma_b = \frac{E\,w}{r}\sqrt{\frac{3}{1-\nu^2}}
$$

with $w$ the free radial growth: 1.82 times the hoop stress with open ends, 1.55 times with closed ends, before the
axial membrane stress is added. So:

- **A wall that meets a stiff flange must thicken** into a land, blended into the shell — the edge bending is too large
  for the membrane wall.
- **Check $r/t$.** Below about 10 the wall is thick and the membrane formula under-predicts the inner-surface stress;
  use Lamé's solution ([Case sizing](../../motor/case-sizing/)).
- **Compression is different.** Under external pressure or axial load a thin shell buckles long before it yields — see
  [Shell buckling](../../verify/shell-buckling/).

## Lugs and pins

A lug carrying a load $P$ along its axis on a pin in a double-shear clevis has five first-cut checks:

- **Net tension** across the hole: $P/\big((W-D)\,t\big)$ against $F_{tu}$.
- **Shear-out** of the end on two planes: $P/\big(2(e - D/2)\,t\big)$ against $F_{su}$ — conservative.
- **Bearing** on the hole: $P/(d\,t)$ against $F_{bru}$, which depends on the edge distance $e/D$.
- **Pin shear**, in double shear: $P/(2\cdot\pi d^2/4)$.
- **Pin bending**, with the lug's load spread over its thickness $t$ and the clevis plates (each $t_2$) reacting at
  their mid-thickness across a gap $g$:

$$
M = \frac{P}{2}\left(\frac{t}{4} + g + \frac{t_2}{2}\right)
$$

$$
\sigma = \frac{32M}{\pi d^3}
$$

Pin bending usually governs, and it grows with every millimetre of gap. Real lugs need more than this: oblique and
transverse loads, the stress concentration at the hole for fatigue, and the efficiency factors of the Melcon–Hoblit
method or ASME BTH-1. The first cut tells you whether the lug is roughly the right size and which check to refine.

## Columns

A slender column buckles at the Euler load, with $K$ the effective-length factor and $r = \sqrt{I/A}$:

$$
P_{cr} = \frac{\pi^2 EI}{(KL)^2}
$$

| Ends | Theoretical $K$ | Design $K$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pinned–pinned | 1.0 | 1.0 |
| Fixed–free | 2.0 | 2.1 |
| Fixed–pinned | 0.7 | 0.8 |
| Fixed–fixed | 0.5 | 0.65 |

Design values are larger because real "fixed" ends rotate. A short column yields as it buckles, and Euler over-predicts
it. Below the transition slenderness $KL/r = \pi\sqrt{2E/F_{ty}}$, use the Johnson parabola:

$$
\sigma_{cr} = F_{ty} - \frac{F_{ty}^2}{4\pi^2 E}\left(\frac{KL}{r}\right)^2
$$

Real columns are crooked and loaded off-centre. With an eccentricity $e$, the secant formula gives the peak stress:

$$
\sigma_{max} = \frac{P}{A}\left[1 + \frac{ec}{r^2}\sec\theta\right]
$$

$$
\theta = \frac{KL}{2r}\sqrt{\frac{P}{EA}}
$$

It is not linear in $P$, so apply the factor to the load before using it. Thin-walled tubes can also buckle locally,
in the wall, before the column buckles as a whole.

## Hand calcs and the finite-element model

- **Before meshing**, the hand calc says where the detail is needed: several elements across each edge zone
  ($\pi/\beta$), several through the thickness where a solid part bends.
- **Material cards first.** Units, constants and tables checked before anything else — see
  [FEA material cards](../../verify/fea-cards/).
- **Reactions next.** The model's reactions must equal the applied loads. If they do not, nothing else in it can be
  trusted.
- **Then the far field.** Away from details, the model's membrane stresses and deflections should match the hand calc.
  Deflections should sit between the simply supported and clamped bounds.
- **Then the peaks.** They should be where the hand calc says — at the edge, the hole, the root — and of the right
  order. A peak far above it at a single node is often a singularity at a sharp corner or a point load, not a stress.
- **When they disagree** where the hand calc should be good, find out why before trusting either: the model, its loads
  or units, or the hand calc's idealisation.

## Worked example — hand checks for the 300 mm motor's test campaign

Five quick checks on the motor and the hardware that tests it. The yield factor of 1.10 and ultimate factor of 1.25
are **for this example only** — press **Load worked example** and use the tabs.

1. **The case as a beam.** In transport the loaded motor, 137.81 kg, sits in two cradles 1000 mm apart. Take 3 g
   vertical, for this example: 4.05 kN along the span. As a simply supported ⌀302.75 × 1.377 mm tube,
   $M = WL/8 = 0.51$ kN·m, so the bending stress is **5.2 MPa**, the shear 3.1 MPa and the deflection 0.017 mm. The
   margins are over +200: the case as a beam does not matter. At 3.3 diameters between supports it is a short, deep
   beam, so the numbers are only approximate — but they are two hundred times too small to matter. What does matter is
   the concentrated load at each cradle on a 1.4 mm wall — a shell problem, so the cradles must be wide or ringed.
2. **The case wall.** At MEOP, 10 MPa, the hoop stress is $10 \times 150.69 / 1.377 = 1094$ MPa and the axial 547 MPa
   — the [case sizing](../../motor/case-sizing/#worked-example) numbers — with margins of +0.20 on yield and +0.11 on
   ultimate before any weld efficiency. The edge zone is $\pi/\beta = 35$ mm. If the aft flange held the wall rigidly,
   the inner surface would see $547 + 1.55 \times 1094 = 2241$ MPa — above the ultimate strength. The wall must
   thicken into a land of about $2.05 \times 1.377 = 2.8$ mm where it meets the flange. The hand calc sets that before
   any model exists.
3. **The proof-test blanking plate.** For the [proof test](../../verify/proof-testing/) at 11 MPa, the aft closure is
   replaced by a flat steel plate on an illustrative 285 mm bolt circle. Take it as simply supported at the bolt circle
   and loaded over its whole area — conservative, since the pressure stops at the 260 mm seal. With a quenched and
   tempered steel at $F_{ty} = 655$ and $F_{tu} = 860$ MPa (typical values), it needs 21.5 mm. A 25 mm plate sees
   **441 MPa** at the centre (MS +0.35 yield, +0.56 ultimate) and deflects 1.0 mm — 0.04 of its thickness, so the
   formula holds. Its edge rotates 0.61°, which a face seal must follow ([Sealing strategy](../../seals/sealing-strategy/)).
   A hemispherical head of the same radius would need 1.3 mm.
4. **The test stand's thrust rod.** The motor's 29.5 kN thrust at MEOP ([Load paths](../../joints/load-paths/#worked-example--the-300-mm-motors-load-path))
   goes to the load cell through a 25 × 2.5 mm S355 tube, 600 mm pin to pin. $r = 8.0$ mm, so $KL/r = 75$ — below the
   transition at 107. Johnson gives **47.3 kN**, MS +0.28 at 1.25 × the thrust. Euler would have said 63.6 kN and
   +0.73 — 35% unconservative. With 0.6 mm (L/1000) of crookedness, the secant formula gives 233 MPa at 1.10 × the
   thrust: +0.53 to first yield.
5. **The rod's clevis ends.** Each end is a 10 mm S355 lug, 36 mm wide, with a 12.2 mm hole 18 mm from the end, on a
   12 mm alloy-steel pin between two 6 mm clevis plates with 0.5 mm gaps. Typical allowables: lug $F_{tu} = 470$,
   $F_{su} = 282$, $F_{bru} = 705$ MPa; pin $F_{tu} = 1000$, $F_{su} = 600$ MPa. At 1.25 × 29.5 = 36.8 kN: net tension
   +2.04, shear-out +0.82, bearing +1.30, pin shear +2.69 — and **pin bending +0.54**, which governs, as it usually
   does. Doubling the gaps to 1 mm cuts it to +0.42.

Five checks and a page of arithmetic. Each one either clears a part or tells the detailed model exactly where to look:
the cradles, the flange land and the seal at the plate's edge.

## Where this stops

- **Idealisations.** Real parts are part beam, part plate, part shell. The hand calc describes the regions where one
  idealisation holds, and bounds the rest.
- **Linear elastic, small deflection.** No plasticity, no large-deflection membrane action in plates, no contact.
- **Static.** Dynamic loads, fatigue and fracture need their own checks — [Fatigue](../fatigue/),
  [Random vibration](../../verify/random-vibration/).
- **Lugs:** axial load and first-cut allowables only. **Columns:** no local wall buckling, no lateral–torsional
  buckling of open sections.
- **Rectangular plates** use coefficients for ν = 0.3 and uniform pressure with all four edges alike.

## On the drawing

A hand calc's assumptions become requirements:

- **The dimensions it sized**, as minimums where they matter — the plate thickness, the flange land and its blend.
- **Material and condition** behind the allowables — specification, heat treatment, the properties it assumed.
- **Straightness** of struts when the column check assumed it, and **pin fit and clevis gaps** when pin bending was
  checked with them.
- **Edge distances** of lugs and holes.
- **Supports** the beam check pointed to — cradle width, saddle or ring.
