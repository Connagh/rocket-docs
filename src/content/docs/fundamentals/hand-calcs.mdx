## Why this matters

A finite-element model gives a result for all the data that you put into it, also incorrect loads, incorrect units and
incorrect boundary conditions. A hand calc tells you what result to expect. You use it three times:

- **Before the model, to size the part.** The thickness, section and pin size come from closed-form solutions. The
  model then refines a design that is already almost correct.
- **To find the failure mode that governs.** Five quick checks often show that four failure modes are not important and
  one is. That one tells you where the model needs detail.
- **After the model, to check it.** Reactions, far-field stresses and deflections must agree with the hand calc where
  its idealisation is valid. If they do not agree, there is an error. It costs less to find it now.

Reviewers also expect hand calcs. It is difficult to check a margin that comes only from a contour plot. A different
engineer can follow a hand calc line by line.

## How to make one

1. **Draw the free body.** Show all loads and all reactions, and check that they balance.
2. **Decide what the part is:** a beam, a plate, a shell, a lug or a column. Also decide which region of the part the
   idealisation describes. Most real parts are several of these together.
3. **Put limits on the boundary conditions.** A bolted edge is not simply supported and not clamped, so calculate the
   two cases. If the design passes the two cases, the edge condition is not important. If it does not, the edge needs a
   better model.
4. **Calculate in one consistent set of units** (N, mm and MPa on this site). Compare with the
   [allowables](../../materials/allowables/) and the factors of your programme ([Margin policy](../../verify/margin-policy/)).
5. **Check the result.** Check the order of magnitude, the limiting cases, and the direction of change when you change
   an input.
6. **Write the assumptions.** Each one is a requirement on the drawing or a question for the detailed model.

<Analogy title="Checking the bill">
  You do not calculate a restaurant bill again to the penny. You round the values and add them in your head. If the
  total is two times your estimate, you look more carefully. A hand calc is that estimate for a finite-element model.
  It is approximate and quick, and it is very good at finding a result that is ten times incorrect.
</Analogy>

## Beams

The bending stress and the peak shear stress are as follows. $Z = I/c$ is the section modulus, and $Q$ is the first
moment of area above the neutral axis:

$$
\sigma = \frac{M}{Z}, \qquad \tau_{max} = \frac{VQ}{Ib}
$$

The peak shear is 1.5 times the mean shear $V/A$ for a rectangle, 4/3 times for a solid round, and approximately 2 times
for a thin tube. For a load $W$ (a point load, or the total of a uniform load) on a span $L$:

| Supports and load | Peak moment | Peak deflection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cantilever, end load | $WL$ | $WL^3/3EI$ |
| Cantilever, uniform load | $WL/2$ | $WL^3/8EI$ |
| Simply supported, central load | $WL/4$ | $WL^3/48EI$ |
| Simply supported, uniform load | $WL/8$ | $5WL^3/384EI$ |
| Fixed both ends, central load | $WL/8$ | $WL^3/192EI$ |
| Fixed both ends, uniform load | $WL/12$ | $WL^3/384EI$ |

- **Deflection changes as $L^3$.** Thus the span is much more important than the section. If you halve the span, the
  deflection decreases by eight times.
- **Short, deep beams** (span less than approximately five depths) also deflect in shear, and beam theory is then only
  approximate.
- **Thin-walled sections fail locally.** Flanges and walls buckle or cripple before the bending stress of the beam gets
  to yield. A thin tube in bending is a [shell-buckling](../../verify/shell-buckling/) problem.

## Flat plates

A plate carries pressure only by bending. Thus it is much less efficient than a shell. For a solid circular plate of
radius $a$ and thickness $t$, under a uniform pressure $q$ (Roark):

$$
\sigma_{SS} = \frac{3(3+\nu)\,q a^2}{8t^2} \;\text{at the centre}
$$

$$
\sigma_{clamped} = \frac{3\,q a^2}{4t^2} \;\text{at the edge}
$$

The centre deflections are $\frac{(5+\nu)\,q a^4}{64(1+\nu)D}$ and $\frac{q a^4}{64D}$, where $D = Et^3/12(1-\nu^2)$.
A rectangular plate with a short side $b$ follows the same pattern, $\sigma = \beta\,q b^2/t^2$ and
$w = \alpha\,q b^4/Et^3$:

| Long side / short side | 1.0 | 1.5 | 2.0 | 3.0 | ∞ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\beta$, simply supported (centre) | 0.287 | 0.487 | 0.610 | 0.713 | 0.750 |
| $\beta$, clamped (mid long edge) | 0.308 | 0.453 | 0.497 | 0.498 | 0.500 |

- **Stress changes as $1/t^2$,** and deflection as $1/t^3$. A flat closure needs ten or twenty times the thickness of a
  dome that does the same work. This is why pressure vessels have domed heads.
- **These are small-deflection solutions.** They are valid while the deflection is less than approximately half the
  thickness. At larger deflections, the plate also stretches as a membrane and becomes stiffer, and these formulas give
  a stress that is too high.
- **A long plate is a beam strip.** At an aspect ratio of three, it is within a few per cent of the strip.

## Thin shells

A thin shell carries pressure as membrane tension. The usual results come from equilibrium only:

$$
\sigma_\theta = \frac{pr}{t}, \qquad \sigma_x = \frac{pr}{2t}
$$

Here $r$ is the mean radius. A sphere has $pr/2t$ in all directions. With closed ends, the cylinder grows radially by
$\frac{pr^2}{Et}\left(1 - \tfrac{\nu}{2}\right)$. These results are valid **away from edges**. Where something stops
the growth of the wall (a stiff flange, a ring, or a closure of different stiffness), the wall bends. The edge zone has
the parameter:

$$
\beta = \frac{\left[3(1-\nu^2)\right]^{1/4}}{\sqrt{rt}}
$$

The bending decreases to almost zero within approximately $\pi/\beta \approx 2.4\sqrt{rt}$, which is 35 mm on the
300 mm case. At a fully clamped edge, the axial bending stress at the surface is:

$$
\sigma_b = \frac{E\,w}{r}\sqrt{\frac{3}{1-\nu^2}}
$$

Here $w$ is the free radial growth. This is 1.82 times the hoop stress with open ends, and 1.55 times with closed ends,
before you add the axial membrane stress. Thus:

- **A wall that meets a stiff flange must become thicker** at a land, blended into the shell. The edge bending is too
  large for the membrane wall.
- **Check $r/t$.** Below approximately 10, the wall is thick, and the membrane formula gives an inner-surface stress
  that is too low. Use the Lamé solution ([Case sizing](../../motor/case-sizing/)).
- **Compression is different.** Under external pressure or axial load, a thin shell buckles long before it yields. See
  [Shell buckling](../../verify/shell-buckling/).

## Lugs and pins

A lug with a load $P$ along its axis, on a pin in a double-shear clevis, has five first checks:

- **Net tension** across the hole: $P/\big((W-D)\,t\big)$ against $F_{tu}$.
- **Shear-out** of the end on two planes: $P/\big(2(e - D/2)\,t\big)$ against $F_{su}$. This is conservative.
- **Bearing** on the hole: $P/(d\,t)$ against $F_{bru}$, which depends on the edge distance $e/D$.
- **Pin shear,** in double shear: $P/(2\cdot\pi d^2/4)$.
- **Pin bending.** The load of the lug spreads over its thickness $t$. The clevis plates (each $t_2$) react at their
  mid-thickness, across a gap $g$:

$$
M = \frac{P}{2}\left(\frac{t}{4} + g + \frac{t_2}{2}\right)
$$

$$
\sigma = \frac{32M}{\pi d^3}
$$

Pin bending usually governs, and it increases with each millimetre of gap. Real lugs need more checks than these:
oblique and transverse loads, the stress concentration at the hole for fatigue, and the efficiency factors of the
Melcon–Hoblit method or ASME BTH-1. The first checks tell you if the size of the lug is approximately correct, and which
check to refine.

## Columns

A slender column buckles at the Euler load. $K$ is the effective-length factor and $r = \sqrt{I/A}$:

$$
P_{cr} = \frac{\pi^2 EI}{(KL)^2}
$$

| Ends | Theoretical $K$ | Design $K$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pinned–pinned | 1.0 | 1.0 |
| Fixed–free | 2.0 | 2.1 |
| Fixed–pinned | 0.7 | 0.8 |
| Fixed–fixed | 0.5 | 0.65 |

The design values are larger because real "fixed" ends turn. A short column yields while it buckles, and Euler gives a
load that is too high. Below the transition slenderness $KL/r = \pi\sqrt{2E/F_{ty}}$, use the Johnson parabola:

$$
\sigma_{cr} = F_{ty} - \frac{F_{ty}^2}{4\pi^2 E}\left(\frac{KL}{r}\right)^2
$$

Real columns are not straight, and their loads are off-centre. With an eccentricity $e$, the secant formula gives the
peak stress:

$$
\sigma_{max} = \frac{P}{A}\left[1 + \frac{ec}{r^2}\sec\theta\right]
$$

$$
\theta = \frac{KL}{2r}\sqrt{\frac{P}{EA}}
$$

This stress is not linear in $P$. Thus apply the factor to the load before you use the formula. Thin-walled tubes can
also buckle locally, in the wall, before the full column buckles.

## Hand calcs and the finite-element model

- **Before meshing,** the hand calc tells where detail is necessary: several elements across each edge zone
  ($\pi/\beta$), and several through the thickness where a solid part bends.
- **Check the material cards first.** Check the units, constants and tables before all other items. See
  [FEA material cards](../../verify/fea-cards/).
- **Then check the reactions.** The reactions of the model must be equal to the applied loads. If they are not, you
  cannot trust any other result from the model.
- **Then check the far field.** Away from details, the membrane stresses and deflections of the model must agree with
  the hand calc. Deflections must be between the simply supported limit and the clamped limit.
- **Then check the peaks.** They must be where the hand calc puts them (at the edge, the hole or the root), and of the
  correct order. A peak at one node that is much higher is frequently a singularity at a sharp corner or a point load,
  not a stress.
- **If they do not agree** where the hand calc must be good, find the cause before you trust either result. The cause
  can be the model, its loads or units, or the idealisation of the hand calc. The full set of checks is on
  [FEA & test](../../verify/fea-and-test/).

## Worked example: hand checks for the 300 mm motor's test campaign

These are five quick checks on the motor and on the hardware that tests it. The yield factor of 1.10 and the ultimate
factor of 1.25 are **for this example only**. Push **Load worked example** and use the tabs.

1. **The case as a beam.** During transport, the loaded motor (137.81 kg) sits in two cradles 1000 mm apart. Use 3 g
   vertical for this example: 4.05 kN along the span. As a simply supported ⌀302.75 × 1.377 mm tube,
   $M = WL/8 = 0.51$ kN·m. Thus the bending stress is **5.2 MPa**, the shear stress is 3.1 MPa and the deflection is
   0.017 mm. The margins are more than +200, so the case as a beam is not important. At 3.3 diameters between supports
   it is a short, deep beam, so the numbers are only approximate. But they are two hundred times too small to be
   important. The important load is the concentrated load at each cradle on a 1.4 mm wall. This is a shell problem,
   thus the cradles must be wide or have rings.
2. **The case wall.** At MEOP (10 MPa), the hoop stress is $10 \times 150.69 / 1.377 = 1094$ MPa and the axial stress
   is 547 MPa. These are the [case sizing](../../motor/case-sizing/#worked-example) numbers. The margins are +0.20 on
   yield and +0.11 on ultimate, before any weld efficiency. The edge zone is $\pi/\beta = 35$ mm. If the aft flange
   held the wall rigidly, the inner surface would have $547 + 1.55 \times 1094 = 2241$ MPa, which is more than the
   ultimate strength. The wall must become thicker, with a land of approximately $2.05 \times 1.377 = 2.8$ mm where it
   meets the flange. The hand calc sets this before there is a model.
3. **The proof-test blanking plate.** For the [proof test](../../verify/proof-testing/) at 11 MPa, a flat steel plate
   replaces the aft closure, on an illustrative 285 mm bolt circle. Assume that it is simply supported at the bolt
   circle and loaded over its full area. This is conservative, because the pressure stops at the 260 mm seal. With a
   quenched and tempered steel at $F_{ty} = 655$ and $F_{tu} = 860$ MPa (typical values), it needs 21.5 mm. A 25 mm
   plate has **441 MPa** at the centre (MS +0.35 yield, +0.56 ultimate) and deflects 1.0 mm. This is 0.04 of its
   thickness, so the formula is valid. Its edge turns 0.61°, and a face seal must follow this
   ([Sealing strategy](../../seals/sealing-strategy/)). A hemispherical head of the same radius would need only 1.3 mm.
4. **The thrust rod of the test stand.** The 29.5 kN thrust of the motor at MEOP
   ([Load paths](../../joints/load-paths/#worked-example-the-300-mm-motors-load-path)) goes to the load cell through a
   25 × 2.5 mm S355 tube, 600 mm from pin to pin. $r = 8.0$ mm, so $KL/r = 75$, which is below the transition at 107.
   Johnson gives **47.3 kN**, MS +0.28 at 1.25 × the thrust. Euler would give 63.6 kN and +0.73, which is 35%
   unconservative. With 0.6 mm (L/1000) of crookedness, the secant formula gives 233 MPa at 1.10 × the thrust: +0.53 to
   first yield.
5. **The clevis ends of the rod.** Each end is a 10 mm S355 lug, 36 mm wide, with a 12.2 mm hole 18 mm from the end. It
   is on a 12 mm alloy-steel pin between two 6 mm clevis plates, with 0.5 mm gaps. Typical allowables: lug
   $F_{tu} = 470$, $F_{su} = 282$, $F_{bru} = 705$ MPa; pin $F_{tu} = 1000$, $F_{su} = 600$ MPa. At 1.25 × 29.5 =
   36.8 kN, the margins are: net tension +2.04, shear-out +0.82, bearing +1.30, pin shear +2.69, and **pin bending
   +0.54**. Pin bending governs, as it usually does. If the gaps increase to 1 mm, it decreases to +0.42.

These are five checks on one page of arithmetic. Each check clears a part, or tells the detailed model exactly where
to look: the cradles, the flange land, and the seal at the edge of the plate.

## Where this stops

- **Idealisations.** Real parts are partly beam, partly plate and partly shell. The hand calc describes the regions
  where one idealisation is valid, and puts limits on the other regions.
- **Linear elastic, small deflection.** No plasticity, no large-deflection membrane action in plates, and no contact.
- **Static loads only.** Dynamic loads, fatigue and fracture need their own checks. See [Fatigue](../fatigue/) and
  [Random vibration](../../verify/random-vibration/).
- **Lugs:** axial load and first-check allowables only. **Columns:** no local wall buckling, and no lateral–torsional
  buckling of open sections.
- **Rectangular plates** use coefficients for ν = 0.3, and uniform pressure with all four edges the same.

## On the drawing

The assumptions of a hand calc become requirements:

- **The dimensions that it sized,** as minimums where they are important: the plate thickness, the flange land and its
  blend.
- **The material and condition** of the allowables: the specification, the heat treatment and the assumed properties.
- **Straightness** of struts if the column check assumed it, and **pin fit and clevis gaps** if the pin bending check
  used them.
- **Edge distances** of lugs and holes.
- **The supports** that the beam check found necessary: cradle width, saddle or ring.
