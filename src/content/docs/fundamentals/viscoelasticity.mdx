## Why this matters

All the rubber seals, propellant grains, adhesive bondlines, insulation layers and composite matrices in a motor are
viscoelastic. If you treat them as springs, you will:

- **calculate a sealing force that is too high** after storage, and a seal that follows a joint too fast when cold;
- **get grain stresses incorrect by a factor of ten,** because the load can be a slow cool-down or a fast ignition;
- **not see the slow loss of bolt preload** through a composite flange, or the creep of a bonded joint under a
  sustained load.

All the complications on this page come from one property: the material remembers.

## Two experiments, mirror images

- **Relaxation.** Keep a strain constant and monitor the stress decrease. The relaxation modulus is
  $E(t) = \sigma(t)/\varepsilon_0$.
- **Creep.** Keep a stress constant and monitor the strain increase. The creep compliance is
  $J(t) = \varepsilon(t)/\sigma_0$.

They describe the same material, but they are **not reciprocals**: $J(t)\,E(t) \le 1$, and creep occurs later in time
than relaxation. The exact relation is $\int_0^t E(t-s)\,J(s)\,ds = t$. The calculator uses it to change one into the
other.

<Analogy title="Memory foam">
  If you push on a memory-foam pillow, it moves slowly. If you remove your hand, the dent fills slowly. If you put the
  pillow in a cold location, it becomes hard and slow, and the dent takes much longer to recover. Seals, propellant and
  adhesives do the same, on time scales from microseconds to years.
</Analogy>

## The Prony series

Real polymers relax over many decades of time. Thus the useful model is the **generalised Maxwell** model: a long-term
spring in parallel with many spring–dashpot arms, one for each decade. Its relaxation modulus is a **Prony series**:

$$
E(t) = E_\infty + \sum_{i} E_i\, e^{-t/\tau_i},
\qquad
E_0 = E_\infty + \sum_i E_i
$$

$E_0$ is the **instantaneous** (glassy) modulus, and $E_\infty$ is the **long-term** (rubbery) modulus. The relaxation
times $\tau_i$ are usually fixed at one for each decade over the important range. The $E_i$ are fitted to test data by
least squares, and they must stay positive. The same series gives the response to a sinusoidal load:

$$
E'(\omega) = E_\infty + \sum_i E_i \frac{\omega^2\tau_i^2}{1+\omega^2\tau_i^2},
\qquad
E''(\omega) = \sum_i E_i \frac{\omega\tau_i}{1+\omega^2\tau_i^2},
\qquad
\tan\delta = \frac{E''}{E'}
$$

$E'$ is the stored part, $E''$ is the part lost as heat, and $\tan\delta$ is their ratio.

### Into a solver

Finite-element codes use the series in normalised form, $g_i = E_i/E_0$, with the instantaneous value as the elastic
modulus. Two errors are frequent:

- **The sum of the $g_i$ must be less than 1.** The remainder, $1 - \sum g_i = E_\infty/E_0$, is the long-term
  fraction. A sum of 1 means that the material relaxes fully, and the solver rejects it.
- **The elastic card must agree.** Give the instantaneous modulus, not a datasheet "Young's modulus" measured at an
  unknown rate. Alternatively, declare the long-term basis and give $E_\infty$. If you mix the two, all the stresses in
  the model are incorrect by a factor.

A third error is less easy to see: **put the arms sufficiently close together.** One arm for each decade fits $E(t)$
well, but it causes visible ripples in $\tan\delta$, because each arm adds its own loss peak. Two arms for each decade
give smooth dynamic properties. The presets in the calculator use two.

Solvers usually apply the $g_i$ to the shear modulus. For an almost incompressible material ($E \approx 3G$), the
normalised values are the same. If the bulk modulus also relaxes, it needs its own terms.

## Time–temperature superposition

**Cold has the same effect as slow.** A lower temperature makes all relaxation processes slower by the same factor
$a_T$. Thus a test at low temperature over a few seconds shows what occurs at room temperature over days. If you move
short tests at several temperatures along the log-time axis until they overlap, you get a **master curve**. It covers
many more decades than one test.

Above the glass transition, the shift factor follows the WLF equation:

$$
\log_{10} a_T = \frac{-C_1\,(T - T_r)}{C_2 + T - T_r}
$$

With the reference at the glass transition, the general constants are $C_1 = 17.44$ and $C_2 = 51.6$ K. For a
different reference temperature $T_r$, they become $C_1 = 17.44 \times 51.6 / (51.6 + T_r - T_g)$ and
$C_2 = 51.6 + T_r - T_g$. At $T_r = T_g + 50$ K, this gives 8.86 and 101.6 K, which are the values that the
[propellant page](../../motor/propellant/#explorer) uses. WLF is valid from approximately $T_g$ to $T_g + 100$ K. Below
$T_g$, glassy polymers such as composite matrices usually follow an Arrhenius-type shift.

### Dynamic mechanical analysis

A DMA test applies a sinusoidal load to a sample while the temperature changes. As the material goes through its glass
transition, $E'$ decreases by two or three orders of magnitude and $\tan\delta$ has a peak. The peak is above the $T_g$
from a slow thermal test, and it moves up with the test frequency. Thus always give the frequency with a DMA $T_g$.

## Worked example: a fluoroelastomer seal

This is an illustrative seal compound, similar to FKM: $E_\infty = 6$ MPa and 27 arms, two for each decade from 0.1 µs
to $10^6$ s, so $E_0 = 950$ MPa. $T_g = -20$ °C, and the data refer to 20 °C, so $C_1 = 9.82$ and $C_2 = 91.6$ K. These
are the starting values of the calculator.

1. **The stiffness depends on the time.** At 20 °C, the modulus 1 s after loading is 11.6 MPa. At 40 °C it is 7.6 MPa.
   At 0 °C it is 44 MPa.
2. **Storage.** At 40 °C, $a_T = 0.0174$. Thus one year of squeeze at 40 °C causes the same relaxation as 58 years at
   20 °C. The sealing force decreases to **79%** of its value one second after the squeeze.
3. **The gap opens.** If the seal is released at 40 °C, it recovers **63%** of its squeeze within 0.1 s. If it is
   released at 0 °C, where all processes are approximately 32,000 times slower than at 40 °C, it recovers only
   **6.4%**.
4. **The lesson.** A seal must follow the joint as it opens. When it is cold, it cannot. This was the mechanism of the
   loss of *Challenger*: the O-rings were too cold to expand as the field joint opened under pressure. The design
   responses are on the [O-ring page](../../seals/o-ring-design/): keep the seal warm, select a compound with a lower
   glass transition, add squeeze, and prevent the joint from opening.

The **propellant preset** is the same illustrative propellant as in the
[explorer on the propellant page](../../motor/propellant/#explorer), written as a 33-arm Prony series that a solver can
use.

## What it means for seals, propellant and composites

### Seals

- **The sealing force relaxes in storage,** and real compounds also take a chemical set as they age. Design the minimum
  squeeze for the relaxed, aged condition. See [Storage & ageing](../../verify/storage-ageing/).
- **The low-temperature recovery** is measured by the temperature-retraction (TR) test. Specify it at the lowest
  temperature at which the seal must *follow movement*, not only at the lowest temperature that it must survive.
- **Below the glass transition,** a seal is a hard plastic, and it cannot seal a part that moves.

### Propellant

- During slow cool-downs, the grain has a **relaxed modulus**. At ignition, it has a modulus nearer to the **glassy**
  value, often ten times stiffer. This is why the [grain page](../../motor/propellant-grain/) treats the two load cases
  separately.
- The strain capability also depends on rate and temperature. Grain analyses use measured Prony series and shift
  factors, with cumulative damage over the service life.

### Composites and adhesives

- **Fibre-dominated** properties (along the carbon fibres) almost do not creep. **Matrix-dominated** properties (shear,
  transverse and through-thickness) do creep, and more so as the temperature approaches the glass transition of the
  matrix. Moisture decreases that glass transition. Thus hot and wet is the critical condition.
- **Bolted composite joints lose preload** as the laminate creeps through its thickness. The usual solutions are metal
  inserts or bushes, checks of the preload loss over time, and a retorque policy where permitted.
- **Stress rupture.** Aramid and glass fibres can fail after long periods under a constant load. Carbon fibre is much
  less susceptible. This is the basis of the stress-rupture requirements for composite-overwrapped pressure vessels.
- **Bondlines creep** under sustained shear, especially when hot. Sustained-load allowables are much lower than the
  short-term strength.
- Composite design practice keeps the maximum operating temperature a margin below the **wet** glass transition
  temperature of the matrix.

## Where this stops

- **Linear viscoelasticity:** small strains and no damage. Filled rubbers become softer after the first load (the
  Mullins effect), and propellants dewet. These two effects are non-linear.
- **Thermorheologically simple:** one shift factor for all arms. Multi-phase materials can be different.
- **WLF range:** approximately $T_g$ to $T_g + 100$ K.
- **Pressure** also shifts the response, in the opposite direction to temperature. **Ageing** changes the Prony series.
- **Bulk behaviour** is elastic here. Only the deviatoric response relaxes.

## On the drawing and in the specifications

- **Seal compounds:** specify the low-temperature retraction and the compression set at a stated temperature and time,
  not only the hardness.
- **Each viscoelastic property** on a drawing or in a specification needs its temperature and its rate or time:
  "modulus at 20 °C, 1 minute", not only "modulus".
- **Composites:** give the maximum operating temperature, with its basis in the wet glass transition. Use
  sustained-load allowables for joints that have a permanent load.
- **Analyses:** record the source of the Prony data, its reference temperature and its shift law, so that somebody can
  check the material model.
