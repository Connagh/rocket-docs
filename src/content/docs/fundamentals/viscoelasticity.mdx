## Why this matters

Every rubber seal, propellant grain, adhesive bondline, insulation layer and composite matrix in a motor is
viscoelastic. Treat them as springs and you will:

- **overestimate the sealing force** a seal keeps through storage, and how fast it can follow a joint when cold;
- **get grain stresses wrong by an order of magnitude**, depending on whether the load is a slow cool-down or a fast
  ignition;
- **miss the slow loss of bolt preload** through a composite flange, or the creep of a bonded joint under sustained
  load.

Every complication on this page follows from one change: the material remembers.

## Two experiments, mirror images

- **Relaxation** — hold a strain and watch the stress fall. The relaxation modulus is $E(t) = \sigma(t)/\varepsilon_0$.
- **Creep** — hold a stress and watch the strain grow. The creep compliance is $J(t) = \varepsilon(t)/\sigma_0$.

They describe the same material, but they are **not reciprocals**: $J(t)\,E(t) \le 1$, and creep happens later in
time than relaxation. They are linked exactly by $\int_0^t E(t-s)\,J(s)\,ds = t$, which is how the calculator turns one
into the other.

<Analogy title="Memory foam">
  Press a memory-foam pillow and it gives slowly; take your hand away and the dent fills back in slowly. Put the
  pillow somewhere cold and it turns hard and sluggish — the dent takes far longer to recover. Seals, propellant and
  adhesives do the same, on time scales from microseconds to years.
</Analogy>

## The Prony series

Real polymers relax over many decades of time, so the useful model is the **generalised Maxwell** model: a long-term
spring in parallel with many spring–dashpot arms, one per decade. Its relaxation modulus is a **Prony series**:

$$
E(t) = E_\infty + \sum_{i} E_i\, e^{-t/\tau_i},
\qquad
E_0 = E_\infty + \sum_i E_i
$$

$E_0$ is the **instantaneous** (glassy) modulus and $E_\infty$ the **long-term** (rubbery) one. The relaxation times
$\tau_i$ are usually fixed at one per decade over the range of interest, and the $E_i$ fitted to test data by
least squares, kept positive. The same series gives the response to a sinusoidal load:

$$
E'(\omega) = E_\infty + \sum_i E_i \frac{\omega^2\tau_i^2}{1+\omega^2\tau_i^2},
\qquad
E''(\omega) = \sum_i E_i \frac{\omega\tau_i}{1+\omega^2\tau_i^2},
\qquad
\tan\delta = \frac{E''}{E'}
$$

$E'$ is the part stored, $E''$ the part lost as heat, and $\tan\delta$ their ratio.

### Into a solver

Finite-element codes take the series in normalised form, $g_i = E_i/E_0$, with the elastic modulus given as the
instantaneous value. Two errors are common:

- **The $g_i$ must sum to less than 1.** The remainder, $1 - \sum g_i = E_\infty/E_0$, is the long-term fraction. A
  sum of 1 means the material relaxes away completely, and the solver will reject it.
- **The elastic card must match.** Give the instantaneous modulus, not a datasheet "Young's modulus" measured at some
  unknown rate — or declare the long-term basis and give $E_\infty$. Mixing the two scales every stress in the model.

A third, quieter one: **space the arms closely enough.** One arm per decade fits $E(t)$ well but leaves visible ripples
in $\tan\delta$, because each arm adds its own loss peak; two per decade gives smooth dynamic properties. The presets
in the calculator use two.

Solvers usually apply the $g_i$ to the shear modulus. For a nearly incompressible material ($E \approx 3G$) the
normalised values carry over; if the bulk modulus relaxes as well, it needs its own terms.

## Time–temperature superposition

**Cold behaves like slow.** Lowering the temperature slows every relaxation process by the same factor $a_T$, so a
test at low temperature over a few seconds shows what happens at room temperature over days. Shifting short tests
made at several temperatures along the log-time axis until they overlap builds a **master curve** spanning far more
decades than any single test.

Above the glass transition, the shift factor follows the WLF equation:

$$
\log_{10} a_T = \frac{-C_1\,(T - T_r)}{C_2 + T - T_r}
$$

With the reference at the glass transition, the general constants are $C_1 = 17.44$ and $C_2 = 51.6$ K. Referred to
any other temperature $T_r$, they become $C_1 = 17.44 \times 51.6 / (51.6 + T_r - T_g)$ and $C_2 = 51.6 + T_r - T_g$.
At $T_r = T_g + 50$ K that gives 8.86 and 101.6 K — the values the [propellant page](../../motor/propellant/#explorer)
uses. WLF holds from about $T_g$ to $T_g + 100$ K. Below $T_g$, glassy polymers such as composite matrices usually
follow an Arrhenius-type shift instead.

### Dynamic mechanical analysis

A DMA test pushes a sample sinusoidally while sweeping the temperature. As the material passes through its glass
transition, $E'$ drops by two or three orders of magnitude and $\tan\delta$ peaks. The peak sits above
the $T_g$ from a slow thermal test, and moves up with test frequency — so always quote the frequency with a DMA
$T_g$.

## Worked example — a fluoroelastomer seal

An illustrative FKM-like seal compound: $E_\infty = 6$ MPa and 27 arms, two per decade from 0.1 µs to $10^6$ s, so
$E_0 = 950$ MPa;
$T_g = -20$ °C, with the data referred to 20 °C, so $C_1 = 9.82$ and $C_2 = 91.6$ K. These are the calculator's
starting values.

1. **Stiffness depends on when you ask.** At 20 °C the modulus 1 s after loading is 11.6 MPa. At 40 °C it is 7.6 MPa;
   at 0 °C, 44 MPa.
2. **Storage.** At 40 °C, $a_T = 0.0174$, so a year squeezed at 40 °C does as much relaxing as 58 years at 20 °C. The
   sealing force falls to **79%** of its value one second after squeezing.
3. **The gap opens.** Released at 40 °C, the seal gives back **63%** of its squeeze within 0.1 s. Released at 0 °C —
   where every process runs about 32,000 times slower than at 40 °C — it gives back only **6.4%**.
4. **The lesson.** A seal has to follow the joint as it opens. Cold, it cannot. That was the mechanism behind the loss
   of *Challenger*: O-rings too cold to spring back as the field joint opened under pressure. The design responses —
   keep the seal warm, choose a compound with a lower glass transition, add squeeze, and stop the joint opening — are
   on the [O-ring page](../../seals/o-ring-design/).

## What it means for seals, propellant and composites

### Seals

- **Sealing force relaxes in storage**, and real compounds also take a chemical set as they age. Design the minimum
  squeeze on the relaxed, aged state — see [Storage & ageing](../../verify/storage-ageing/).
- **Low-temperature recovery** is measured by the temperature-retraction (TR) test. Specify it at the lowest
  temperature at which the seal must *follow movement*, not just the one it must survive.
- **Below the glass transition** a seal is a hard plastic, and seals nothing that moves.

### Propellant

- The grain sees a **relaxed modulus** during slow cool-downs and a **glassy-ward modulus** at ignition — often ten
  times stiffer. That is why the [grain page](../../motor/propellant-grain/) handles the two load cases separately.
- Strain capability depends on rate and temperature too, and grain analyses use measured Prony series and shift
  factors with cumulative damage over the service life.

### Composites and adhesives

- **Fibre-dominated** properties — along the carbon fibres — barely creep. **Matrix-dominated** ones — shear,
  transverse and through-thickness — do, increasingly as the temperature approaches the matrix's glass transition.
  Moisture lowers that glass transition, so hot-and-wet is the critical condition.
- **Bolted composite joints lose preload** as the laminate creeps through its thickness. Metal inserts or bushes,
  checks of preload loss over time, and a retorque policy where allowed are the usual answers.
- **Stress rupture.** Aramid and glass fibres can fail after long periods under constant load; carbon fibre is far
  less susceptible. This is the basis of the stress-rupture requirements for composite-overwrapped pressure vessels.
- **Bondlines creep** under sustained shear, especially when hot. Sustained-load allowables sit well below short-term
  strength.
- Composite design practice keeps the maximum operating temperature a margin below the matrix's **wet** glass
  transition temperature.

## Where this stops

- **Linear viscoelasticity**: small strains, no damage. Filled rubbers soften with the first loading (the Mullins
  effect) and propellants dewet; both are non-linear.
- **Thermorheologically simple**: one shift factor for every arm. Multi-phase materials can break this.
- **WLF range**: about $T_g$ to $T_g + 100$ K.
- **Pressure** also shifts the response, in the opposite sense to temperature, and **ageing** changes the Prony series
  itself.
- **Bulk behaviour** is treated as elastic here; only the deviatoric response relaxes.

## On the drawing and in the specifications

- **Seal compounds:** specify low-temperature retraction and compression set at a stated temperature and time — not
  hardness alone.
- **Any viscoelastic property** quoted on a drawing or in a specification needs its temperature and its rate or
  time: "modulus at 20 °C, 1 minute", not just "modulus".
- **Composites:** state the maximum operating temperature with its basis in the wet glass transition, and use
  sustained-load allowables for permanently loaded joints.
- **Analyses:** record where the Prony data came from, its reference temperature and its shift law, so the material
  model can be checked.
