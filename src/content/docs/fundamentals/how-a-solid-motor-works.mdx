## The whole idea in one picture

A solid rocket motor has no pumps, valves or moving parts. Fuel and oxidiser are premixed into a solid rubbery
**grain** that is cast directly into the **case**. Once lit, the exposed surface burns and makes hot gas, the gas
pressurises the case, and the only way out is through the **nozzle**. Everything about the motor follows from that
arrangement.

<Analogy title="A pressure cooker that is also the stove">
  The case is a pressure cooker, the grain is the fuel burning inside it, and the nozzle throat is the valve on the lid.
  The difference from a kitchen pressure cooker is that here the "stove" burns *faster* the higher the pressure
  gets. Whether that ends in a steady hiss or an explosion depends on one number, which the rest of this page is about.
</Analogy>

## How the propellant burns

Composite propellant burns only at its exposed surface, and that surface recedes **perpendicular to itself** at the
**burn rate** $r$, typically 5–20 mm/s for tactical and booster propellants. The burn rate depends strongly on chamber
pressure, and over a useful range it follows Saint-Robert's (Vieille's) law:

$$
r = a\,p_c^{\,n}
$$

- $a$ — burn-rate coefficient, set by the propellant formulation and its initial temperature.
- $n$ — pressure exponent, usually about 0.3–0.5 for AP/HTPB propellants.

<Analogy title="Toast under a grill">
  Burning propellant is like bread under a grill: only the surface facing the heat changes, and it works its way in
  layer by layer. Turn the grill up (raise the pressure) and it browns faster — but not proportionally faster. That
  "not proportionally" is the exponent $n < 1$.
</Analogy>

Because the surface recedes everywhere at once, the **shape** of the bore decides how the burning area $A_b$ changes as
the grain burns back. A plain circular bore grows in area as it opens up (progressive thrust); a star or slotted bore can
be shaped to keep the area nearly constant (neutral thrust). Grain design is how the motor's thrust–time curve is set.

## Why chamber pressure settles at one value

Gas is made at a rate set by the burning area and burn rate, and leaves through the choked throat at a rate set by the
throat area and the chamber pressure. Pressure rises until the two are equal:

$$
\underbrace{\rho_p\,A_b\,a\,p_c^{\,n}}_{\text{gas made}}
\;=\;
\underbrace{\frac{p_c\,A_t}{c^*}}_{\text{gas leaving}}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
p_c = \left(\rho_p\, a\, c^*\, \frac{A_b}{A_t}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-n}}
$$

where $\rho_p$ is propellant density, $A_t$ is throat area and $c^*$ is the characteristic velocity — a measure of how
much hot gas the propellant makes per unit of throat flow. The ratio $K_n = A_b/A_t$ is the designer's main lever.

<Analogy title="A bath with the tap on and the plug out">
  Water flows in from the tap and out through the plug hole. The level rises until the outflow — which grows with depth —
  matches the inflow. A solid motor does the same thing with pressure instead of water level. The twist: in a motor the
  tap opens further as the level rises ($p^n$). As long as the plug hole "wins" ($n<1$), the level still settles. If
  the tap ever won ($n \geq 1$), the bath would overflow, however big the plug hole.
</Analogy>

Try it. The orange curve is gas made, the blue line is gas leaving; where they cross is the operating pressure.

## The consequence for hardware: small changes are amplified

Rearranging the balance shows how a fractional change in burning area, burn rate or throat area turns into a change in
pressure:

$$
\frac{\Delta p_c}{p_c} \approx \frac{1}{1-n}\,\frac{\Delta K_n}{K_n}
$$

With $n = 0.4$, a 1% change in burning area moves pressure by about 1.7%. That amplification is why the **maximum
expected operating pressure (MEOP)** that the case is designed to sits well above the nominal pressure. It has to
cover, all at once:

- **Hot propellant.** Burn rate rises with initial grain temperature. The pressure sensitivity
  $\pi_K = \left(\partial \ln p_c / \partial T\right)_{K_n}$ is typically around 0.1–0.3 %/°C, so the spread between a
  cold-soaked and a hot-soaked motor can be tens of percent.
- **Manufacturing spread** in burn rate, grain geometry and throat diameter.
- **Ignition spike** and any **erosive burning** early in the burn, when gas speeds along the bore are highest.

<Aside type="caution" title="Hardware designers inherit MEOP — they do not set it">
  MEOP is a ballistics output that already includes these effects. Sizing a case on the nominal pressure and finding
  out later that the hot-day value is 15–25% higher is a classic and expensive mistake.
</Aside>

## From pressure to thrust

Thrust is chamber pressure acting over the throat, multiplied by the nozzle's thrust coefficient $C_F$:

$$
F = C_F\, p_c\, A_t
$$

$C_F$ is typically about 1.3–1.8, depending on the nozzle expansion ratio and the ambient pressure — it measures how well
the nozzle turns pressure into exhaust momentum. Thrust therefore follows the chamber pressure trace, which follows the
burning area, which follows the grain shape.

## What to take away

1. **Pressure is self-regulating** only because $n < 1$. Propellants are chosen for low, stable $n$.
2. **$K_n$ sets pressure.** Grain geometry sets $A_b$ through the burn; throat erosion slowly increases $A_t$.
3. **Changes are amplified by $1/(1-n)$** — so temperature, tolerances and transients must all be inside MEOP.
4. **The case sees MEOP, proof and burst** — which is where the next topic,
   [case &amp; pressure vessel sizing](../../motor/case-sizing/), picks up.
