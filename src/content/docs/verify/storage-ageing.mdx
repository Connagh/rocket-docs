## Why this matters

A motor is built once and then waits — often for years, sometimes for decades — and it has to work on its last day
in store exactly as on its first. Meanwhile it is changing: seals take a compression set, adhesives and bondlines absorb
moisture and oxidise, bolt preload relaxes, coatings break down at edges, and greases dry out. The service life a
programme can claim is set by its fastest-ageing part. Claiming it takes two things: a **prediction** from accelerated
tests, and **evidence** from surveillance of real aged hardware.

### What ages, and what to watch

| Item | How it ages | Design response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Elastomer seals | Compression set, oxidation, loss of low-temperature resilience | Budget the set into the minimum squeeze ([O-ring design](../../seals/o-ring-design/)); record cure dates; keep surveillance samples |
| Adhesive and bonded joints | Moisture uptake, plasticisation, interface degradation | Humidity-aged allowables; protect the bondline edge; witness coupons |
| Liner and insulation bonds | Plasticiser migration to and from the propellant; hardening | Compatibility testing; bond-strength surveillance with the energetics team |
| Bolted joints | Preload relaxation, embedment, creep of coatings and gaskets | Long grips, no soft layers in the clamp, a retorque policy if allowed |
| Coatings and platings | Breakdown at edges and damage; galvanic attack | Coat the cathode too; inspect edges and fastener heads |
| Lubricants and greases | Drying, separation, migration onto seals | Compatible with the elastomer; specify the quantity, not "lightly" |
| Connectors and cabling | Contact corrosion; insulation embrittlement | Sealed connectors; continuity and insulation checks in surveillance |

## Ageing is chemistry, and chemistry is temperature

Most ageing is a chemical reaction — oxidation, hydrolysis, cross-linking — and reaction rates follow the Arrhenius
law, $k = A\,e^{-E_a/RT}$. Life is inversely proportional to rate, so the **acceleration factor** between a test
temperature and the service temperature is

$$
AF = \exp\!\left[\frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_\text{service}} - \frac{1}{T_\text{test}}\right)\right]
\qquad R = 8.314 \ \text{J/(mol·K)},\ T \text{ in kelvin}
$$

The **activation energy** $E_a$ sets how steeply life falls with temperature:

- The old rule of thumb that "ageing doubles every 10 °C" is just one value of $E_a$: about **53 kJ/mol** near room
  temperature. At 80 kJ/mol the rate doubles every 6.5 K.
- $E_a$ belongs to the **mechanism**, not the material name. Values for polymer oxidation fall broadly between 50 and
  120 kJ/mol, and the answer is very sensitive to it. Measure it; do not borrow it.

<Analogy title="Milk on the kitchen counter">
  Milk keeps for a week in the fridge and spoils in a day on a warm counter — a few degrees make a huge difference to
  the chemistry. Now imagine a fridge that warms up for a few hours every afternoon. The milk spoils sooner than in a
  fridge held steadily at the same average temperature, because the warm hours do far more harm than the cold hours
  undo.
</Analogy>

## The average temperature is the wrong average

A store that swings between 12 °C and 38 °C does not age hardware like a store held at the average. With
$E_a = 79$ kJ/mol, an hour at 38 °C does as much ageing as 16 hours at 12 °C. The right average is the **mean kinetic
temperature** — the constant temperature that would cause the same total ageing as the real, varying record:

$$
T_\text{MKT} = \frac{E_a/R}{-\ln\!\left[\dfrac{1}{n}\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} e^{-E_a/(R\,T_i)}\right]}
$$

for $n$ equally spaced readings, in kelvin. The MKT is always at or above the arithmetic mean, and the gap grows with
the size of the swings and with $E_a$. The idea comes from pharmaceutical storage (Haynes, 1971), where it is standard
practice. **You cannot calculate it without a temperature log**, which is a good reason to require one in the storage
specification.

## Predicting service life from accelerated tests

The method, as laid out for rubbers in ISO 11346:

1. **Choose the property and its end-of-life limit** — the compression set at which a seal no longer has enough
   squeeze, or the bond strength below which a joint no longer meets its load.
2. **Age samples at three or more raised temperatures**, in the right condition — seals compressed, bonds at the right
   humidity — and record the time each takes to reach the limit.
3. **Plot ln(life) against 1/T.** One mechanism gives a straight line, whose slope is $E_a/R$.
4. **Extrapolate to the service MKT**, and compare with the required life using your programme's margin.

The traps:

- **One mechanism only.** Push the test temperature too high and a different chemistry takes over. You then extrapolate
  the wrong answer with great precision. Points that fall off the line at the hot end are the warning.
- **Thick sections age from the outside in.** At high test temperatures oxygen is used up near the surface faster than
  it can diffuse inward, so the skin ages and the core does not — a pattern that never happens in service. Thick parts
  need modest test temperatures, or a check for this.
- **Long extrapolations magnify every error.** A 10% error in $E_a$ can move a predicted life by a factor of 1.5 or
  more. The calculator shows how much for your data.
- **Field ageing can disagree with the lab.** Long-term studies of rubbers stored for decades have found lives shorter
  than the Arrhenius extrapolation predicted. Treat a prediction as a hypothesis for surveillance to test.

## Worked example — a seal in a hot store

Illustrative test data for an elastomer seal: the time to reach its compression-set limit is 1400 h at 80 °C, 330 h at
100 °C and 90 h at 120 °C. These are the calculator's starting values.

1. **Fit.** ln(life) against 1/T gives a slope of 9526 K, so $E_a = 9526 \times 8.314 = 79.2$ kJ/mol, with
   $r^2 \approx 1.000$.
2. **Store.** An unconditioned store in a hot climate, read every three hours over a typical day: 12, 18, 24, 31, 38,
   29, 21, 15 °C. The mean is 23.5 °C; the MKT is **26.9 °C**.
3. **Life in this store.** 18.9 years at the MKT. The plain average would have said 27.2 years — 44% optimistic.
4. **How sure?** The coolest test is 53 K above the store, so a 10% error in $E_a$ moves the answer by a factor of
   1.6.
5. **Test plan.** At 80 °C the acceleration factor is 119, so 30 days of oven ageing represents about 9.7 years in
   this store, and 62 days would represent 20.
6. **Against the requirement.** If 20 years were required, this seal falls short in this store. The options: store
   the motors at a controlled 20 °C (39.9 years), choose a more resistant compound, or claim a shorter life now and
   extend it with surveillance evidence.

## Surveillance

A prediction says what should happen; surveillance says what did. A surveillance programme:

- **Holds back representative hardware and witness samples** — seal samples, bonded coupons, sample cartons —
  stored with the fleet, under the same conditions.
- **Withdraws them on a schedule** and tests them: dimensions and leak checks, NDT (radiography or CT for debonds and
  cracks), dissection, mechanical tests of bonds and seals and, for complete motors, static firing of aged units.
- **Trends the results** against the prediction and the end-of-life limit, and extends — or shortens — the service
  life on the evidence.

Design for it from the start: put witness samples in the build, make bondlines inspectable, record cure dates and
batch traceability, and require a temperature log in store.

## Where this stops

- **One mechanism with a constant activation energy.** Several mechanisms, humidity, oxygen supply and mechanical
  stress each need their own treatment.
- **A representative record.** The MKT is only as good as the log, and one hot day is not a year — use records that
  cover the whole storage cycle.
- **Non-energetic hardware only.** Propellant and igniter ageing, including chemical stability, belongs to the
  energetics specialists.
- **ISO 11346** also describes fitting by time–temperature shifting, and a maximum temperature of use; this page uses
  the simplest form.

## On the drawing and in the specifications

- **Record cure dates and batch** for every elastomer, and apply the shelf-life rules of ISO 2230 or SAE ARP5316
  before assembly.
- **State the storage conditions** — temperature range, humidity, protection from ozone and UV — in the storage and
  handling specification, and require a temperature log.
- **Identify life-limited items** with the basis of their life, so a surveillance result can be traced back to a
  prediction.
- **Build in witness samples** — coupons bonded and cured with the hardware — so there is something to test that is
  not the flight article.
