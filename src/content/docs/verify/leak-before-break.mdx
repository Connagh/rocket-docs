## Why this matters

Every case contains flaws; fracture control is about what they do. A crack growing in the wall can end in one of two
ways:

- **Leak before break.** The crack grows through the wall and becomes a through crack short enough to be stable. Gas
  escapes, the pressure drops, and the failure is visible and contained.
- **Break before leak.** The crack goes critical while still buried, or breaks through already too long to hold. The
  case bursts with no warning, releasing all of its stored energy at once.

Which one you get is set by toughness, stress and wall thickness. It is a design decision whether or not anyone makes
it deliberately.

<Analogy title="A slow puncture or a blowout">
  A slow puncture hisses, the tyre goes soft, and you pull over. A blowout gives no warning at all. Both start with
  damage in the wall. The difference is whether the hole, once it opens, stays a small hole — and that depends on how
  tough the wall is compared with how hard it is being pulled.
</Analogy>

## Two questions

1. **Does the flaw get through the wall before it becomes critical?**
2. **When it gets through, is the through crack stable?**

### 1. Growing through the wall

Real flaws are **surface cracks**, roughly half-ellipses of depth $a$ and length $2c$. Their stress intensity comes
from Newman and Raju's fit to finite-element results:

$$
K = \sigma\sqrt{\frac{\pi a}{Q}}\;F\!\left(\frac{a}{c}, \frac{a}{t}, \varphi\right),
\qquad
Q \approx 1 + 1.464\left(\frac{a}{c}\right)^{1.65}
$$

$K$ is different at each point of the front: at the **deepest point** it drives the crack through the wall; at the
**surface points** it drives it along. Each point grows by the Paris law, $da/dN = C\,\Delta K^m$. A long, shallow
flaw rounds out towards a semicircle as it deepens, and close to the back face the surface points speed up and the
crack lengthens.

**Breakthrough** happens when the crack reaches the back face — or when the thin ligament ahead of it fractures or
yields first. Either way, the flaw becomes a through crack about as long as its surface length $2c$. If $K$ at the
surface points reaches $K_{IC}$ first, the crack runs along the wall while still buried, and the case bursts.

### 2. Is the through crack stable?

A through crack in a flat plate has $K = \sigma\sqrt{\pi c}$. In a pressurised cylinder its edges **bulge** outward,
which opens the crack further. The Folias factor $M$ captures this:

$$
K = M\,\sigma\sqrt{\pi c},
\qquad
M = \sqrt{1 + 0.6275\,z - 0.003375\,z^2},
\qquad
z = \frac{(2c)^2}{D\,t}
$$

(the modified-B31G form, for $z \le 50$). In a tough material the ligaments at the crack tips yield before they
fracture, so the crack also fails by **plastic collapse** when $M\,\sigma = \sigma_\text{flow}$, with the flow stress
taken here as the mean of yield and tensile strength. The through crack holds while

$$
\sigma_\text{hoop} < \min\!\left(\frac{K_{IC}}{M\sqrt{\pi c}},\; \frac{\sigma_\text{flow}}{M}\right)
$$

That gives the longest stable through crack, $2c_\text{crit}$. The design is **leak before break** if the crack breaks
through shorter than that, and the ratio of the two lengths is the margin.

[Irwin's simple screen](../../motor/case-sizing/#leak-before-break) — a flat-plate through crack with half-length equal
to the wall must be stable — is a quick version of the same argument, and gives the same verdict in the examples
below.

## Why high-strength cases usually fail the test

The stable crack length goes as $(K_{IC}/\sigma)^2$. Toughness tends to fall as strength rises, and high-strength
cases run at high stress, so $c_\text{crit}$ shrinks fast: D6AC at 1094 MPa holds a through crack only about 2.6 mm
long, in a wall 1.4 mm thick. There is barely room for a crack to get through before it is already too long.

**Size works against you too.** At the same hoop stress, a bigger motor has a proportionally thicker wall. The crack
must be longer to get through it, while the stable length is unchanged. A small case that leaks first can become a
large case that bursts, in the same material at the same stress.

## What it means for a motor case

<Aside type="caution" title="A leak in a burning motor is not benign">
  In flight, a crack through a burning motor's case vents hot combustion gas, which usually burns through and ends the
  flight. Leak-before-break does not make a flight failure safe. Fracture-control standards for spaceflight hardware
  give leak-before-burst credit only where the leak itself is not hazardous.
</Aside>

Where it does earn its keep:

- **On the proof-test stand.** A flaw that grows during proof leaks on the stand — water seeping from a hydrostatic
  test is easy to see — instead of bursting the case next to the test crew.
- **As a measure of robustness.** The ratio of stable length to breakthrough length says how much a missed flaw could
  grow before it matters.
- **In the rest of the system.** Pressurant bottles and actuator accumulators carry gas whose leak may be harmless, and
  there leak-before-burst can carry real weight in the fracture-control argument.

Where it does not hold — most high-strength steel cases — safety rests on **screening and inspection** instead: the
[proof test](../proof-testing/) guarantees no flaw larger than its screened size survives, and NDT and the
crack-growth life take it from there.

## Worked example — three cases at the same MEOP

All three are the 300 mm, 10 MPa case from [case sizing](../../motor/case-sizing/#worked-example), each sized there
with the same factors. Each starts from a surface flaw 0.25 mm deep and 1 mm long ($a/c = 0.5$), cycled from zero to
MEOP.

1. **D6AC** — 1.377 mm wall, hoop stress 1094 MPa, $K_{IC} = 70$ MPa√m. The longest stable through crack is 2.58 mm
   ($c_\text{crit} = 1.29$ mm; $M = 1.005$, so bulging barely matters this short; fracture governs). The flaw grows for
   about 1800 cycles with the martensitic-steel upper-bound growth law. By then it is 92% through the wall and 3.3 mm
   long, and its surface points reach $K_{IC}$: it runs along the case. **Break before leak.** Irwin's screen agrees:
   $c_\text{crit}/t = 0.95 < 1$.
2. **15CDV6** — a lower-strength, tougher steel. Sized to the same MEOP its wall is 2.23 mm and the hoop stress
   679 MPa, with $K_{IC} = 90$ MPa√m. The longest stable through crack is 10.2 mm. The flaw breaks through 5.7 mm
   long. **Leak before break**, with a margin of 1.8 on length. The price: a wall 62% thicker, so a case about 60%
   heavier.
3. **Maraging 18Ni-250** — stronger *and* tougher ($K_{IC} = 100$ MPa√m). A 1.21 mm wall at 1242 MPa still leaks
   first, with a margin of 1.29 — and the case is lighter than the D6AC one (10.6 kg against 11.8 kg). Toughness, not
   low stress, can buy leak-before-break; the price is in the material, not the mass.

Within one class of steel, the trade is plain: thinning the wall for mass costs leak-before-break. Moving to a
tougher alloy can buy it back without the mass — at a material cost.

<Aside type="note" title="Few cycles, but the shape still matters">
  A motor case sees only a handful of pressure cycles — proof, leak check, one firing — so fatigue growth in service is
  small. The growth model is here for the crack's *shape* as it deepens, which decides how long it is when it breaks
  through.
</Aside>

## Where this stops

- **Screening only.** Linear-elastic fracture with a separate plastic-collapse check. Ductile materials are properly
  assessed with tearing resistance, and fracture and collapse together on a failure assessment diagram (BS 7910, R6).
- **The surface-crack fit** covers $a/t \le 0.8$ and $a/c \le 1$; beyond that the calculator extrapolates. Real
  breakthrough can be dynamic and lengthen the crack.
- **Toughness** must be measured at the right temperature, orientation and thickness. Welds and heat-affected zones are
  often the least tough material in the case.
- **Axial cracks** are assessed, since hoop stress is twice the axial stress. Circumferential cracks need their own
  check where axial or bending loads are high.
- **Environment.** Stress-corrosion and sustained-load cracking are not included.
- **Detection.** A leak only counts if it is noticed in time to act — that half of the argument is not calculated here.

## On the drawing

- **Write down the fracture-control approach** — leak-before-break or safe-life — in the drawing notes or the fracture
  control plan, so inspection and test know what they are protecting.
- **Specify minimum toughness** in the material specification, at the temperature and orientation that matter. The
  verdict depends on it, and a datasheet typical value is not a requirement.
- **Tie NDT acceptance to the initial flaw** the analysis assumed. If the analysis starts from 0.25 mm, the inspection
  has to find 0.25 mm reliably — or the proof test has to screen it.
- **Make proof testing an acceptance requirement** with pressure, hold and leak criteria stated — see
  [Proof testing](../proof-testing/).
