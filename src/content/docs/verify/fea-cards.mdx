## Why this matters

A bad mesh shows. A bad material card does not. A density in the wrong units, a Poisson's ratio of 0.49 for propellant,
an engineering stress–strain curve entered as true, a temperature table that stops below the operating temperature —
each of these runs, converges and produces smooth, plausible contours. Card errors are among the commonest in
finite-element work, and among the cheapest to catch.

Every card is also a set of decisions: which data, on what basis (typical or minimum), at what temperature and rate,
in which form — elastic, plastic, orthotropic, hyperelastic, viscoelastic. A motor model needs all of them: steel or
composite case, aluminium and titanium hardware, rubber insulation and seals, and a propellant grain that is nothing
like any of the others.

## What goes on a card

| Material | On the motor | Model form | Watch for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Metals | Case, closures, skirts, bolts | Isotropic elastic; true-stress plastic table; all against temperature | Units; engineering against true; typical against minimum |
| Fibre composites | Wound case, skirts | Orthotropic, per ply or laminate | Material axes; major and minor Poisson's ratios; moisture and temperature |
| Rubbers | Insulation, O-rings, flex bearing | Hyperelastic on a bulk modulus; viscoelastic where rate matters | Poisson's ratio; the cold end near the glass transition |
| Propellant and liner | The grain | Viscoelastic on a bulk modulus, with time–temperature shift | Rate and temperature of the load case; stress-free temperature |
| Carbon, graphite, phenolics | Nozzle | Orthotropic and strongly temperature-dependent | Data at the temperatures the part reaches |

Thermal models need conductivity, specific heat and density for every material too — see
[Transient thermal](../../motor/transient-thermal/). Strength is not on the elastic card: margins come from the
results, checked against [allowables](../../materials/allowables/).

## Metals

**Elastic.** Young's modulus $E$ and Poisson's ratio $\nu$, with $G = E/2(1+\nu)$ implied — give a solver two of the
three, never three that disagree. Stiffness is always typical; there is no "minimum modulus".

**Plastic.** Solvers want **true stress against true plastic strain**, starting at zero plastic strain. From an
engineering curve, up to necking:

$$
\sigma_t = \sigma\,(1 + \varepsilon), \qquad \varepsilon_t = \ln(1 + \varepsilon)
$$

$$
\varepsilon_p = \varepsilon_t - \sigma_t/E
$$

With only tensile properties, a Ramberg–Osgood curve $\varepsilon = \sigma/E + 0.002\,(\sigma/F_{ty})^n$ through the
0.2% yield and through the ultimate at the uniform elongation $e_u$ gives the shape:

$$
n = \frac{\ln\!\left[(e_u - F_{tu}/E)/0.002\right]}{\ln(F_{tu}/F_{ty})}
$$

- **Typical or minimum?** Typical curves for stiffness and for correlating with tests; minimum (A- or B-basis) curves
  when the plastic analysis is itself the strength check. Do not mix them in one model.
- **Beyond the last row** many solvers hold the stress flat — Abaqus does. The true curve keeps rising after necking,
  so a table that ends at the ultimate is conservative for load but says nothing about failure.

**Temperature.** Every property that changes over the model's range needs a table against temperature, and the table
must cover the range: outside it, most solvers hold the end values rather than extend them.

**Thermal expansion.** Handbooks give either the **instantaneous** coefficient — the slope of the expansion curve at a
temperature — or the **secant** (mean) coefficient from a reference temperature. Solvers want secant values and the
reference they are measured from (Abaqus ZERO, the Ansys reference temperature, Nastran TREF):

$$
\bar\alpha(T) = \frac{1}{T - T_{ref}}\int_{T_{ref}}^{T} \alpha\,dT
$$

## Composites

An orthotropic card has nine constants: $E_1, E_2, E_3$, $\nu_{12}, \nu_{13}, \nu_{23}$, $G_{12}, G_{13}, G_{23}$.
Symmetry links the major and minor Poisson's ratios, $\nu_{ji} = \nu_{ij}E_j/E_i$, and the constants describe a real
material only if (Lempriere):

- every modulus is positive, and $|\nu_{ij}| < \sqrt{E_i/E_j}$;
- the compliance determinant is positive:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Delta = 1 &- \nu_{12}\nu_{21} - \nu_{23}\nu_{32} \\
&- \nu_{13}\nu_{31} - 2\nu_{21}\nu_{32}\nu_{13} > 0
\end{aligned}
$$

- **Conventions differ.** Some sources and solvers tabulate $\nu_{21}$ where others mean $\nu_{12}$. Typing the minor
  ratio where the major one belongs passes every check above and is still wrong.
- **For a unidirectional ply** the 2–3 plane is usually isotropic: $E_3 = E_2$ and $G_{23} = E_2/2(1+\nu_{23})$.
- **Orientation.** The right constants on the wrong material axes pass every check too. Plot the axes in the model.
- **Ply or laminate.** A wound case is built from ply data and winding angles; a smeared laminate card hides
  bending–stretching coupling and through-thickness behaviour.

## Rubbers and propellant

Rubber and propellant change shape easily but hardly change volume: their bulk modulus $K$ is thousands of times their
shear modulus. Poisson's ratio is then a derived quantity, sensitive in its fourth decimal place:

$$
\nu = \frac{3K - E}{6K}
$$

<Analogy title="A water balloon">
  Squeeze a water balloon and it bulges somewhere else — easy to reshape, almost impossible to make smaller. Give it a
  Poisson's ratio of 0.49 in a model and it becomes a sponge: still soft in shape, but now soft in volume too, sixty
  times too soft for propellant. Every result that depends on squeezing it — pressure on a bonded grain, a seal in its
  groove — goes wrong.
</Analogy>

- **Take $K$ from a volumetric test** and derive $\nu$; never round to 0.49 or 0.5.
- **Hyperelastic forms** — neo-Hookean ($C_{10} = G/2$, $D_1 = 2/K$), Mooney–Rivlin, Ogden — are fitted to uniaxial,
  planar and equibiaxial tests together. A fit to one test alone can go unstable in another mode; check stability over
  the strain range the model reaches.
- **Viscoelastic.** Stiffness depends on rate and temperature, so a card belongs to a load case. Time-dependent loads
  need a Prony series and a shift function — see [Viscoelasticity](../../fundamentals/viscoelasticity/) — and
  thermal loads need the stress-free temperature ([Propellant grain](../../motor/propellant-grain/)).
- **Use hybrid (mixed) elements.** Ordinary elements lock when the material is nearly incompressible and come out far
  too stiff.

## Where the numbers come from

| Material | Sources | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Metals | MMPDS; material specification minimums; certificates for the lot | MMPDS gives design allowables, typical moduli, stress–strain curves and temperature effects |
| Composites | CMH-17; supplier data; tests (ASTM D3039, D6641, D7078) | Datasheet values are typical, usually room temperature and dry |
| Rubbers | Tests: uniaxial (ASTM D412), planar, equibiaxial, volumetric | Hardness gives only a rough modulus |
| Propellant | Tests to the ICRPG/JANNAF mechanical behaviour manual: tension over rates and temperatures, stress relaxation, bulk modulus | Aged as well as fresh; the grain's capability comes from the same programme |

A supplier's "typical" value is not an allowable, and a handbook value at room temperature says nothing about 150 °C.
The source, its basis, the temperature and the material condition go on the card with the numbers.

## Checking a card before you trust it

1. **Units.** One consistent system. Check the density through the wave speed $\sqrt{E/\rho}$ — a few thousand m/s
   for any metal — and the model's total mass against the CAD or the scales. A static model will not show a density
   error; the first vibration or acceleration case will.
2. **Consistency.** Isotropic: $E$, $\nu$ and $G$ agree. Orthotropic: symmetry, the bounds and the determinant.
   Nearly incompressible: $\nu$ from a measured $K$.
3. **Basis and purpose.** Typical or minimum, matched to how the margins will be calculated.
4. **Plastic tables.** True values, first row at zero plastic strain, rising, ending at necking — and known behaviour
   beyond.
5. **Temperature and rate.** Tables cover the model's range; rubber and propellant data match the load case;
   expansion is secant, with its reference temperature.
6. **Orientation** of orthotropic materials in the model.
7. **Single-element tests.** Load one element and compare with the card:

| Test | Load | Must reproduce |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Uniaxial stress | Stretch, sides free | $E$; $\nu$ from the lateral strain; the plastic curve |
| Pure shear | Shear | $G$ |
| Hydrostatic | Pressure on every face | $K$ |
| Free heating | Uniform temperature change, no restraint | Thermal strain, $\bar\alpha\,\Delta T$ |
| Mass | Gravity or a frequency | Density |

8. **Traceability.** Source, basis, temperature, condition, revision and owner. A card is a controlled input, like a
   drawing.

## Worked example — cards for the 300 mm motor's model

The starting values in each tab of the calculator are this example's. The uniform elongation, the ply constants and the
expansion coefficients are illustrative.

1. **D6AC case, elastic and units.** In mm, N, tonne and seconds: $E = 205\,000$ MPa, $\nu = 0.29$, so
   $G = 79\,457$ MPa; density $7.85 \times 10^{-9}$ t/mm³. The wave speed is $\sqrt{205\,000/7.85\times10^{-9}}$ mm/s
   $= 5110$ m/s — right for steel. Type the density as 7850 instead and the wave speed falls to 5 mm/s: the
   10.2 kg cylinder now weighs $1.02 \times 10^{10}$ t. A static pressure analysis is unaffected — the error waits for
   the first vibration load.
2. **D6AC case, plastic.** $F_{ty} = 1450$ MPa, $F_{tu} = 1520$ MPa and, for this example, 5% uniform elongation
   give $n = 64.9$ — very little hardening, as in most high-strength steels. The table runs from 1394 MPa at zero
   plastic strain to **1596 MPa at 4.10% true plastic strain**: true stress at necking is 5% above the engineering
   ultimate. One element stretched in the model must retrace the table, then hold 1596 MPa flat.
3. **Carbon-fibre skirt, orthotropic.** A typical carbon/epoxy ply — $E_1 = 160$ GPa, $E_2 = E_3 = 9$ GPa,
   $\nu_{12} = 0.32$, $\nu_{23} = 0.45$, $G_{12} = 5$ GPa, $G_{23} = 3.1$ GPa — passes: determinant 0.781,
   $\nu_{21} = 0.018$, and $G_{23}$ matches $E_2/2(1+\nu_{23}) = 3.10$ GPa. Swap $E_1$ and $E_2$ but keep the Poisson's
   ratios, and $\nu_{21}$ becomes 5.7 and the determinant −1.03: **not a material**. Type the minor ratio 0.018 as
   $\nu_{12}$ instead, and every check passes while the ply's sideways contraction is 18 times too small. Only the
   source can catch that one.
4. **Propellant.** The grain's relaxed modulus is 3.6 MPa and its bulk modulus 3.5 GPa
   ([Propellant grain](../../motor/propellant-grain/)), so $\nu = 0.499829$, $G = 1.20$ MPa, $C_{10} = 0.600$ MPa and
   $D_1 = 5.7 \times 10^{-4}$ MPa⁻¹. A guessed $\nu = 0.49$ gives $K = 60$ MPa — **58 times too soft** in volume. Under
   10 MPa the grain's volume would shrink 16.7% instead of 0.29%. The ignition-strain term that uses $p/K$ on the grain
   page would read $10/60$ instead of $10/3500$, and the predicted bore strain would jump from 5.6% to 71%. Round the
   other way, to 0.4999, and $K$ is 1.7 times too stiff.
5. **Aluminium closure, thermal.** After burnout, heat soaks back into an aluminium closure to 150 °C. Take
   instantaneous $\alpha$ rising from $21 \times 10^{-6}$/K at −50 °C to $25 \times 10^{-6}$/K at 150 °C, assembled
   and referenced at 20 °C. The secant coefficient to 150 °C is $23.7 \times 10^{-6}$/K, and the thermal strain
   3081 µε. Enter the instantaneous 25 as if it were secant and the closure grows **5.5% too much**. And if the card's
   tables stopped at 100 °C, the solver would use 100 °C stiffness, strength and expansion all the way to 150 °C.

## Where this stops

- **Cards, not data.** Every number on this page is an example; design values come from your sources and tests.
- **Simple forms.** Plasticity here is isotropic hardening from a monotonic curve. Cyclic loading, kinematic hardening,
  rate dependence of metals, damage and failure need more.
- **Linear expansion data.** Real coefficients are curves; the calculator takes a straight line between two points.
- **Composites** need failure criteria, through-thickness and process data that the elastic card does not carry.

## On the drawing

The card is not on the drawing, but they must agree:

- **Material and condition** — specification, temper or heat treatment — match the card's source.
- **Process-dependent properties** — fibre volume, winding angle, cure, propellant batch and age — are controlled on
  the drawing or its process specifications if the model relies on them.
- **The analysis report** lists each card with its source, basis, temperature range and revision.
