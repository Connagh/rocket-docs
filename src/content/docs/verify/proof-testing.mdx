## Why this matters

Every solid motor case is normally proof tested before it is insulated and loaded with propellant. It is the last
check between manufacture and a vessel that will hold an energetic load for years. A proof test catches:

- **Workmanship and material errors** — a wrong heat treatment, a weak weld, a wall machined too thin, a bad batch of
  material.
- **Cracks nobody found.** This is the subtle one, and the reason proof is part of the fracture-control argument rather
  than just a quality check.
- **Leaks** at welds, bosses and seals.

## What a proof test proves — and what it doesn't

<Analogy title="A sieve">
  Pour a bag of gravel through a sieve. You never look at a single stone, but you know for certain that every stone
  that passed through is smaller than the mesh. A proof test is a sieve for cracks: any case carrying a crack big
  enough to fail at proof pressure fails on the test stand, safely. Every case that survives has only cracks smaller
  than the "mesh size" — even cracks no inspection could ever have found.
</Analogy>

<ProofSieve />

It does **not** prove:

- **That the case has margin to burst.** Proof is below burst on purpose. Burst capability is shown by analysis and by
  testing qualification articles to failure.
- **That flaws stay small.** Cracks grow with every pressurisation and, in some environments, under sustained load. The
  proof screen is the *starting* size for a life argument, not the end of it.
- **That the case survives loads other than pressure** — thrust, bending, handling, external pressure.
- **That the test did no harm.** Proof is the highest stress the part will see before flight. It can grow sub-critical
  cracks and, in a susceptible material and environment, leave the case worse than it found it.

## Choosing the proof factor

The proof pressure is MEOP times a proof factor (see the [pressure ladder](../../motor/case-sizing/#the-three-pressures)).
The factor is squeezed from both sides:

| Pushes the factor **up** | Pushes the factor **down** |
| --- | --- |
| Smaller screened flaw, so more crack-growth life | Proof stress must stay below yield, with margin for local peaks |
| Must cover strength lost at the service temperature | The test can grow cracks and cause sustained-load damage |
| Must cover toughness lost at the service temperature | Composites: fibre damage, stress-rupture and damage accumulation |
| Must screen to a size NDT cannot reliably see | Good hardware lost in the test, schedule and cost |

The governing standard sets the factor for the vessel class — typically somewhere between about 1.05 and 1.5 — and the
fracture-control plan checks it buys enough life. Two corrections are routinely applied on top:

- **Temperature.** If the case is weaker at its service temperature than at the test temperature, raise the proof
  pressure by the ratio of strengths, so the proof stress-to-strength ratio covers the service condition.
- **Toughness.** The screen only works if the material is at least as tough in the test as in service. Toughness of
  high-strength steels usually falls with temperature, so a warm proof can screen *nothing* for a cold-day firing. The
  calculator below shows how quickly that happens.

## The flaw-screening argument

For a crack of depth $a$ in a wall at stress $\sigma$, linear-elastic fracture mechanics gives the stress intensity
$K = Y\sigma\sqrt{\pi a}$, where $Y$ is a geometry factor (about 1.12 for a long, shallow surface crack). The crack
runs unstably when $K$ reaches the fracture toughness $K_{IC}$. So:

$$
a_\text{screen} = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{K_{IC,\text{proof}}}{Y\,\sigma_\text{proof}}\right)^2
\qquad
a_\text{crit} = \frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{K_{IC,\text{service}}}{Y\,\sigma_\text{MEOP}}\right)^2
$$

Their ratio is the heart of proof-test logic (Tiffany and Masters, 1965):

$$
\frac{a_\text{crit}}{a_\text{screen}} = R^2 \left(\frac{K_{IC,\text{service}}}{K_{IC,\text{proof}}}\right)^2
$$

where $R$ is the proof factor. A 1.10 proof leaves room for the surviving cracks to grow by 21% before they become
critical at MEOP. Raising $R$ helps as $R^2$; losing toughness in service hurts as the square of the toughness ratio.

The crack-growth life between the two comes from the Paris law, $da/dN = C\,(\Delta K)^m$. With a constant $Y$ it
integrates in closed form:

$$
N = \frac{a_\text{crit}^{\,1-m/2} - a_\text{screen}^{\,1-m/2}}{C\,\left(Y\,\Delta\sigma\sqrt{\pi}\right)^m\,(1 - m/2)}
$$

Fracture-control standards for flight pressure vessels typically require the analysed life to be at least **four
times** the service life.

## Worked example — the D6AC case

The case from the [case-sizing example](../../motor/case-sizing/#worked-example): hoop stress 1094 MPa at 10 MPa MEOP,
1.377 mm wall, $K_{IC} \approx 70$ MPa√m, proof factor 1.10, long surface crack ($Y = 1.12$).

1. **Proof stress.** $1.10 \times 1094 = 1204$ MPa — 83% of the 1450 MPa yield.
2. **Screened flaw.** $\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{70}{1.12 \times 1204}\right)^2 = 0.86$ mm. No case that passes proof has
   a crack deeper than this.
3. **Critical flaw at MEOP.** $\frac{1}{\pi}\left(\frac{70}{1.12 \times 1094}\right)^2 = 1.04$ mm. The ratio is
   $1.10^2 = 1.21$.
4. **Life.** Martensitic steel, Barsom's upper bound $da/dN = 1.35 \times 10^{-10}\,\Delta K^{2.25}$, full 0–MEOP
   cycles: about **105 pressurisations** to grow from 0.86 to 1.04 mm. For a single firing plus one leak check, with a
   life factor of 4, the requirement is 8 — a margin of about 13.
5. **NDT.** If inspection can only promise to find cracks deeper than about 1.3 mm, it cannot support this argument on
   its own: the critical size is smaller than what NDT sees. Proof carries the fracture case.

Two warnings come out of the same numbers:

- **Cold service.** If toughness at −40 °C is 20% lower (56 MPa√m), the ratio becomes $1.21 \times 0.8^2 = 0.77$.
  Survivors of a room-temperature proof may then carry cracks that fail on a cold day. The proof screen is worthless
  unless the test is done cold or the toughness drop is ruled out.
- **Deep cracks.** The critical crack is 75% of the wall. A constant $Y$ underestimates $K$ that deep, so the real sizes
  are smaller. This is also the [break-before-leak](../../motor/case-sizing/#leak-before-break) case the case-sizing
  page flagged. A released analysis uses a proper surface-crack solution.

## Test media and safety

### Why we proof with liquid

<Analogy title="A full suitcase versus a compressed spring">
  Sitting on a suitcase packed with books barely squashes it, and nothing much springs back when you stand up. That is
  a liquid: it hardly compresses, so it stores very little energy. A gas is a coiled spring — every bit of work done to
  squeeze it is waiting to come back out the instant the vessel opens.
</Analogy>

The energy released if the vessel fails is:

$$
E_\text{liquid} = \frac{p^2 V}{2K}
\qquad
E_\text{gas} = \frac{p V}{\gamma - 1}\left[1 - \left(\frac{p_\text{atm}}{p}\right)^{\frac{\gamma-1}{\gamma}}\right]
\qquad
E_\text{wall} = \tfrac{1}{2}\,p\,\Delta V
$$

For the 85-litre example case at 11 MPa: water stores about **2.3 kJ**, and the steel wall's own elastic strain energy
adds 5.2 kJ — 7.5 kJ in total, about 2 g of TNT. The same test with nitrogen stores about **1.7 MJ**, roughly
0.4 kg of TNT: **over 200 times more**. That is why proof tests are hydrostatic unless there is a compelling reason
otherwise, and why pneumatic proof needs special approval, remote operation and much larger exclusion zones.

**Trapped air** is gas energy hidden inside a hydrostatic test. It matters most in large, low-pressure tests, where the
liquid stores little energy of its own. Vessels are filled from the bottom and bled from the highest point until only
liquid comes out.

### The medium must not attack the vessel

- **Stress-corrosion cracking.** High-strength steels can crack under sustained stress in plain water. Proof water is
  inhibited and its chemistry controlled, hold times are kept short, and the case is drained and dried promptly.
- **Chemistry matters for every alloy.** Titanium pressure tanks failed during Apollo-era testing with methanol, and
  chlorides attack stainless steels. Check the test medium against the material as carefully as the propellant.
- **Temperature.** Steel cases are tested well above any ductile–brittle transition, and at a temperature that keeps
  the toughness argument above valid.

### The rig and the schedule

<ProofRig />

<ProofProfile />

- **Remote operation.** No one is near a pressurised vessel, and the cell is cleared and barricaded before pressure
  rises above a low leak-check level.
- **Relief valve** set above proof pressure and below anything that would damage the vessel; rupture discs as backup.
- **Two independent, calibrated pressure measurements.** A single gauge that reads low can over-pressurise a case.
- **Stepped pressurisation** with holds, and a slow final ramp, so a leak or an abnormal strain reading stops the test
  early.
- **Records.** Gauge calibrations, liquid temperature, pressure and strain histories, and the serial number of the part
  form the acceptance record.

## Acceptance criteria

A case passes proof when the test specification's criteria are all met. Typically:

1. **No burst and no leak.** Leaks are judged visually and by the pressure hold — after correcting for temperature.
2. **No significant permanent deformation.** Measured by volumetric expansion (water jacket), strain gauges or
   dimensional checks before and after. A widely used convention for gas cylinders limits permanent expansion to 10% of
   the total expansion at test pressure. Motor cases use programme-specific limits.
3. **Strain tracks prediction.** Strain gauges should follow the predicted elastic response linearly. A knee in the
   strain–pressure curve means local yielding.
4. **Post-proof NDT** to look for cracks the test may have grown.
5. **Composite cases** add acoustic-emission criteria, because fibre and matrix damage makes noise long before it shows.

### Why hold pressure drifts

A liquid-full vessel is very stiff, so tiny volume changes move the pressure a lot. A change in liquid temperature
changes its volume:

$$
\frac{dp}{dT} = \frac{\beta_\text{liquid} - 3\alpha_\text{vessel}}{1/K + C}
$$

where $\beta$ is the liquid's volumetric expansion coefficient, $\alpha$ the vessel's linear expansion coefficient, and
$C = \frac{1}{V}\frac{dV}{dp}$ the vessel's volumetric compliance. For the example case it is about **0.12 MPa per
kelvin** — over 1% of proof pressure for a single degree. Most hold-pressure "leaks" are the liquid cooling.

## Where this stops

<Aside type="caution" title="Screening, not a fracture-control plan">
  These tools use linear-elastic fracture mechanics with a constant geometry factor and class-typical growth data.
  A released proof-test argument uses measured toughness and crack-growth data at the right temperature and
  environment, surface- and embedded-crack solutions, crack growth during the proof cycle itself, sustained-load
  (environment-assisted) cracking, and the governing standard's factors.
</Aside>

- **Thin, tough walls** may tear in a ductile way before linear-elastic fracture mechanics applies. Elastic-plastic
  methods are then needed.
- **Composite cases** are not screened this way. Their proof logic is about damage, stress rupture and acoustic
  emission, not a single crack.

### Differences from REF·DB v1

<Aside type="note" title="Crack-growth constants corrected">
  v1 labelled the ferrite–pearlite constants ($6.9\times10^{-12}$, $m = 3.0$) as "low-alloy / maraging steel". D6AC,
  maraging and other martensitic case steels use Barsom's martensitic upper bound ($1.35\times10^{-10}$, $m = 2.25$),
  which predicts faster growth at the stress intensities that matter here. v1's austenitic coefficient also did not
  match the metric form of Barsom's line; it is now $5.6\times10^{-12}$, $m = 3.25$.
</Aside>
