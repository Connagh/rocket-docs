## Why this matters

A finite-element model always produces an answer, in colour, to four significant figures. Whether it is right depends
on decisions the contour plot does not show: what was left out, how fine the mesh is where it matters, what holds the
model in place, how the loads go in. Most wrong analyses are not wrong because the solver failed. They are wrong
because the model answered a different question from the one being asked — and nobody checked.

Two words help keep this straight:

- **Verification** — are the equations being solved right? Code checks, mesh convergence, equilibrium.
- **Validation** — are they the right equations? Comparison with test, which is the only thing that can show the
  idealisation is good enough.

NASA-STD-7009 and ASME V&V 10 set out both for models whose results support decisions.

<Analogy title="A map">
  A map is useful because it leaves things out: a road map has no contour lines, a hiking map no traffic lights. Use
  the wrong map and every detail can be accurate while the route is wrong. A model is a map of the part for one
  question. Converging the mesh makes the map sharper; it does not make it the right map.
</Analogy>

## From question to model

Start with the question — burst margin of the case, seal-gap opening at the aft joint, bolt load, natural frequency of
a bracket — then choose the simplest model that can answer it:

| Model | Good for | Leaves out |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hand calc | Sizing; far-field stresses; checking everything else | Detail, interaction |
| Axisymmetric shell | Case, domes and skirts under pressure and axial load; junction bending | Bolts, local 3-D detail, non-axisymmetric loads |
| Axisymmetric solid | Closures, bosses, flange sections, thick parts, through-thickness stress | Discrete bolts (smeared into a ring), bending loads |
| 3-D sector with cyclic symmetry | One bolt pitch of a flange joint, with contact and preload | Loads that are not the same at every bolt |
| Full 3-D | Bending and side loads, real asymmetric detail, assemblies | Nothing — and costs the most to check |

- **Symmetry needs symmetric loads too.** An axisymmetric case model cannot take a bending moment; a quarter model
  cannot take an asymmetric load.
- **Shells or solids.** Thin walls ($r/t$ above about 10) are shells; junctions, lands and thick parts are solids.
  Linear solid elements lock in bending — use quadratic elements, or several linear ones through the thickness.
- **Linear or nonlinear.** Large deflection of thin shells, plasticity near burst, and contact in joints and seals each
  make the model nonlinear, with its own convergence and checks.

## Mesh and convergence

A finite-element result carries a discretisation error that shrinks as the mesh is refined. The only way to know how
big it is: solve on three meshes and watch the quantity you will report. For element sizes $h_1 < h_2 < h_3$ with a
constant ratio $r$ and results $f_1, f_2, f_3$:

$$
p = \frac{\ln\big[(f_3 - f_2)/(f_2 - f_1)\big]}{\ln r}
$$

$$
f_{exact} \approx f_1 + \frac{f_1 - f_2}{r^p - 1}
$$

The **grid convergence index**, $\text{GCI} = 1.25\,|(f_2 - f_1)/f_1|\,/\,(r^p - 1)$, puts a band on the finest result
(Roache). Three conditions make this mean something:

- **Monotonic convergence** — the changes shrink and keep their sign. Oscillating or growing changes mean refine
  further, or something is singular.
- **The asymptotic range** — the observed order $p$ is near the element's theoretical order, and
  $\text{GCI}_{32}/(r^p\,\text{GCI}_{21})$ is near 1. Meshes too coarse for the feature give confident nonsense.
- **The quantity that matters**, at the point that matters. Displacements converge on coarse meshes; peak stresses need
  several elements across the zone where they change — for a shell edge, the zone $\pi/\beta \approx 2.4\sqrt{rt}$
  from [Hand calcs](../../fundamentals/hand-calcs/#thin-shells).

## Singularities

Some points have no converged stress at all. At a sharp internal corner, the elastic stress is infinite: near the
corner it grows as $r^{\lambda-1}$, with Williams' exponent $\lambda = 0.545$ for a 90° corner and 0.5 for a crack. Every
halving of the mesh multiplies the reported peak by $2^{1-\lambda} = 1.37$ — for ever.

<FeaConvergence />

The same happens at point loads, point constraints, contact edges and the corners of bonded dissimilar materials. So:

- **Model the real radius.** If the drawing has a 0.5 mm fillet, the model has one too, meshed finely enough to
  converge. The radius then becomes a drawing requirement.
- **Read stresses where a method defines them.** Through-thickness linearisation into membrane and bending for
  pressure-vessel codes; hot-spot extrapolation from fixed distances for welds; stress intensity for cracks.
- **Spread loads and supports** over a realistic area. Introduce them with a coupling that does not stiffen the part —
  Nastran RBE3 or an Abaqus distributing coupling — unless the real part is rigid.

## Boundary conditions and loads

- **Remove every rigid-body motion and nothing more.** Under-constrained models will not solve; over-constrained ones
  solve happily and report stresses the real part never sees. Fixing the end of a case model rigidly, when the real
  flange grows and rotates with it, creates the clamped-edge bending of the
  [hand calc](../../fundamentals/hand-calcs/#thin-shells) — about twice the hoop stress, out of nothing.
- **Free bodies stay free.** A motor in flight is held by nothing: balance its loads with inertia (inertia relief)
  rather than inventing a support.
- **Pressure goes on every wetted face** — the closures and nozzle as well as the wall. Check the reaction.
- **Symmetry planes** need the symmetry conditions, not just a cut.

## Contact and preload

Joints and seals bring contact: surfaces that can separate, slide and carry pressure only in compression.

- **Preload first, then load.** Bolt pretension in its own step; check the clamp force against the
  [Bolted joints](../../joints/bolted-joints/) calculation before applying service loads.
- **Initial gaps and overlaps** in the geometry become forces or free play at the first increment — clean them up.
- **Converge contact too.** Contact pressure needs a mesh fine enough on both surfaces; check penetration and that the
  contact status is not chattering between increments.
- **Watch the artificial terms.** Stabilisation or hourglass energy should be a small fraction of the strain energy —
  a few per cent at most — or the result comes from the numerics, not the part.
- **Seal gaps** come from separation at the seal line, at the coldest, lowest-preload case — see
  [Sealing strategy](../../seals/sealing-strategy/).

## Before the results go in a report

1. **Inputs** — geometry at the drawing revision; [material cards](../fea-cards/) checked; units.
2. **Reactions** equal the applied loads, to round-off. A static model is in equilibrium or it has a missing load.
3. **Deformed shape** — animate it, at a sensible scale. It should move the way the part would.
4. **Far field** against the hand calc: membrane stresses, displacements, frequencies.
5. **Convergence** of every reported quantity, at its point.
6. **Singularities** identified, and no result read at one.
7. **Diagnostics** — solver warnings, element distortion, negative eigenvalues, energy balance in nonlinear and
   explicit runs.
8. **Sensitivity** — bound the uncertain inputs: support stiffness, friction, gaps, material scatter.
9. **Validation** — compare with test where one exists ([Test correlation](../test-correlation/) is the next
   step).
10. **Record** — question, assumptions, inputs and their sources, checks and results, so another engineer can repeat
    it.

## The model and the test

The model plans the test and the test checks the model:

- **Place gauges in gentle gradients**, where a small error in position does not matter, and compare with the
  model's strain averaged over the gauge length, on the same surface.
- **Gauge both surfaces** wherever the part bends — one surface cannot separate membrane from bending.
- **Rosettes** where the principal directions are not known. For a rectangular rosette with gauges $a$, $b$, $c$ at
  0°, 45° and 90°:

$$
\varepsilon_{1,2} = \frac{\varepsilon_a + \varepsilon_c}{2} \pm R
$$

$$
R = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{(\varepsilon_a - \varepsilon_b)^2 + (\varepsilon_b - \varepsilon_c)^2}
$$

- **A proof test is a validation test.** Gauged at the right places, it checks the case model at a known load on the
  real hardware — see [Proof testing](../proof-testing/).

## Worked example — checking a model of the case at its aft flange

The question: the peak stress in the 300 mm D6AC case wall where it meets the aft flange, at MEOP (10 MPa). The model
is the simplest that can answer it: an axisymmetric shell, here a real one-dimensional finite-element model of the
wall that runs in the **Mesh convergence** tab, with the end clamped to stand for the flange.

1. **The free body.** The first run had pressure on the wall but not on the closures. The far-field hoop stress was
   1094 MPa — exactly $pr/t$, so it looked right. But the axial reaction was **zero**, against the
   $10 \times \pi \times 150^2 = 707$ kN it must carry. The radial growth gave it away too: 0.804 mm, 17% more than
   the closed-end 0.688 mm, and exactly the open-ended value. With the end load applied, both agree. (These first-run
   values are the defaults in the **Equilibrium & far field** tab.)
2. **Mesh convergence.** Elements of 16, 8 and 4 mm give peak axial stresses of 1811, 2108 and 2205 MPa. Monotonic,
   observed order 1.62, extrapolated **2252 MPa**, GCI 2.7%, asymptotic ratio 1.05 — a study that can be trusted.
   The exact answer for this model is 2241 MPa: the 4 mm mesh is 1.6% low, the extrapolation 0.5% high. A study on
   32, 16 and 8 mm meshes — fewer than five elements across the 35 mm edge zone — gives an order of 0.87 and
   extrapolates to 2467 MPa, 10% high. Coarser still, it does not converge at all.
3. **Converged is not right.** The 2241 MPa is real for this model and an artefact for the case: it comes from the
   clamp. The [hand calc](../../fundamentals/hand-calcs/#thin-shells) gives the same number for a rigid edge, about
   twice the hoop stress. The real flange grows and rotates with the wall, so the model must include it — or at least
   its stiffness — and the wall needs a land where it joins. Sharpen the land's inside corner to a true 90° and the
   peak would climb by 1.37 times with every halving of the mesh: model the drawing's fillet radius instead.
4. **The test.** At the 11 MPa proof test, a rosette 100 mm from the flange — nearly three edge zones away, in a gentle
   gradient — reads, for this example, 1260, 3170 and 4950 µε at 0°, 45° and 90° from the axis. In the
   **Strain gauges** tab: 1190 MPa hoop and 603 MPa axial, against the model's 1204 and 602 — 1.2% and 0.2%. The
   membrane model is validated there. The edge region needs its own gauges, or the comparison waits for
   [test correlation](../test-correlation/).

## Where this stops

- **Method, not a solver manual.** Element formulations, contact algorithms and solver settings differ between codes;
  follow your code's documentation and your organisation's modelling guidelines.
- **Richardson extrapolation** assumes smooth convergence in the asymptotic range. It does not apply at singular
  points or to quantities that switch behaviour, such as contact status or buckling modes.
- **The edge model** is linear, axisymmetric and thin-shell: a demonstration with a known answer, not a design tool.
- **Validation needs tests** designed for it — loads, boundary conditions and measurements the model can reproduce.

## On the drawing

- **Radii and lands the model relied on** — as minimum dimensions, so the part cannot be made sharper than analysed.
- **The drawing revision** each model represents, recorded in the analysis.
- **Gauge locations, orientations and surfaces** on the test drawing or procedure, matching the model's read-out
  points.
