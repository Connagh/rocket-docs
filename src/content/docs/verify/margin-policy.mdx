## Why this matters

The margin of safety is the currency of a stress report: one number per failure mode that says whether a part is
strong enough, and by how much. Reviewers do not check every calculation — they check that every margin is built from
a consistent set. Most review findings come from four errors:

- **Mixed pairs** — a yield allowable against an ultimate load, or the reverse.
- **The wrong basis** — a typical or room-temperature value in a hot, single-load-path check.
- **Missing factors** — a fitting or casting factor the specification requires, left out.
- **Stacked conservatism** — a pessimistic load, times a pessimistic allowable, times a pessimistic model, giving a
  heavy part and a margin nobody believes.

## Load levels

<LoadLevels />

- **Limit load** is the most severe load expected in service, defined by your programme's load rules — for a pressure
  vessel, MEOP.
- **Yield design load** is limit × the yield factor. At it there must be no yielding that harms function: no
  permanent set that matters, no leak, no misalignment.
- **Ultimate design load** is limit × the ultimate factor. At it there must be no rupture or collapse.
- **For pressure vessels** the proof and burst factors play these roles — see [case sizing](../../motor/case-sizing/)
  and [proof testing](../proof-testing/).

## The margin of safety

$$
MS = \frac{\text{allowable}}{k \times \text{applied}} - 1
\qquad
\text{pass when } MS \ge 0
$$

with $k$ the factor for that check, the applied value at limit level, and the allowable on the right basis at the
right condition. European practice often reports the **reserve factor** instead, $RF = MS + 1$, which passes when
$RF \ge 1$.

The formula works with stresses only when stress is proportional to load. For **buckling, contact, preloaded joints,
large deflections and plasticity**, factor the *load* and analyse at the factored load:
$MS = \text{failure load} / (k \times \text{limit load}) - 1$. Whether thermal stresses and preloads are factored, and
by what, is a programme rule — state it in the report.

<Analogy title="A bathroom scale">
  A scale that reads in pounds and a weight limit written in kilograms can both be correct, and the comparison still
  wrong. A margin is the same: allowable, factor and load are each fine on their own, but they must be in the same
  "units" — yield with yield, ultimate with ultimate, the same temperature, the same direction.
</Analogy>

### Pairing — the most common finding

The [case-sizing worked example](../../motor/case-sizing/#worked-example) has a hoop stress of 1094 MPa at MEOP, a
yield allowable of 1305 MPa and an ultimate allowable of 1368 MPa (weld efficiency included), with example factors of
1.10 and 1.25:

| Pairing | Margin | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Yield allowable, yield factor | $1305 / (1.10 \times 1094) - 1 = +0.08$ | Correct |
| Ultimate allowable, ultimate factor | $1368 / (1.25 \times 1094) - 1 = 0.00$ | Correct |
| Yield allowable, ultimate factor | $1305 / (1.25 \times 1094) - 1 = -0.05$ | A false failure |
| Ultimate allowable, yield factor | $1368 / (1.10 \times 1094) - 1 = +0.14$ | False comfort |

Run yield and ultimate as **separate rows**, every time.

## Special factors

Specifications often require extra factors for particular features, which multiply the basic factor:

| Factor | Applies to |
| --- | --- |
| Fitting factor | Joints and fittings, where the stress state is uncertain |
| Bearing factor | Bearing at fastener holes with clearance or vibration |
| Casting factor | Cast parts, tied to the inspection level |
| Weld factor | Welded joints, tied to procedure and inspection |
| Model-uncertainty factor | Analysis methods not validated by test |

Missing a required one makes every margin downstream wrong. Their values, like the basic factors, come from your
specification — this site does not hold them.

## Combined loading

- **The same stress component from several loads** — add the stresses at limit level, then factor the total. For
  ductile metals under combined normal and shear stress, use von Mises: $\sigma_{vm} = \sqrt{\sigma^2 + 3\tau^2}$.
- **Different load types or failure modes** — a fastener in tension and shear, a shell under axial load, bending and
  pressure — use an interaction equation:

$$
\sum_i R_i^{\,e_i} \le 1,
\qquad
R_i = \frac{k \times \text{applied}_i}{\text{allowable}_i}
$$

Linear interaction ($e = 1$) is the most conservative; elliptical ($e = 2$) is common for fasteners in tension and
shear. Use the form and exponents your governing standard specifies — for spaceflight fasteners, NASA-STD-5020.

The margin is how far **all the loads can grow together** before reaching the curve: $MS = \lambda - 1$, where
$\sum (\lambda R_i)^{e_i} = 1$. For linear interaction that is $1/\sum R_i - 1$; for elliptical it is
$1/\sqrt{R_1^2 + R_2^2} - 1$. A common shortcut, $1/\sum R_i^2 - 1$, is wrong and overstates the margin badly.

## Conservatism and the heavy-part rule

Conservatism is fine when it is known. Record where it sits — the load, the allowable, the model — so it can be
removed deliberately if mass becomes a problem, rather than discovered at the end. And on a mass-driven part, a large
margin is itself a finding: as a rule of thumb, above about 0.5 either the part is heavier than it needs to be, or a
different failure mode or load case governs and has not been found yet.

## The margin report

One row per failure mode per load case, with everything a reviewer needs to check it without asking:

| Column | What goes in it | Example — the hoop-burst row |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Item / location | The part and where on it | Case cylinder, mid-length |
| Load case | Which limit case | MEOP |
| Failure mode | What would happen | Hoop burst |
| Applied (limit) | Limit-level value, with units | 1094 MPa |
| Factor(s) | Basic and special factors, with their source | Ultimate 1.25 (example), spec clause |
| Allowable | Value, basis, condition and source | 1368 MPa, A-basis ultimate × weld 0.9, 20 °C, MMPDS |
| MS | The margin | 0.00 |
| Reference | The calculation or analysis run | Case-sizing calc, §3 |

- **Summarise the lowest margin per item** at the front, and never hide a negative one.
- **Units in every column**, and the analysis or finite-element run referenced, so the margin can be traced.
- **State the status** — preliminary, design or final — because allowables and loads mature during a programme.

## Worked example — a motor case and its closure bolts

The calculator's rows are the 300 mm D6AC case from earlier pages, plus its aft-closure bolts. The factors are
**example values only**: yield 1.10, ultimate 1.25, buckling 1.25, and a fitting factor of 1.15 on the bolts. Press
**Load worked example** to load them.

1. **Hoop yield at proof:** $1305 / (1.10 \times 1094) - 1 = +0.08$.
2. **Hoop burst:** $1368 / (1.25 \times 1094) - 1 = 0.00$ — exactly on the line, because the case was sized to burst.
3. **Buckling of the empty case under vacuum:** $0.127 / (1.25 \times 0.101) - 1 = +0.00$ — see
   [shell buckling](../shell-buckling/#worked-example--the-300-mm-case-unpressurised).
4. **Closure bolt, tension and shear** — 18 kN on a 60 kN allowable, 4 kN on 40 kN (illustrative), factored by
   $1.25 \times 1.15 = 1.44$: $R_1 = 0.431$, $R_2 = 0.144$. Elliptical interaction gives
   $\lambda = 1/\sqrt{0.431^2 + 0.144^2} = 2.20$, so **MS = +1.20**. Linear interaction would give +0.74; the wrong
   shortcut $1/(R_1^2 + R_2^2) - 1$ would claim +3.84.

The lowest margin is the hoop burst at 0.00: the case governs. The bolt margin of +1.20 is a finding under the
heavy-part rule — either something else sizes those bolts (preload, joint separation, thread engagement) and needs its
own row, or they could be smaller. [Bolted joints](../../joints/bolted-joints/) works those checks for the same closure.

## Where this stops

- **Factors, load definitions and margin rules** come from your programme's requirements — for example NASA-STD-5001
  or ECSS-E-ST-32-10 for spaceflight structures. This page explains how they fit together; it does not supply them.
- **Linear responses only** for stress-based margins. Non-linear responses need load-based margins at the factored
  load.
- **Fatigue and fracture** are judged on life and flaw size with their own factors — see
  [Fatigue](../../fundamentals/fatigue/) and [Proof testing](../proof-testing/).

## On the drawing and in the report

- **The drawing carries what the margins assumed:** material specification and temper, finish, inspection class,
  weld and casting quality levels. If a margin needed a casting factor tied to an inspection level, that level must be
  on the drawing.
- **The report carries the set:** for every row, load, factor and allowable with their sources — and the lowest margin
  per item up front.
