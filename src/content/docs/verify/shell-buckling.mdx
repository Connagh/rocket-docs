## Why this matters

With pressure inside it, a motor case is a tension structure, and tension structures do not buckle. But a case is
**without pressure** for most of its life: in storage and transport, on the launcher, on top of a booster that is
already firing, after burnout, and during manufacture and test. In these conditions it is a thin shell in compression.
Thin shells fail by **buckling**. They fail suddenly and elastically, at stresses much lower than yield, and much lower
than the prediction of classical theory.

These are the loads on a case without pressure:

- **Axial compression:** an upper-stage motor that carries the inertia of all the items above it while the stage below
  fires; stacking and handling; thrust that goes through a motor that is not yet burning.
- **Bending:** ground winds on a vehicle on the launcher, lifting and transport, and flight loads before ignition.
- **External pressure:** a case with a vacuum inside, for a vacuum leak check or for processing; a sealed case that
  cools down; submerged launch; pressure differences across closures.

Buckling is a **stiffness** problem. The critical stress depends on Young's modulus and on $t/r$, not on the yield
strength. Thus a stronger alloy gives no benefit unless it is also stiffer, or unless the wall becomes thicker.

## Why thin shells buckle early

For a perfect cylinder, classical theory gives the axial buckling stress:

$$
\sigma_\text{cl} = \frac{E\,t}{r\sqrt{3\,(1-\nu^2)}} \;\approx\; 0.605\,\frac{E\,t}{r} \qquad (\nu = 0.3)
$$

Real cylinders in axial compression buckle much below this value. Thin cylinders buckle at a quarter of it or less. The
cause is **imperfections**: out-of-roundness, weld shrinkage, thickness variation, small dents, load eccentricity and
imperfect end supports. Axial compression is the worst case, because the buckling of the perfect shell is *unstable*.
When it starts, the load that the shell can carry decreases quickly. A small initial dent puts the shell directly onto
that decreasing path.

<Analogy title="Standing on an empty drinks can">
  Stand carefully on an empty aluminium can and it holds your weight. Hit its side lightly with a pencil and it
  collapses immediately. The material did not change. The pencil made a dent, and a thin shell in compression is very
  sensitive to dents. A motor case is a very large, very strong drinks can.
</Analogy>

### The SP-8007 knockdown

Designers do not try to predict the imperfections of each shell. They multiply the classical result by an empirical
**knockdown factor** $\gamma$, which is a lower limit of many years of test results. For unstiffened cylinders, NASA
SP-8007 gives:

$$
\gamma_\text{axial} = 1 - 0.901\,(1 - e^{-\phi}),
\qquad
\gamma_\text{bending} = 1 - 0.731\,(1 - e^{-\phi}),
\qquad
\phi = \frac{1}{16}\sqrt{\frac{r}{t}}
$$

| $r/t$ | 50 | 100 | 300 | 1000 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\gamma$, axial compression | 0.68 | 0.58 | 0.40 | 0.22 |
| $\gamma$, bending | 0.74 | 0.66 | 0.52 | 0.37 |

The thinner the shell, the larger the decrease. These are **lower limits**, fitted to tests from the 1930s to the
1960s. Many of those shells were made to less accurate standards than a modern case. The factors are safe and often
very conservative. Programmes that have the budget measure their real imperfections and use non-linear analysis and
test. The NASA Shell Buckling Knockdown Factor project develops less conservative factors with this method.

### Bending is gentler

In bending, only a narrow strip of the shell carries the peak compression. Thus it is less probable that the worst
imperfection is exactly there. The knockdown is smaller, and the bending stress is:

$$
\sigma_b = \frac{M}{\pi r^2 t}
$$

### External pressure is a different problem

Under external pressure, the shell collapses into lobes around its circumference. For a cylinder of moderate length
with its ends held round (Batdorf):

$$
p_\text{cl} = \frac{1.04\sqrt{Z}\;\pi^2 E\,t^3}{12\,(1-\nu^2)\,r\,L^2},
\qquad
Z = \frac{L^2}{r\,t}\sqrt{1-\nu^2}
$$

This gives $p_\text{cl} \propto E\,t^{2.5} / (L\,r^{1.5})$. A very long tube becomes oval, with the limit
$p_\text{cl} = E\,(t/r)^3 / \left(4(1-\nu^2)\right)$. There are three results:

- **The wall thickness has the largest effect.** The buckling pressure increases as $t^{2.5}$ to $t^3$, not as $t$.
- **The length is important.** If you halve the distance between the features that hold the shell round, the buckling
  pressure doubles. A stiffening ring does this.
- **It is less sensitive to imperfections** than axial compression. SP-8007 applies one correlation factor, 0.56, to
  lateral pressure.

<Analogy title="The crushed tank car">
  A steel rail tank car that is cleaned with steam and sealed while it is still hot can collapse when the steam inside
  condenses. Only the atmosphere pushed on it, approximately 0.1 MPa. A shell designed to hold pressure from inside can
  have very little resistance to the same pressure from outside.
</Analogy>

### Combined loads

SP-8007 recommends straight-line interactions for axial compression with bending, and for axial compression with
lateral pressure. The sum of all three is a simple screening check:

$$
R_a + R_b + R_p \le 1,
\qquad
R = \frac{\text{applied}}{\text{allowable}}
$$

With the buckling factor of safety $FS$ of your programme, the requirement becomes $FS\,(R_a + R_b + R_p) \le 1$. A
pressure on the end closures of a sealed case also adds axial compression: $q\,\pi r^2$.

### Does buckling govern at all?

If $\gamma\,\sigma_\text{cl}$ is more than yield, the wall yields first, and buckling is not the problem. Between the
two, as the buckling stress approaches the proportional limit of the material, the elastic formula gives a value that
is too high, and a plasticity correction is necessary. The calculator tells you in which regime you are.

<Aside type="tip" title="Internal pressure is a stiffener">
  Even a small internal pressure puts the wall in tension, removes imperfections and cancels compression. The
  stainless-steel "balloon" tanks of the early Atlas rocket were so thin that they had to stay pressurised, also on the
  ground. Without pressure, they would collapse under their own weight. Liquid-propellant tanks use this effect on
  purpose. A solid motor is usually at ambient pressure until ignition. Thus the wall itself must carry the load cases
  without pressure.
</Aside>

## Worked example: the 300 mm case, unpressurised

This is the D6AC case from [case sizing](../../motor/case-sizing/#worked-example): 300 mm internal diameter, 1.377 mm
wall, and 1000 mm of cylinder between the domes that hold it round. $E = 205$ GPa, $\nu = 0.29$ (from the [material library](../../materials/selection/)) and the
yield is 1450 MPa. The mean radius is $r = 150 + 1.377/2 = 150.69$ mm.

1. **Slenderness.** $r/t = 150.69 / 1.377 = 109.4$.
2. **Classical stress.** $\sigma_\text{cl} = 0.6033 \times 205{,}000 \times 1.377 / 150.69 = 1130$ MPa.
3. **Knockdowns.** $\phi = \sqrt{109.4}/16 = 0.654$, so $\gamma_\text{axial} = 1 - 0.901(1 - e^{-0.654}) = 0.5675$
   and $\gamma_\text{bending} = 0.649$.
4. **Axial capacity.** $\sigma_\text{cr} = 0.5675 \times 1130 = 641$ MPa. This is 44% of yield, so elastic buckling
   governs. $P_\text{cr} = 641 \times 2\pi \times 150.69 \times 1.377 = 836$ kN.
5. **Bending capacity.** $\sigma_\text{cr} = 0.649 \times 1130 = 734$ MPa, so
   $M_\text{cr} = 734 \times \pi \times 150.69^2 \times 1.377 = 72.1$ kN·m.
6. **Flight loads.** Assume 20 kN axial and 3 kN·m bending (from [Load paths & joints](../../joints/load-paths/)):
   $R = 20/836 + 3/72.1 = 0.024 + 0.042 = 0.066$. The loads could increase by 15 times before the case buckles.
7. **Empty case with a vacuum inside,** for example for a vacuum leak check before the propellant is loaded. There is
   one atmosphere outside, 0.101 MPa. $Z = \frac{1000^2}{150.69 \times 1.377}\sqrt{0.916} = 4612$ (moderate length), so
   $p_\text{cl} = 0.225$ MPa. With the 0.56 correlation factor, $p_\text{allow} = 0.126$ MPa. Thus
   $R_p = 0.101 / 0.126 = 0.80$. The pressure on the closures adds 7.2 kN of axial load ($R_a = 0.009$). The total is
   0.81. The loads could increase only **1.24 times** before the case buckles.

The case that holds 12.5 MPa from inside has a reserve of only 1.24 against 0.1 MPa from outside. If this passes
depends on the buckling factor of safety of your programme. Any factor above 1.24 fails. The solutions, in the
calculator:

- **Vent the case.** Never put a vacuum in, or seal and cool, a thin empty case before you check this load case.
- **Add a ring at mid-length.** $L = 500$ mm doubles the buckling pressure: the reserve becomes 2.45.
- **Make the wall 10% thicker.** The buckling pressure increases by 27%, and the reserve becomes 1.57. But this adds
  mass everywhere.

## Where this stops

- **Unstiffened, isotropic, elastic cylinders with their ends held round.** Shells with rings and stringers, cones
  (NASA SP-8019) and domes (NASA SP-8032) have their own methods. A dome under external pressure is also very sensitive
  to imperfections.
- **Composite cases** are orthotropic. Their buckling depends on the bending and membrane stiffnesses of the laminate,
  and the knockdowns are different.
- **Local load introduction** at skirts, joints, lugs and support cradles causes local compression that a uniform
  membrane check does not see. This needs a finite-element analysis.
- **Knockdowns are lower limits,** not predictions. Less conservative designs use measured imperfections, non-linear
  analysis and test.
- **Plasticity.** Near the proportional limit, the elastic formula is unconservative.
- **Internal pressure** makes the shell more stable. This page treats only the case without pressure.
- **A case-bonded grain supports the wall** as an elastic foundation, and it increases the buckling load of a loaded
  motor. Usually it is ignored for conservatism. It is not present in an empty case. This is why manufacture and test
  are often the most critical buckling cases.

## On the drawing

- **Control roundness and dents.** The knockdown assumes imperfections that are not worse than those in the test data.
  The out-of-roundness tolerance and the dent acceptance criteria on the cylinder are buckling requirements as well as
  manufacturing requirements.
- **Use the minimum wall.** Calculate buckling allowables on the minimum thickness that the drawing permits, after
  machining and any chemical milling.
- **Define the supports.** Give the lifting points, cradle positions and support pads on the drawing or in the handling
  specification, because they decide where the local compression goes.
- **Tell what must not occur.** If the external-pressure reserve is small, write "Vent before you apply a vacuum" or
  "Do not seal while hot" in the handling notes.
- **Stiffening rings are structure.** Their attachment, weld inspection and tolerances need the same care as the shell.
