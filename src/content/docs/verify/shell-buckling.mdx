## Why this matters

Pressurised, a motor case is a tension structure, and tension structures do not buckle. But a case spends most of
its life **unpressurised**: in storage and transport, on the launcher, sitting on top of a booster that is already
firing, after burnout, and during manufacture and test. Then it is a thin shell in compression — and thin shells fail
by **buckling**: suddenly, elastically, at stresses far below yield, and far below what classical theory predicts.

When an unpressurised case gets loaded:

- **Axial compression** — an upper-stage motor carrying the inertia of everything above it while the stage below
  fires; stacking and handling; thrust passed through a motor that is not yet burning.
- **Bending** — ground winds on a vehicle on the launcher, lifting and transport, flight loads before ignition.
- **External pressure** — a case evacuated inside, for a vacuum leak check or processing; a sealed case cooling down;
  submerged launch; pressure differences across closures.

Buckling is a **stiffness** problem. The critical stress depends on Young's modulus and on $t/r$, not on yield
strength, so a stronger alloy buys nothing unless it is also stiffer — or the wall gets thicker.

## Why thin shells buckle early

For a perfect cylinder, classical theory gives the axial buckling stress

$$
\sigma_\text{cl} = \frac{E\,t}{r\sqrt{3\,(1-\nu^2)}} \;\approx\; 0.605\,\frac{E\,t}{r} \qquad (\nu = 0.3)
$$

Real cylinders in axial compression buckle well below this — thin ones at a quarter of it or less. The cause is
**imperfections**: out-of-roundness, weld shrinkage, thickness variation, small dents, load eccentricity and imperfect
end supports. Axial compression is the worst case because the perfect shell's buckling is *unstable*: the moment it
starts, the load it can carry drops sharply. A small initial dent puts the shell straight onto that falling path.

<Analogy title="Standing on an empty drinks can">
  Stand carefully on an empty aluminium can and it holds your weight. Tap its side with a pencil and it crumples
  instantly. Nothing about the material changed — the tap made a dent, and a thin shell in compression is enormously
  sensitive to dents. A motor case is a very large, very strong drinks can.
</Analogy>

### The SP-8007 knockdown

Designers do not try to predict each shell's imperfections. They multiply the classical answer by an empirical
**knockdown factor** $\gamma$, drawn beneath decades of test results. NASA SP-8007 gives, for unstiffened cylinders,

$$
\gamma_\text{axial} = 1 - 0.901\,(1 - e^{-\phi}),
\qquad
\gamma_\text{bending} = 1 - 0.731\,(1 - e^{-\phi}),
\qquad
\phi = \frac{1}{16}\sqrt{\frac{r}{t}}
$$

| $r/t$ | 50 | 100 | 300 | 1000 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\gamma$, axial compression | 0.68 | 0.58 | 0.40 | 0.22 |
| $\gamma$, bending | 0.74 | 0.66 | 0.52 | 0.37 |

The thinner the shell, the more it is knocked down. These are **lower bounds**, fitted to tests from the 1930s to the
1960s, many on shells made to looser standards than a modern case. They are safe and often very conservative.
Programmes that can afford it measure their real imperfections and use non-linear analysis and test instead — NASA's
Shell Buckling Knockdown Factor project is developing less conservative factors this way.

### Bending is gentler

In bending, only a narrow strip of the shell carries the peak compression, so the worst imperfection is less likely to
sit exactly there. The knockdown is milder, and the bending stress is

$$
\sigma_b = \frac{M}{\pi r^2 t}
$$

### External pressure is a different problem

Under external pressure the shell collapses into lobes around its circumference. For a cylinder of moderate length
with its ends held round (Batdorf):

$$
p_\text{cl} = \frac{1.04\sqrt{Z}\;\pi^2 E\,t^3}{12\,(1-\nu^2)\,r\,L^2},
\qquad
Z = \frac{L^2}{r\,t}\sqrt{1-\nu^2}
$$

which works out to $p_\text{cl} \propto E\,t^{2.5} / (L\,r^{1.5})$. A very long tube goes oval, with the limit
$p_\text{cl} = E\,(t/r)^3 / \left(4(1-\nu^2)\right)$. Three things follow:

- **Wall thickness dominates** — buckling pressure grows as $t^{2.5}$ to $t^3$, not as $t$.
- **Length matters** — halve the distance between the features holding the shell round and the buckling pressure
  doubles. That is what a stiffening ring does.
- **It is less imperfection-sensitive** than axial compression. SP-8007 applies a single correlation factor, 0.56,
  to lateral pressure.

<Analogy title="The crushed tank car">
  A steel rail tank car, steam-cleaned and sealed while still hot, can be crushed flat as the steam inside condenses.
  Nothing pushed on it but the atmosphere — about 0.1 MPa. A shell designed to hold pressure from inside can have very
  little resistance to the same pressure from outside.
</Analogy>

### Combined loads

SP-8007 recommends straight-line interactions for axial compression with bending, and for axial compression with
lateral pressure. Summing all three is a simple screening check:

$$
R_a + R_b + R_p \le 1,
\qquad
R = \frac{\text{applied}}{\text{allowable}}
$$

With your programme's buckling factor of safety $FS$, the requirement becomes $FS\,(R_a + R_b + R_p) \le 1$. A pressure
acting on the end closures of a sealed case adds axial compression too: $q\,\pi r^2$.

### Does buckling govern at all?

If $\gamma\,\sigma_\text{cl}$ comes out above yield, the wall yields first and buckling is not the problem. Between the
two, as the buckling stress approaches the material's proportional limit, the elastic formula over-predicts and a
plasticity correction is needed. The calculator tells you which regime you are in.

<Aside type="tip" title="Internal pressure is a stiffener">
  Even modest internal pressure pre-tensions the wall, pulls imperfections out and offsets compression. The early Atlas
  rocket's stainless-steel "balloon" tanks were so thin that they had to be kept pressurised even on the ground, or they
  would collapse under their own weight. Liquid-propellant tanks use this deliberately. A solid motor normally sits at
  ambient pressure until ignition, so its unpressurised load cases have to be carried by the wall itself.
</Aside>

## Worked example — the 300 mm case, unpressurised

The D6AC case from [case sizing](../../motor/case-sizing/#worked-example): 300 mm internal diameter, 1.377 mm wall,
1000 mm of cylinder between the domes that hold it round. $E = 205$ GPa, $\nu = 0.3$, yield 1450 MPa. Mean radius
$r = 150 + 1.377/2 = 150.69$ mm.

1. **Slenderness.** $r/t = 150.69 / 1.377 = 109.4$.
2. **Classical stress.** $\sigma_\text{cl} = 0.6052 \times 205{,}000 \times 1.377 / 150.69 = 1134$ MPa.
3. **Knockdowns.** $\phi = \sqrt{109.4}/16 = 0.654$, so $\gamma_\text{axial} = 1 - 0.901(1 - e^{-0.654}) = 0.5675$ and
   $\gamma_\text{bending} = 0.649$.
4. **Axial capacity.** $\sigma_\text{cr} = 0.5675 \times 1134 = 643$ MPa — 44% of yield, so elastic buckling governs.
   $P_\text{cr} = 643 \times 2\pi \times 150.69 \times 1.377 = 839$ kN.
5. **Bending capacity.** $\sigma_\text{cr} = 0.649 \times 1134 = 736$ MPa, so
   $M_\text{cr} = 736 \times \pi \times 150.69^2 \times 1.377 = 72.3$ kN·m.
6. **Flight loads.** Say 20 kN axial and 3 kN·m bending: $R = 20/839 + 3/72.3 = 0.024 + 0.041 = 0.065$. The loads
   could rise 15 times before the case buckles.
7. **Evacuated empty case** — a vacuum leak check before the case is loaded, say. One atmosphere outside, 0.101 MPa.
   $Z = \frac{1000^2}{150.69 \times 1.377}\sqrt{0.91} = 4597$
   (moderate length), so $p_\text{cl} = 0.226$ MPa and, with the 0.56 correlation factor, $p_\text{allow} = 0.127$ MPa.
   $R_p = 0.101 / 0.127 = 0.80$. Pressure on the closures adds 7.2 kN of axial load ($R_a = 0.009$). Total 0.81: the
   loads could rise only **1.24 times** before buckling.

The case that holds 12.5 MPa from inside has a reserve of 1.24 against 0.1 MPa from outside. Whether that passes
depends on your programme's buckling factor of safety — any factor above 1.24 fails. The fixes, in the calculator:

- **Vent it.** Never evacuate or seal-and-cool a thin empty case without checking this load case.
- **Add a ring at mid-length.** $L = 500$ mm doubles the buckling pressure: reserve 2.45.
- **Thicken the wall by 10%.** Buckling pressure rises 27%: reserve 1.57 — at a cost in mass everywhere.

## Where this stops

- **Unstiffened, isotropic, elastic cylinders with their ends held round.** Ring- and stringer-stiffened shells, cones
  (NASA SP-8019) and domes (NASA SP-8032) have their own methods. A dome under external pressure is itself very
  imperfection-sensitive.
- **Composite cases** are orthotropic: buckling depends on the laminate's bending and membrane stiffnesses, and the
  knockdowns differ.
- **Local load introduction** — at skirts, joints, lugs and support cradles — creates local compression that a uniform
  membrane check does not see. That needs finite-element analysis.
- **Knockdowns are lower bounds**, not predictions. Less conservative designs use measured imperfections, non-linear
  analysis and test.
- **Plasticity.** Near the proportional limit the elastic formula is unconservative.
- **Internal pressure** stabilises the shell. This page treats the unpressurised case only.
- **A case-bonded grain supports the wall** like an elastic foundation and raises the buckling load of a loaded
  motor. It is usually ignored for conservatism — and it is not there at all for an empty case, which is why
  manufacturing and test are often the most critical buckling cases.

## On the drawing

- **Control roundness and dents.** The knockdown assumes imperfections no worse than those in the test base.
  Out-of-roundness tolerance and dent acceptance criteria on the cylinder are buckling requirements as much as
  manufacturing ones.
- **Use the minimum wall.** Buckling allowables are calculated on the minimum thickness the drawing allows, after
  machining and any chemical milling.
- **Define the supports.** Lifting points, cradle positions and support pads belong on the drawing or handling
  specification, since they decide where local compression goes.
- **Say what must not happen.** "Vent before evacuating" or "do not seal while hot" belongs in the handling notes when
  the external-pressure reserve is small.
- **Stiffening rings** are structure: their attachment, weld inspection and tolerances need the same care as the
  shell.
