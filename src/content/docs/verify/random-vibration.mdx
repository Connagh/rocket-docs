## Why this matters

Vibration finds the problems that the static analysis did not find. Examples are a bracket sized for 10 g that gets
30 g at resonance, a connector that frets, a nut that becomes loose, and a harness that wears through. A solid motor is
shaken during transport and handling, on the vehicle, and by its own burn. A static load has one value. A random
environment does not: it is a spectrum. Each part responds to the region of the spectrum near its own natural
frequency.

## The PSD: power spread over frequency

A random environment is specified as an **acceleration spectral density** (ASD, often called the PSD) in g²/Hz. This
tells how much of the mean-square acceleration is in each 1 Hz band.

- **The area under the ASD is the mean square,** in g². Its square root is the overall level, **Grms**.
- **Specifications give breakpoints,** connected by straight lines on log-log axes, with slopes in dB per octave. Here
  a decibel is $10\log_{10}$ of an ASD ratio, and an octave is a doubling of frequency.
- **+3 dB doubles the ASD,** and increases Grms by $\sqrt{2}$ (41%). **+6 dB doubles Grms.**

The area of one segment with a slope of $s$ dB/oct, where $n = s/3.01$, is:

$$
\int_{f_1}^{f_2} \text{ASD}\,df = \frac{a_1 f_1}{n+1}\left[\left(\frac{f_2}{f_1}\right)^{n+1} - 1\right],
\qquad n = -1:\; a_1 f_1 \ln\frac{f_2}{f_1}
$$

The profile on this page is **illustrative and not from a standard**. It increases at +3 dB/oct from 20 to 80 Hz,
stays at 0.04 g²/Hz to 500 Hz, and decreases at −3 dB/oct to 2000 Hz. Its areas are $1.50 + 16.80 + 27.79 = 46.09$ g²,
which gives **6.79 Grms**.

<Analogy title="A graphic equaliser">
  The PSD is the equaliser display of the vibration. It shows how loud each frequency band is. Grms is the overall
  volume. Two environments with the same Grms can sound very different. They can also break different parts, because
  each part responds only to its own band.
</Analogy>

## What Grms means

<RandomSignal />

- **The rms of a zero-mean signal is its standard deviation.** Thus a response of 10 Grms has σ = 10 g.
- **Design to 3σ.** A Gaussian response is within ±3σ for 99.73% of the time. Thus 3 × Grms is the usual peak.
- **Some peaks are higher.** For a narrow-band response, the peaks follow a Rayleigh distribution: a fraction
  $e^{-k^2/2}$ of them are more than $k\sigma$. This is 1.1% above 3σ and 0.03% above 4σ. A two-minute test at 150 Hz
  has 18,000 cycles, thus approximately 200 peaks are above 3σ and 6 are above 4σ. If one overload can break the part
  (for example a brittle material, a bond or a ceramic), 3σ is not sufficient.

## Miles' equation: a part picks its own band

Install a part on a base and shake the base. A single mode amplifies the input near its natural frequency $f_n$ by up
to $Q$ (the transmissibility peak). At $\sqrt{2}\,f_n$ it passes the input without change, and above that it isolates
it. If the input is approximately flat across the resonance, **Miles' equation** gives the rms response:

$$
G_\text{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}\, f_n\, Q\; \text{ASD}(f_n)}
\qquad
Q = \frac{1}{2\zeta}
$$

This is a peak response ASD of $Q^2 \times \text{ASD}$ over an effective bandwidth of $\tfrac{\pi}{2}\,f_n/Q$. Only the
input near $f_n$ is important. This is why the frequency of a part decides its environment.

<Analogy title="A child on a swing, pushed at random">
  Random pushes come at all times. Only the pushes that occur in rhythm with the swing make it go higher. Most of the
  other pushes cancel. A swing with good bearings (high Q) goes higher, but it responds only to a smaller range of
  rhythms.
</Analogy>

**Assumptions:** one mode; light damping; an input that is approximately flat across the half-power bandwidth $f_n/Q$;
a rigid base whose motion the part does not change; and a Gaussian, steady input. The calculator compares Miles with
the full integral of input × transmissibility². The integral does not need the flatness assumption.

**The octave rule:** keep the natural frequency of a part at least one octave away from that of the structure that
holds it. The usual target is two times higher. If you do not, the two resonances multiply, and the part gets a much
larger input than the input at the base.

## From response to design load

- **The 3σ acceleration** is $3\,G_\text{rms}$ of the response. The **equivalent static load** is
  $F = m \cdot 3G_\text{rms} \cdot g$. Apply it at the centre of mass, in the axis of the vibration, one axis at a time.
- **The margin** uses the factor of your programme on that load, against the allowable at the mount, as in
  [Margin policy](../margin-policy/). The method to combine random loads with steady loads (add or root-sum-square) is a
  programme rule.
- **Displacement.** The motion relative to the base is $z_\text{rms} \approx G_\text{rms}\,g/(2\pi f_n)^2$. With Miles
  it changes as $f_n^{-3/2}$. Thus a softer mount has fewer g, but it moves much further. Check the 3σ displacement
  against all clearances.

## Fatigue: Steinberg's three bands

A narrow-band response cycles at approximately $f_n$. Thus an exposure of $T$ seconds is approximately $f_n T$ cycles.
**Steinberg's method** puts 68.3% of the cycles at 1σ stress, 27.1% at 2σ and 4.33% at 3σ. It then adds the damage with
[Miner's rule](../../fundamentals/fatigue/) on the S-N curve of the part.

The S-N line is steep: life changes with stress to a power of approximately 6 to 12. Thus **the rare 3σ cycles cause
most of the damage**. An increase of +3 dB in the input multiplies the stress by 1.41 and the damage by $1.41^m$. This
is 8 times for $m = 6$, and approximately 70 times for $m = 12$. If you double the duration, the damage only doubles.
For this reason, test levels are controlled in dB.

## Stress-based and strain-based failure

Each strength check or life check compares one quantity with its capability. The correct quantity depends on what
controls the load, and if the material stays elastic.

- **Stress-based checks** compare a stress with a stress allowable: static strength against $F_{ty}$ and $F_{tu}$, and
  high-cycle fatigue on an S-N curve. They are valid while the material stays elastic, so that stress follows load.
  Most vibration of metal hardware is in this region: $10^5$ to $10^9$ cycles at stresses much less than yield.
- **Strain-based checks** compare a strain with a strain capability. They are necessary when stress is not a good
  measure:
  - **the material yields in each cycle,** for example a notch root at proof, or a part cycled through large thermal
    or pressure loads. The stress then stops increasing, but the strain continues to increase;
  - **the deformation is imposed,** for example by thermal mismatch, cure shrinkage or an enforced deflection. The
    geometry then sets the strain, for all stiffnesses;
  - **the stress relaxes.** Propellant, liners and elastomers are viscoelastic. The stress decreases, but the strain
    stays (see [Propellant grain](../../motor/propellant-grain/));
  - **the plies share a strain, not a stress.** Composite laminates are usually designed to strain allowables.

<StrainLife />

The strain-life equation adds the elastic line of Basquin to the plastic line of Coffin–Manson. The transition life
$2N_t$ is where they are equal:

$$
\frac{\Delta\varepsilon}{2} = \frac{\sigma'_f}{E}\,(2N_f)^{b} + \varepsilon'_f\,(2N_f)^{c},
\qquad
2N_t = \left(\frac{\varepsilon'_f\,E}{\sigma'_f}\right)^{1/(b-c)}
$$

At a notch that yields, **Neuber's rule** gives the local strain. The local $\sigma\varepsilon$ is equal to the value
of the elastic notch stress, $\sigma\varepsilon = (K_t S)^2/E$. Solve this together with the cyclic stress-strain
curve. Usually, Smith–Watson–Topper is used for the mean stress.

| | Stress-based | Strain-based |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Compares | Stress with a stress allowable | Strain with a strain capability |
| Correct when | The material stays elastic and the load is a force | It yields in each cycle, the deformation is imposed, or the stress relaxes |
| Fatigue curve | S-N (Basquin); Goodman for mean stress | ε-N (Coffin–Manson + Basquin); Neuber at notches; SWT for mean stress |
| Lives | Longer than the transition life | Shorter than it. The transition comes sooner in stronger, less ductile metals |
| On a motor | Brackets and fittings under vibration; the case at MEOP | Notch roots that yield at proof; grain and liner during cool-down and ignition; seals; composite laminates |

## Miner's rule: adding up the damage

$$
D = \sum_i \frac{n_i}{N_i} \qquad \text{failure predicted at } D = 1
$$

Each block of $n_i$ cycles at one amplitude uses $n_i/N_i$ of the life at that amplitude. $N_i$ comes from the S-N
curve or the strain-life curve, as applicable to the cycle. The
[Fatigue](../../fundamentals/fatigue/#variable-loads-miners-rule) page has the details and a calculator.

- **Count the cycles first.** Use the three bands of Steinberg for a narrow-band random response, rainflow counting for
  a wide-band or measured history, and a table of blocks for a spectrum of discrete events.
- **Add all the environments that the part gets in its life.** The bracket of a flight motor collects damage from
  transport, from each acceptance test (and each retest), and from flight:
  $D = D_\text{transport} + D_\text{acceptance} + D_\text{flight}$. Qualification tests are higher and longer than
  acceptance tests, so that one unit shows margin on that total. The levels and durations come from the test standard.
- **The rule is linear and does not consider the sequence.** Real damage depends on the sequence: large cycles at the
  start usually decrease the remaining life at small amplitudes. Also, the measured D at failure has a large scatter
  around 1. Thus programmes set a limit on the damage sum below 1, or apply a factor on life. These are programme rules.
- **Cycles below the endurance limit** cause no damage in the rule. But occasional large cycles can decrease the limit.
  The Haibach extension continues the line below the limit.
- **Propellant grains** use cumulative-damage models with the same principle. Damage comes from cool-downs, thermal
  cycles, transport and ageing, at rates that depend on strain or stress, temperature and time. The calculation is
  similar to Miner's rule, but the material model is different. See [Storage & ageing](../storage-ageing/).

## What it means for a solid rocket motor

| Item | What vibration does | What to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nozzle and exit cone | A heavy mass cantilevered from the aft closure, often with a low first mode | 3σ load at the nozzle-to-closure joint, displacement against the skirt and actuators, joint preload |
| Installed units: brackets, electronics, actuators, sensors | Amplified at their own frequency; connectors fret, fasteners become loose | Miles and the 3σ load at the mount, the octave rule against the skirt, [locking features](../../joints/locking-and-retention/) |
| Harnesses and raceways | Bundles move and wear across spans with no support | Tie-down spacing, clearance to edges, strain relief at connectors |
| Igniter housing and closure fittings | Mass on a boss; thread and seal loads | 3σ load on the thread and boss; seal compression kept at peak load |
| Propellant grain and bondlines | A heavy, well-damped [viscoelastic](../../fundamentals/viscoelasticity/) mass, much stiffer at vibration frequencies than in storage | Bondline stress at the modes of the grain; cumulative damage over long transport |
| Case, skirts and attach rings | The load path to the vehicle and fixtures; sound can drive thin skirts and panels | Skirt and ring loads; acoustic environment for thin panels; local modes near installed units |

The environment comes from four sources:

- **Transport and handling.** Low levels for long durations by road, rail, air or sea, over the life of the motor. Many
  hours of cycles add up. See [Storage & ageing](../storage-ageing/) for the surveillance that finds the damage.
- **Launch or carriage.** The environment of the vehicle at the motor interface, from the environment specification of
  the programme.
- **The burn of the motor.** Combustion and flow shake the case across a wide band, and pressure oscillations can
  increase at the acoustic modes of the bore. Their prediction is a ballistics and test subject. When the environment is
  defined, the method on this page is applicable to the hardware.
- **Test.** Qualification and acceptance on a shaker, with levels, tolerances and durations from the applicable test
  standard.

## Worked example: a 4 kg unit on an aft-skirt bracket

An electronics unit of 4 kg is installed on a bracket on the aft skirt. The bracket gives $f_n = 150$ Hz with $Q = 10$.
The input at the skirt is the illustrative 6.79 Grms profile above, for 120 s in each axis. The mount allowable is
2000 N (illustrative), and the factor of safety is **1.25 for this example only**. Push **Load worked example**.

1. **Miles:** $\text{ASD}(150) = 0.04$, so $G_\text{rms} = \sqrt{\pi/2 \times 150 \times 10 \times 0.04} = 9.71$ g. The
   exact integral gives **9.66 g**. Miles is 0.5% high, because the input decreases below 80 Hz and above 500 Hz.
2. **3σ load:** $3 \times 9.66 = 29.0$ g, so $F = 4 \times 29.0 \times 9.81 = $ **1137 N**.
3. **Margin:** the design load is $1.25 \times 1137 = 1421$ N, and $MS = 2000/1421 - 1 = $ **+0.41**.
4. **Displacement:** 0.106 mm rms and **0.32 mm at 3σ**. This is much less than the 2 mm clearance to the adjacent
   harness.
5. **Peaks:** $150 \times 120 = 18{,}000$ cycles, so approximately 200 peaks are above 3σ and 6 are above 4σ.
6. **Fatigue:** with 4 MPa for each g at the bracket root (notch included), the 1σ stress is 38.6 MPa. Machined 6061-T6
   has $S_e = 96$ MPa at $5\times10^8$ cycles, after the surface, size and 99% reliability factors (from the
   [Fatigue](../../fundamentals/fatigue/) page). Steinberg gives $D = 1.6\times10^{-5}$ for each axis. **96% of this
   comes from the 779 cycles at 3σ** (116 MPa).

In this test, strength limits the bracket, not fatigue. Two changes show the trade-offs:

- **Increase the input by 3 dB** (0.08 g²/Hz on the plateau). The damage increases by approximately 70 times, to
  $1.2\times10^{-3}$. If you double the duration, the damage only doubles.
- **Make the bracket softer,** to $f_n = 75$ Hz. The response decreases to 6.5 g rms, but the motion increases by 2.7
  times, to 0.85 mm at 3σ. Also, a mode near the mode of the skirt would not obey the octave rule.

## Where this stops

- **One mode on a rigid base.** Real parts have several modes. Use the modal effective masses and a finite-element
  random response. On a shaker, the rigid fixture over-tests heavy items, compared with a flexible flight mount. Force
  limiting and notching (NASA-HDBK-7004) must be agreed with the programme.
- **Gaussian, steady input.** Transport has shocks and non-Gaussian peaks. Pyroshock and other transients use a shock
  response spectrum. Sine vibration is a separate test.
- **Linear.** Gaps, friction and preload loss change Q with level.
- **Steinberg is an approximation** for narrow-band responses. For wide-band responses, use rainflow counting on a time
  history, or a spectral method such as the Dirlik method.
- **The grain is viscoelastic.** Its stiffness and damping depend on frequency and temperature. See
  [Viscoelasticity](../../fundamentals/viscoelasticity/).

## On the drawing

- **The drawing gives what the analysis assumed:** fastener torque and locking features, staking or bonding of
  components, harness tie-down spacing, and minimum clearances.
- **The test specification gives the environment:** levels, tolerances, axes and durations. The drawing or the
  qualification plan refers to it. It does not repeat it.
